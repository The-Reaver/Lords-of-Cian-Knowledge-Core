# 04 — Deception: how people lie in conversation, and how listeners catch it or miss it

Research brief for writing tellers who lie, tellers who are honestly wrong, and listeners who believe or doubt them. It covers the real-world evidence only and asserts no canon facts.

---

## 1. Scope and search method

**Scope.** This brief covers spoken and written accounts given in conversation, testimony, interrogation, and retelling. It answers four questions. What does a lie sound like compared with a truthful account? How good are listeners at telling them apart? Why do listeners fail? What makes a liar more exposed? Physiological lie detection (polygraph, imaging) is out of scope except where it bears on the myths.

**Method.** I searched the meta-analytic literature first: Psychological Bulletin, Personality and Social Psychology Review, Law and Human Behavior, Legal and Criminological Psychology, Communication Research, and Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition. Then I took the most-cited primary studies and theory papers those reviews build on. Citations were checked by web search this session, which confirmed authors, title, venue, and year, plus the headline effect sizes where a search snippet carried them. Partway through, the session's search budget ran out and outbound access to Crossref, PubMed, and publisher sites was blocked. Some well-known items therefore could not be re-checked. They are marked **[unverified this session]** in the reference list. Some cue-level effect sizes from DePaulo et al. (2003) are given from the paper's published cue table as I recall it and are marked **(recalled)**. Check them against the original before quoting them as exact.

**Evidence grades.**
- **STRONG:** a meta-analysis, or a finding replicated across independent labs.
- **MODERATE:** consistent primary studies, or one narrower review.
- **CONTESTED/WEAK:** mixed results, small effects that may be inflated, or theory without strong tests.

**The one fact to keep in mind throughout.** Every cue to deception is weak. DePaulo et al. (2003) combined 1,338 estimates of 158 cues. Only a minority of cues were reliable, and the reliable ones averaged about d = 0.25. Luke (2019) shows that publication bias and low power have probably inflated even those. **No single tell is reliable.** Each verbal marker below shifts the odds; none of them proves a lie. That works in a novelist's favor: a careful reader should *suspect* a liar from a pattern of markers, never from one gesture.

## 2. Findings

### F1. People catch lies only slightly better than chance, and they lean toward belief.
**Grade: STRONG.**
- **Overall accuracy.** In Bond & DePaulo (2006), across 206 documents and roughly 24,000 judges, the average was **54%** correct against a 50% baseline.
- **The split (recalled).** Truths were classified correctly about 61% of the time and lies about 47%. That gap is the **truth bias**.
- **Experts.** Police, judges, and other professionals did little better than students.
- **Why judges fail.** Hartwig & Bond (2011), a lens-model meta-analysis, found the main reason is not that lie-catchers hold wrong beliefs; their impressions do track the cues that are valid. The behavioural differences between liars and truth-tellers are simply faint.
- **Many cues combined.** Hartwig & Bond (2014) found that combining many cues improves classification well beyond single-cue judgments: a composite multiple-cue correlation in the region of r ≈ .5 (recalled). No human judge integrates cues that way in real time.

**On the page.** A listener in a scene should usually get it wrong, and wrong in the believing direction. A character who "always knows when someone's lying" is a myth. That claim can only be written as hubris, or as luck. Credible lie-catching on the page comes from **checking content against facts**, not from reading faces (see F7 and F8).

### F2. Most people lie rarely; a few lie constantly.
**Grade: STRONG** for the skewed distribution, which is replicated.
- **The original survey.** Serota, Levine & Boster (2010) surveyed 1,000 U.S. adults. They reported a mean of **1.65 lies per day**, but **about 60% reported no lies at all**, and **roughly half of all lies came from about 5% of respondents**.
- **Replications.** The "few prolific liars" pattern replicated across samples and countries (Serota & Levine, 2015).
- **Diary studies.** Earlier diary work found that everyday lies are mostly small, self-serving, or kindly (DePaulo et al., 1996).

**On the page.** Whether a teller lies is a trait as much as a situation. A habitual liar lies about small things, needlessly, and is comfortable doing it. Most characters lie seldom, mostly to smooth a moment or to spare someone, and they are worse at it when they do. A world full of fluent liars reads false. A world where one figure lies the way others breathe, and everyone keeps half-believing him, reads true.

### F3. Truth-Default Theory: listeners don't evaluate honesty at all until something triggers them.
**Grade: MODERATE to STRONG.**
- **The theory.** Levine (2014; developed in Levine, 2020) proposes that people passively assume honesty. Suspicion switches on only when a **trigger** appears: a stated motive to lie, a contradiction with known facts, a third-party warning, or a performance that seems "off."
- **The evidence.** The truth bias in F1 is the theory's anchor.
- **Why the truth-default helps the listener.** Because most people rarely lie (F2), defaulting to belief is usually correct. The default costs the listener most when he meets one of the rare prolific liars.
- **Where detection succeeds.** It works when the listener uses **contextual knowledge and evidence**, not demeanour.
- **Status.** The theory is newer and much of its testing comes from Levine's own group. The truth-bias findings it explains are independently established.

**On the page.** The listener's state, not the liar's skill, decides most outcomes. A believing listener lets contradictions slide past unnoticed. Write him repeating the liar's phrasing back approvingly. Once a trigger fires, the same listener starts asking for specifics: "Which night? Who else was there?" The scene where suspicion turns on is the hinge. Show the trigger plainly, then show the questions change.

### F4. Liars give fewer details, and their details are less checkable.
**Grade: STRONG.**

**Detail counts.**
- In DePaulo et al. (2003), liars were less forthcoming: fewer details, about d = −0.30 (recalled).
- The content-analysis literature agrees strongly. Oberlader et al. (2016) ran 55–56 studies through Criteria-Based Content Analysis (CBCA) and Reality Monitoring (RM). Both separated true from fabricated statements with a **large overall effect, g ≈ 1.0**: g = 1.03, revised to g = 0.98 in the 2019 correction. CBCA and RM did not differ significantly.
- Amado et al. (2016) found that, in adults, most CBCA reality criteria discriminate, though the results were heterogeneous.

**Checkable details: the Verifiability Approach.** Liars face a dilemma: rich detail sounds credible, but checkable detail can be caught. They resolve it by giving detail that **cannot be checked**: feelings, opinions, and unwitnessed actions. They avoid names, times, receipts, and witnesses. Truth-tellers report more verifiable details, g = 0.42, and a higher ratio of verifiable details, g = 0.49 (Palena et al., 2021, meta-analysis of 20 samples). The effect is stronger when the listener warns that details will be checked (the "information protocol").

**On the page.**
- **Truthful teller:** "I came in by the chandler's side door. Bessa was sweeping and she nodded at me. The bell for second watch went while I was still at the counter." In a city setting: "I took the 4 train, and the guy at the newsstand on 149th short-changed me a dime." Names, sequences, and witnesses arrive unprompted, even though they make the account easier to check.
- **Lying teller:** "I was at home. It was a quiet night, I was tired, I just wanted to rest. I felt off all evening." The account has mood but no witness, and time passing but no clock.
- **Pressed for specifics,** the liar offers more of the same uncheckable kind ("I kept to myself"), or names someone who is conveniently gone, dead, or far away.

### F5. Liars' stories have fewer "ordinary imperfections." Truth is messier.
**Grade: STRONG.**
- **Corrections and memory gaps.** DePaulo et al. (2003) found liars make fewer spontaneous corrections (about d = −0.29, recalled) and admit fewer memory gaps (about d = −0.42, recalled).
- **CBCA criteria.** Both are standard CBCA criteria. Their discriminating power is supported in Amado et al. (2016) and in the pooled content-cue effect of Oberlader et al. (2016).
- **Why liars avoid them.** Liars avoid self-corrections and "I don't remember" because they believe these look suspicious, and that belief is mistaken.
- **What truthful accounts contain instead.** Unstructured, out-of-order production; unusual and superfluous details; reproduced dialogue; mentions of the teller's own mental state at the time. Liars, aiming to look credible, tend to **over-structure**.
- **What doesn't discriminate.** Two CBCA criteria failed in adults: self-deprecation and pardoning the perpetrator.

**On the page.**
- **Truthful teller:** "It was the Tuesday — no, Wednesday, because the fish cart had come." "I don't remember what he said first, but the second thing was 'not here.'" The account goes back to fix the order, keeps an odd detail with no purpose (a dog asleep across the doorway, a radio playing a song she hated), and quotes words directly, half-remembered.
- **Lying teller:** the story runs **clean and in chronological order**: "First I arrived, then I spoke to him, then I left." Times are rounded, there are no self-corrections, no "I don't know" anywhere, and every detail serves the conclusion.
- **Watch the retelling.** A liar asked to tell the story backwards stumbles, because his version was built forwards (see F7).

### F6. Liars' accounts are less sensory, less vivid, and more distanced.
**Grade: STRONG** for the direction; the effects are **small**.
- **Reality Monitoring.** Experienced events carry more sensory, spatial, and temporal information. Imagined ones carry more cognitive operations, such as reasoning and inference. RM performs on a par with CBCA (Oberlader et al., 2016).
- **Computer linguistic analysis.** Hauch et al. (2015) meta-analysed 44 studies and 79 cues. Compared with truth-tellers, liars:
  - used **fewer sensory–perceptual words**
  - expressed **more negative emotion**
  - **distanced themselves** more, with fewer self-references
  - showed **more cognitive load**
  - referred **less often to cognitive processes**

  Liars were **not more uncertain** in their language. Overall effects were small and depended on the context.
- **Immediacy.** DePaulo et al. (2003) also found lower verbal and vocal immediacy in lies (recalled): a more indirect, impersonal, evasive manner.

**On the page.**
- **Truthful teller:** "The tar smell off the dock boards. I could hear the boy counting under his breath." "It was cold enough that my keys hurt in my hand."
- **Lying teller:** "It was a normal evening. Things happened the way they usually do." He slips out of the first person ("you just go in and do the work, you know"), uses passives ("the door was opened"), and uses distance words ("that man," "the girl") for people he knows by name.
- **Negative tone can leak** as irritation at being asked at all. On its own, that proves nothing: innocent people resent questioning too.

### F7. Lying is more cognitively demanding, and adding load widens the gap.
**Grade: STRONG** for the interview effect; MODERATE for the mechanism.
- **The meta-analysis.** Vrij, Fisher & Blank (2017) tested three cognitive-approach techniques: imposing load, encouraging the teller to say more, and asking unexpected questions. These raised total detection accuracy to **71%**, against **56%** for standard interviewing.
- **The mechanism.** Liars must construct a story, monitor the listener, suppress the truth, and keep their story consistent. Extra demands therefore hurt them more.
- **The load measures.** Hauch et al. (2015) found liars' language showed more cognitive load, which fits.

**On the page.** Give the liar a rehearsed core that he delivers smoothly. Then have the listener push him off-script:
- "Draw me the room."
- "Tell it from the end."
- "What could the man at the next table see from where he sat?"
- "Where exactly did the cart stand?"

The truthful teller slows down but keeps adding detail, and may say, "Huh, I hadn't thought of that, but he would have seen the window." The liar's fluency collapses in a specific way. He gives long pauses, repeats the rehearsed sentence word for word, retreats to "I already told you," or produces a detail that contradicts the core. He is fluent on the expected questions and thin on the unexpected ones; that unevenness is the mark.

### F8. Withholding evidence until late exposes liars (Strategic Use of Evidence).
**Grade: STRONG.**
- **The finding.** In Strategic Use of Evidence, Hartwig, Granhag and colleagues found that guilty suspects run avoidance and denial strategies. When the interviewer **withholds** what he knows and asks open, then specific questions, liars contradict facts the interviewer already holds. Innocent suspects tell it like it happened, so they stay consistent with the evidence.
- **The evidence base.** Hartwig, Granhag & Luke (2014) meta-analysed the technique and found large effects on statement–evidence inconsistency; the exact pooled d could not be re-checked this session. Trained officers' accuracy improved (Hartwig et al., 2005; Luke, Hartwig & Granhag, 2016).
- **Related strategy research.** Guilty suspects plan more and favour "keep it simple" and "deny" strategies (Hartwig, Granhag & Strömwall, 2007).

**On the page.** The skilled listener stays quiet about what he holds and lets the liar commit:
- "So you never went near the warehouse."
- "Never."
- "Not that week, not any week?"
- "Not ever."

Only then does he put down the chalk-marked tally sheet with the teller's own mark on it. The liar's next move is diagnostic. He **adjusts the story to fit the shown evidence** ("Oh — *that* day, I was only passing"), and the adjustment comes too neatly and too late. A truthful teller who forgot something says, "Oh, right, yes, I forgot, I went on Tuesday," and then supplies surrounding detail nobody asked for. Writing tip: the reader should see the evidence before the liar does. The tension then lies in watching him walk into it.

### F9. Embedded lies: the best lies are mostly true.
**Grade: MODERATE.**
- **What liars report doing.** Leins, Fisher & Ross (2013) found that most liars, given free choice, built their lie from a **real, previously experienced event**: 67% and 86% in two studies. They relocated it to the time or place in question and filled it with typical, routine, recent details.
- **Why content analysis misses it.** Verigin, Meijer & Vrij (2020) found that a lie embedded in an otherwise true statement is **not qualitatively different** from a lie inside a wholly fabricated one, with Bayes factors moderate to strong. The truthful surround does not lend the embedded lie richness. But the true parts of the statement are rich, so a judge rating the **whole** statement is fooled.
- **The grade.** MODERATE: a small set of labs, and consistent results.

**On the page.** The lie is one swapped fact inside a true and vivid story. "I was at the fish market at dawn" is true — but last Thursday, not this one. Or every detail is real except who was present. The craft signal is **detail density** across the account. The true stretches carry sensory, checkable, imperfect detail (F4–F6). The lied stretch, often the single most important point, goes **briefly thin, generic, and routine**: "and then, you know, the usual, I went home." A careful reader feels the texture drop exactly where it matters. Don't flag it; let the drop do the work.

### F10. Consistency is a poor guide, and liars are often *too* consistent.
**Grade: MODERATE.**
- **The research.** Vredeveldt, van Koppen & Granhag (2014), a systematic review, distinguish four kinds of consistency:
  - within one statement
  - between repeated statements
  - between co-tellers
  - between a statement and the evidence

  Only statement–evidence consistency is a robust cue, and it is the one SUE exploits.
- **Repetition.** Between repeated tellings, truth-tellers are not reliably more consistent. Liars who rehearse a script tend to repeat it closely, while truthful memory naturally adds, drops, and reorders details. In DePaulo et al. (2003), inconsistency was not a valid lie cue.
- **Co-tellers.** Liars who planned together tend to give overly matched accounts. Truthful co-witnesses diverge on peripheral details.

**On the page.** Across three retellings, write the truthful teller **drifting at the edges**: new details surface ("Oh — and there was a smell of tar"), peripheral details change, and the core holds. Write the liar **repeating key phrases verbatim** each time ("I was home by the second bell, I was home by the second bell"). He is stable on what he rehearsed and evasive on what he didn't. When two liars back each other up, give them the *same* odd phrasing and the same order, which honest companions never produce.

### F11. Retelling distorts truthful memory too: embellishment is not the same as lying.
**Grade: MODERATE** (primary studies).
- **Distorted but "accurate."** In Marsh & Tversky (2004), people labelled **61% of their everyday retellings as distorted** (exaggerated, minimized, with omissions or additions), yet only 42% as inaccurate. Tellers reshape stories to suit the audience: they **exaggerate to entertain and simplify to inform**.
- **Retelling changes memory.** Later work by the same group shows that biased retelling then reshapes the teller's own memory.
- **Lies across retellings.** A deliberate lie retold tends to grow in the direction of the liar's goal, as a protective detail or a convenient witness is added. Honest embellishment grows toward the **story's effect**: the fish gets bigger, the danger closer.

**On the page.** Two different growth patterns show the difference without a label:
- **Honest embellisher:** each retelling makes the event more dramatic, funnier, or more frightening. "There were three of them" becomes "a whole crew." But the facts that implicate the teller stay put, including his own embarrassing ones.
- **Liar:** each retelling adds **exculpatory** specifics that weren't there before. "I left early" becomes "I left early, and old Tamsin saw me go." Each new detail answers an accusation nobody has made yet in this conversation.

### F12. The popular "tells" are largely myths.
**Grade: STRONG** that they fail as cues.
- **The stereotype.** The pan-cultural belief is that **liars avert their gaze**. The Global Deception Research Team (2006) found it as the dominant answer across 58 countries.
- **The evidence on gaze.** Gaze aversion and eye contact show essentially **no** relationship to lying; the d is near zero in DePaulo et al. (2003) (recalled).
- **Fidgeting.** Fidgeting and self-touching are not reliable either. If anything, liars show slightly **fewer** movements (fewer illustrators and hand movements) because they over-control their bodies.
- **Nonverbal cues generally.** Nonverbal cues are weak and depend on moderators: Sporer & Schwandt (2007), and see DePaulo et al. (2003).
- **The cues that do show.** The non-myth nonverbal and paraverbal cues are small: higher pitch, more apparent tension, raised chin, pupil dilation (recalled, DePaulo et al., 2003). Luke (2019) warns these may be inflated.
- **What the myths cause.** Belief in them is part of why people do no better than chance (Hartwig & Bond, 2011).

**On the page.** Use the myths **as character, not as cues**.
- **A listener who trusts them is easy to fool.** He trusts the steady-eyed liar and suspects the nervous honest witness who stammers, looks at the floor, and twists her cap. That inversion is realistic and dramatically rich.
- **A seasoned liar knows the myth.** He holds eye contact a beat too deliberately and keeps his hands very still.
- **The narrative should never rely** on "he looked away" to signal a lie to the reader.

### F13. Motivated, high-stakes lies show more; practised liars show less.
**Grade: MODERATE** (moderator analyses).
- **The moderators.** In DePaulo et al. (2003), cues were stronger when liars were highly motivated, especially for **identity-relevant** motives rather than money, and when the lie concerned a **transgression**. Planning reduced some cues, such as response latency.
- **Earlier theory and its critics.** Interpersonal Deception Theory (Buller & Burgoon, 1996) frames lying as a strategic, interactive performance: liars monitor the listener and adapt, and listeners' suspicion leaks back into the exchange. The interactive framing is useful, but critics (DePaulo, Ansfield & Bell, 1996) argued the theory is loosely specified and hard to falsify. Its core predictions remain **CONTESTED**.
- **Monitoring.** What survives in later work is that liars do monitor and adjust to the listener. Truth-tellers take their credibility for granted: the "illusion of transparency," innocence taken for granted (Hartwig et al., 2007).

**On the page.**
- **The liar watches the listener.** He checks reactions mid-sentence, adds reassurance when the listener frowns ("— and honestly, you can ask anyone"), and over-answers.
- **The truthful teller watches the event.** He looks inward or at the remembered scene, gets annoyed at irrelevant questions, and assumes he'll be believed: "Why would I lie about that?" He's often less persuasive for it.
- **A liar about something that defines him** (a lie about his honour or his name) shows more strain than one lying about a sum of money.

### F14. Training on verbal content helps; training on body language mostly doesn't.
**Grade: STRONG.**
- **The meta-analysis.** Hauch et al. (2016) covered 30 training studies. They found a small-to-medium training effect on accuracy, g ≈ 0.33, mainly on **lie** accuracy (g ≈ 0.42), not truth accuracy (g ≈ 0.06). Effects were **larger for verbal-content training**; nonverbal, paraverbal, and feedback-only training did less.

**On the page.** A believable expert examiner works from words and records:
- a clerk checking an account against the ledger
- an investigator listening for missing sensory texture
- an old sergeant who asks for the backwards version

She is mostly better at spotting lies, not at confirming truth. She sometimes suspects an honest person because the honest account was thin, and that error is itself realistic.

### F15. Persuasion tactics liars borrow.
**Grade: MODERATE** as applied to deception. The general influence principles are well established (Cialdini, 2009); evidence that liars specifically use them is mostly indirect.
- **Social proof:** "everyone at the dock knows I was there."
- **Authority:** "the magistrate's own man saw me."
- **Liking and reciprocity:** a gift or favour placed just before the claim.
- **Commitment:** getting the listener to agree to small true points first.

Each pushes a truth-defaulting listener (F3) further from triggering suspicion.

**On the page.** The liar spends words **on the listener** rather than on the event: flattery, shared grievance, and invoked witnesses who are never produced. Compare word counts: in a lying passage, a surprising share of the talk is about the relationship and about who would vouch, and little is about what happened.

## 3. Implications by teller state

| Teller state | What they believe | Characteristic verbal profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Informed and truthful** | Knows what happened and says it | Rich, checkable, sensory detail. Self-corrections and admitted gaps. Out-of-order production. Drifts at the edges across retellings and holds at the core. Annoyed by irrelevant questions, assumes he'll be believed. |
| **Uninformed** (passing on what they heard) | Believes the account; doesn't know the details | Thin detail, but **honestly marked as secondhand**: "So I'm told," "Corwen said." Freely says "I don't know." No pressure to fill gaps, and no investment in a particular version. |
| **Mistaken** (sincere but wrong) | Fully believes a false memory or bad information | **Looks like truth on every verbal cue.** Confident, detailed, sensory, consistent, because to her it *is* her memory. She is caught only by **statement–evidence** mismatch, and she reacts to evidence with surprise, not adjustment: "That can't be — I *saw* him." Verbal-cue methods cannot catch her. |
| **Deliberately lying** | Knows the truth and conceals it | Fewer and less checkable details. Over-structured, few imperfections. Distanced language. Rehearsed verbatim phrases. Thin at the embedded lie. Watches the listener. Adjusts to evidence too neatly and too late. |

The **mistaken** teller is the most important distinction for an account-based fiction. Content-based detection measures how a memory is *experienced*, not whether it's *accurate*. A sincerely wrong witness passes every verbal test. Write her with full confidence and texture, and let only an outside fact expose her.

### Side by side: how a liar talks to two different listeners

| | (a) An uninformed, believing listener | (b) A skeptical, informed listener |
|---|---|---|
| **Liar's effort** | Low. Coasts on the truth-default (F3) | High. Monitors, rehearses, over-controls (F7, F13) |
| **Detail** | Free and even generous with colourful detail, since nothing will be checked | Retreats to uncheckable detail: feelings, opinions, absent witnesses (F4) |
| **Structure** | Loose, chatty, may even include harmless self-corrections | Rigid, chronological, a repeated core phrase (F5, F10) |
| **Embellishment** | Grows in the entertaining direction, like honest retelling (F11) | Grows in the **defensive** direction, adding alibis and pre-emptive answers (F11) |
| **Reaction to questions** | Few questions come, so there are no cracks to show | Fluent on the expected questions, falters on the unexpected (F7) |
| **Reaction to evidence** | None shown | Fits the story to each disclosed fact; the timing exposes him (F8) |
| **Relational talk** | Warm and familiar | Heavier: "You know me," "ask anyone," flattery (F15) |
| **What betrays him** | Usually nothing. He is caught later, by facts | Statement–evidence contradiction, and the unevenness between rehearsed and improvised parts |

One practical consequence: **the same liar can be written telling the same lie twice**, to a friend and to an examiner. The reader learns the truth from the *difference* between the two performances, with no narrator comment.

## 4. Implications by account type

These are the account forms this project's archive uses. The research suggests how each should *sound* when its teller is honest, mistaken, or lying.

**Protagonist series** (a long-running account centred on one figure, told close to him). The teller has deep access and high motive to protect the protagonist. Expect **honest embellishment** in the protagonist's favour (F11): victories larger in each retelling, costs softened. Expect **mistaken certainty** (Section 3) about events the teller didn't witness. Reserve actual lies for rare moments, and give them the embedded-lie texture drop (F9): the series stays vivid everywhere except the one scene it protects. Because readers spend so long with this teller, the texture drop is especially legible there.

**Comrade Account** (told by an ally who was present). The best-calibrated honest voice. Expect checkable detail, admitted gaps ("I didn't see the first blow, I was at the gate"), and peripheral drift between retellings. Comrades lie mostly **by omission and kindness** (F2's everyday lies): they leave out a friend's failure. Write omission as a conspicuous **skip in sequence**: the account jumps from before to after with no "during." If two comrades corroborate each other with identical wording, that is a planted signal of a coordinated story (F10).

**Adversary Account** (told by an opponent). High motive to lie, often identity-relevant, so the cues are more visible (F13). Write it with:
- distancing ("the rebel," never a name)
- negative-emotion leakage
- a heavy load of social proof and authority ("as all reasonable men agree," F15)
- detail weighted toward uncheckable characterizations of the opponent's motives rather than his actions (F4)

But an adversary can also be **sincerely mistaken** (Section 3), believing his own side's propaganda. That is often more interesting, since he will pass every verbal test and fail only against the evidence.

**Dossier** (an institutional file: clinical, third-person, compiled). Dossiers rarely lie in the conversational sense. They fail through **mistaken certainty and source contamination**: a confident, well-structured, detail-rich entry built on one bad informant. The verbal tells of deception mostly *don't apply*, because the clerk writing it is truthful about what he was told. Signal unreliability through:
- the **provenance chain**: one source, or "per informant"
- **over-structure**: tidy numbered findings, round figures, certainty ratings that outrun the evidence
- **absence of admitted gaps** where a real investigation would have them (F5)

A dossier that never says "unknown" is suspect. A dossier that says "unknown" in the right places reads as honest.

**Hearsay** (secondhand retelling). The teller is usually **uninformed but sincere** (Section 3). The dominant distortion is audience-tuned retelling (F11): exaggerated for drama and simplified for clarity, and repeated hearsay drifts further each time. Write hearsay with honest markers ("the way I heard it," "they say") and plenty of "I don't know who." Let the details shift between hearsay versions **in the dramatic direction** (bigger numbers, crueller villains, cleaner heroes), not the exculpatory direction. If a hearsay account suddenly carries crisp, protective specifics, that signals someone upstream lied, not that the gossip is lying.

**General rule for all types.** Signal a lie through **pattern and contrast**:
- detail density dropping at the key point
- an over-clean structure
- defensive embellishment across retellings
- a story that bends to fit evidence after it is shown

Never signal it through gaze, fidgeting, or a single gesture. A careful reader can then find it, and a casual reader, like the truth-defaulting listener, will believe it.
