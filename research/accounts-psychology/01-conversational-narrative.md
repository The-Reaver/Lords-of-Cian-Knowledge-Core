# Conversational Narrative: How People Tell Stories Out Loud

Research file 01 of the accounts-psychology series. This is a craft reference for writing spoken
accounts (Comrade, Adversary, Hearsay, and dialogue inside protagonist and Dossier material). It
synthesizes real-world research and is not canon.

---

## 1. Scope and search method

**Question.** What does the research say about how people actually tell stories in talk? The
areas covered:

- the internal structure of an oral narrative
- how a story gets launched, carried, and closed in conversation
- how listeners shape, interrupt, challenge, and repair it
- how tellers claim the right to tell it
- how it changes with the audience
- which performance devices tellers use
- how telling a story positions the teller socially
- what is known about differences by gender, age, and status
- what separates honest, mistaken, and deceptive telling

**Search.** Web searches confirmed authors, years, titles, venues, and DOIs where available. The
search covered:

- the Labovian structural tradition (Labov & Waletzky 1967; Labov 1997, 2013)
- conversation analysis of storytelling (Sacks, Jefferson, Goodwin, Mandelbaum, Stivers, Holt,
  Pomerantz, Arminen)
- epistemics (Heritage & Raymond)
- co-narration (Ochs & Capps, Norrick, Ochs & Taylor)
- performance and voice (Bauman, Tannen, Wolfson, Schiffrin)
- small stories and positioning (Bamberg, Georgakopoulou)
- experimental and cognitive psychology of retelling (Bavelas et al., Marsh & Tversky, Echterhoff
  et al., Pasupathi)
- age and gender studies (Kemper et al., James et al., Johnstone)
- meta-analyses of deception cues (DePaulo et al. 2003; Hauch et al. 2015)

**Count.** 30 sources were confirmed by search. A small number of further citations are given from
memory and marked **[unverified]**. None of them is the only support for a finding.

**A note on evidence.** Conversational storytelling is mostly studied qualitatively, through
conversation analysis, sociolinguistics, and ethnography. There are no meta-analyses of story
*structure* or *turn-taking* as such.

- **The STRONG grade** is reserved for the few areas with meta-analytic or large multi-lab backing:
  the deception-cue literature. Labov's structural model also qualifies, because it has been
  replicated across languages and independent corpora for over 50 years.
- **CA findings.** When independent researchers have documented a conversation-analytic finding
  across many separate data sets, it is graded MODERATE to STRONG. CA does not run
  significance-tested replications, but its core sequential findings (story prefaces, recipient
  continuers, second stories) recur across many independent corpora and languages.
- **Replication.** None of the core findings here has been the subject of a failed replication.
  The usual caveats still apply to effect sizes from small lab studies.

## 2. Findings

### F1. A full oral narrative has a recognizable skeleton, and evaluation is its spine

**Finding.** Labov & Waletzky's model is the most influential in narrative analysis. A fully formed
personal narrative can contain:

- an **abstract** ("what's this about?")
- an **orientation** (who, where, when)
- the **complicating action** (temporally ordered clauses: "and then... and then...")
- the **evaluation** ("so what?")
- a **resolution**
- a **coda**, which returns the talk to the present

The defining unit is the *narrative clause*. Its order matches the order of events, and swapping
two such clauses changes what is understood to have happened. Evaluation is not a separate box; it
is spread throughout the telling. Labov's evaluation devices include:

- **External evaluation.** The teller stops the story: "it was the strangest thing."
- **Embedded evaluation.** The teller quotes their own past thought or speech: "I said to myself,
  this is it."
- **Evaluative action.** "I was shaking."
- **Comparators and negatives.** What *didn't* happen or *could have* happened: "if he'd turned
  left he'd be dead."
- **Intensifiers, repetition, and gesture.**

Evaluation is densest just before the climax. It works as a suspension: the action halts while the
teller tells you why it matters. Fuller narratives are told most often about danger to life, so
"the danger of death" was Labov's original elicitation prompt.

**Grade.** STRONG for the existence and portability of the structure: applied across languages,
communities, and five decades. MODERATE for the claim that all six parts are typical. Many real
conversational stories are fragmentary (see F7).

**Sources.** Labov & Waletzky 1967/1997. Labov 1997. Labov 2013. Labov 1972 **[unverified]**.

**On the page.**

- Let spoken accounts **stop at the high point** and explain themselves before the payoff: "Now
  you have to understand, the tide was already in." The more a teller stalls before the climax, the
  more the moment mattered to them.
- **Negatives and counterfactuals** are a teller's fingerprint: "Nobody drew. Not one of them."
  "Another hand's breadth and I'd not be here telling it."
- **Codas** bring the listener back to the shared present: "And that's why there's no bridge at
  Fallow Ford to this day." "And that's how I met your mother." A coda is a good closing line for a
  Comrade or Hearsay account.
- **Missing parts tell you about the teller.** Someone who gives the complicating action but no
  evaluation is reporting, not narrating: a clerk, a scout, a frightened witness. Someone who gives
  only evaluation and almost no action is gossiping or boasting.

### F2. Reportability fights credibility, and tellers manage that trade-off visibly

**Finding.** A story must be **reportable**, unusual enough to justify the floor it takes. The more
reportable it is, the less **credible**. Labov (1997) states this inverse relationship directly and
treats a great deal of narrative technique as credibility work: objective-sounding detail,
witnesses named, the teller's own skepticism ("I didn't believe it myself"). Labov (2013) adds:

- tellers allocate **praise and blame** through which events they include, delete, or reorder
- a story is built backward from its most reportable event, so the teller chooses where the story
  "starts"
- choosing that start is already an argument about cause and responsibility

**Grade.** MODERATE. It is a foundational and widely adopted framework, applied in many later
studies, but the trade-off itself is a theoretical claim rather than a measured effect.

**Sources.** Labov 1997. Labov 2013. Norrick 2000, which discusses tellability and the limits of
what can be told.

**On the page.**

- **Credibility anchors** come in clusters around the least believable beat: a named witness ("ask
  Tam, he was there"), a sensory specific ("the water was warm. Warm, in winter"), a
  self-disclaimer ("I know how it sounds").
- **The start point is an argument.** A partisan teller begins the story at the other side's
  provocation; the opposing teller begins one step earlier. Two accounts of one event should
  disagree first about *where it begins*.
- **Deletion is blame-management.** The Adversary simply leaves out the one event that would
  re-assign fault, and smooths the gap with "anyway" or "so then."

### F3. A conversational story has to be launched, licensed, and closed in sequence

**Finding.** In conversation, a story is a multi-turn project. The teller must suspend ordinary
turn-taking to hold the floor, which takes several moves:

- **Prefaces and offers.** "You'll never guess what happened at the market." "Did I tell you about
  the ferryman?"
- **The recipient's go-ahead.** "No, what?"
- **The story's entry.** Jefferson (1978) showed that stories are *locally occasioned*: something in
  the talk triggers them, and the teller marks the link ("speaking of horses...", "that reminds
  me").
- **The exit.** The teller re-embeds the story in the conversation, often by tying it back to the
  trigger, so that ordinary turn-taking can resume.

Goodwin (1984) showed that tellers organize the whole participation framework: who is the
addressed recipient, who is a non-addressed overhearer, who is a principal character. Listeners
show by gaze, posture, and timing which part of the story they think they are in.

**Grade.** MODERATE–STRONG. These are core CA findings, re-documented across many independent
corpora since the 1970s.

**Sources.** Jefferson 1978. Goodwin 1984. Sacks 1992. Mandelbaum 1987.

**On the page.**

- **Earn the floor before the story.** A preface and a go-ahead ("Tell it, then") mark someone
  being *allowed* to tell. When a teller skips this, it reads as urgency or rudeness: a messenger
  bursting in.
- **Triggered stories.** In group scenes, have stories set off by a word in someone else's turn:
  "Fire — that's what it was like at Hollin's yard." It feels true to life and shows what is on
  the teller's mind.
- **Story exits.** "Anyway. That's that." "So — no, I won't sail with him." The exit shows what the
  teller thinks the story was *for*.
- **Third parties.** A third party present at the telling changes it, much as Goodwin's
  non-addressed recipient does. The teller may glance at them and edit.

### F4. Listeners co-author the story moment by moment, and they signal two separate things

**Finding.** In experiments, distracted listeners produced fewer responses, especially fewer
*specific* responses (wincing, a matching exclamation, finishing a phrase), and the narrators told
their stories significantly worse, above all at the ending. Listeners are co-narrators (Bavelas,
Coates & Johnson 2000). Stivers (2008) separated two signals:

- **Alignment.** Supporting the *activity* of telling: "mm-hm," "yeah," letting the teller keep the
  floor.
- **Affiliation.** Endorsing the teller's *stance*: nods at the right place, "oh no," "the
  bastard."

Vocal continuers only align. Well-timed nods affiliate. A listener can let you finish while
refusing to take your side.

**Grade.** MODERATE. The Bavelas experiments are one lab and two studies, but the result agrees
with the independent CA literature (Goodwin 1984; Stivers 2008). The alignment/affiliation
distinction is widely adopted.

**Sources.** Bavelas, Coates & Johnson 2000. Stivers 2008. Goodwin 1984.

**On the page.**

- **Teller quality depends on the listener.** A Comrade telling it to a rapt friend gives a full,
  well-ended story. The same Comrade telling it to a bored magistrate gives a flat one that
  trails off: "...and, well. That was it."
- **Withheld affiliation is a tell.** The listener says "go on" and "right" but never "oh no."
  Readers feel the coolness without being told. Use it when an interrogator, a skeptical captain,
  or a suspicious elder hears a story.
- **Pursuit of affiliation.** When a teller gets alignment without affiliation, they *pursue* it:
  they repeat the high point, raise the evaluation ("I'm telling you, he *meant* it"), or ask
  outright ("You see?").

### F5. Entitlement: who has the right to tell, and to feel, a story

**Finding.** Sacks observed that "entitlement to experiences" is unevenly distributed. Witnessing a
wreck entitles you to tell it and to be affected by it in ways the person you tell does not
inherit. Second-hand tellers' rights are weaker and decay with each relay. Heritage & Raymond
(2005) showed that speakers constantly mark epistemic rank within ordinary assessments:

- **Downgrading** a first assessment with tags or evidentials when the other person knows better
  ("it's lovely, isn't it?").
- **Upgrading** a second one to reclaim authority ("oh, it *is*" or "I *know*") when they are the
  one with primary rights.

Pomerantz (1984) showed that when there is doubt, people routinely supply their *source or basis*
("I saw," "I heard from," "she told me herself"). Listeners demand the source when the claim
outruns the teller's apparent access.

**Grade.** STRONG for epistemic marking in assessments, widely replicated across CA data sets and
languages; see also Heritage 2012 **[unverified]**. MODERATE for Sacks's entitlement claim, which
is a foundational observation, extended by much later CA work.

**Sources.** Sacks 1992. Heritage & Raymond 2005. Pomerantz 1984.

**On the page.**

- **Evidential stacking** marks distance from the event, and it works with no technology at all:
  "I saw it." / "I had it from Wren, who saw it." / "They say on the docks that..." / "It's told."
  Hearsay accounts should carry two or three layers, and each layer should cost certainty.
- **Source challenges** are natural listener turns: "Who told you that?" "Were you *there*?" "How
  would you know what he said in a locked room?" The answer, or the dodge, characterizes the
  teller.
- **Authority fights in miniature.** A widow describes her husband's death, and a bystander who
  overheard it adds "terrible, it was terrible." The widow upgrades: "You didn't see his *face*."
  That is a rank correction in one line.
- **Over-claiming access** is a lie signature. Someone who reports another person's private thoughts
  or a closed meeting verbatim, with no stated basis, invites "how do you know?"

### F6. Second stories: listeners answer a story with a story

**Finding.** A common response to a story is a **second story**: a matching experience the
recipient tells to show understanding. It has the same moral point, the same kind of trouble, and
often the recipient in the same role. Sacks described the practice. Arminen (2004) showed it
working as therapeutic identification in Alcoholics Anonymous meetings. Second stories can also
**top**: they escalate ("that's nothing — when *I*..."), which turns affiliation into competition.

**Grade.** MODERATE–STRONG. It is a core CA phenomenon, documented across settings and languages.

**Sources.** Sacks 1992. Arminen 2004. Jefferson 1978, on the sequential environment for stories.
Norrick 2000.

**On the page.**

- **Matching second stories** build bonds: in a crew at a fire or in an alehouse, one loss is
  answered with another and nobody comments on it. The scene says "we are the same kind of people"
  without saying it.
- **Topping** marks status games: the young recruit's story is answered by the veteran's bigger one.
- **Declining to top** is a power move or a kindness. The listener who has the bigger story and
  keeps it back.
- **A misfit second story** is a quiet sign of misunderstanding or self-absorption: one that
  matches the topic but misses the moral point.

### F7. Most real stories are small, collaborative, and unfinished

**Finding.** Ochs & Capps (2001) proposed five dimensions along which everyday narratives vary:

- **tellership** (one teller or many)
- **tellability** (high or low)
- **embeddedness** (detached or woven into the surrounding talk)
- **linearity** (closed temporal order, or open and nonlinear)
- **moral stance** (certain and constant, or uncertain and fluid)

The prototypical "Labovian" story is one extreme: a single teller, highly tellable, detached,
linear, morally certain. Most dinner-table talk sits at the other: several tellers, an open ending,
a moral uncertainty worked out jointly. Bamberg & Georgakopoulou's **small stories** extend this to
fragments: hypothetical, ongoing, future, refused ("I'm not going to say what happened"), and
allusions to shared stories ("like that night at the mill").

**Grade.** MODERATE. Several independent ethnographic and discourse-analytic programs. Widely
adopted, though by nature not quantified.

**Sources.** Ochs & Capps 2001. Bamberg & Georgakopoulou 2008. Norrick 2000.

**On the page.**

- **Insiders** gesture at the story instead of telling it: "after Gull Point, nobody trusted the
  tide-reader." Outsiders get the full version. This is the clearest insider/outsider marker
  available.
- **Refusals** are stories: "I'll not speak of the cellar" tells the reader there was a cellar.
- **Open moral stance.** Let a group's account end in "...and I still don't know if he was right."
  The certain, sealed story is more likely to be the rehearsed or self-serving one.

### F8. Co-narration: shared stories, shared rights, contested tellings

**Finding.** When two people lived through an event together, both have rights to tell it.
Mandelbaum (1987) showed couples:

- jointly launching stories
- one partner prompting the other
- one taking the punchline
- correcting each other's details, sometimes in a supportive way and sometimes as a challenge

Ochs & Taylor (1992) documented family dinner-table narratives in which children and mothers
introduce stories about themselves. Fathers took the role of "primary recipient" and problematizer,
judging the protagonist, which is the "Father knows best" dynamic. Status therefore governs who
evaluates, not just who tells.

**Grade.** MODERATE. Independent CA and ethnographic studies agree; the family study is a single
community sample (Southern California, U.S.), so the gendered pattern is CONTESTED as a
generalization.

**Sources.** Mandelbaum 1987. Ochs & Taylor 1992. Ochs & Capps 2001. Norrick 1997 on retold
familiar stories **[unverified]**.

**On the page.**

- **Correction in shared tellings.** "It was *three* of them." "Two." "Three, Mara, there was the
  one in the boat." Who wins these small fights tells you who holds the story.
- **The judge role.** The senior or powerful person at the table often says little and *judges*:
  "And you let him?" A story told upward to a superior gets evaluated, not affiliated with.
- **Prompting reveals the punchline.** "Tell them what the priest said" hands the climax to the
  partner. The prompter has heard it before.
- **Family legends** are told by several people in fixed slots. A newcomer at the table hears a
  performance, not a report.

### F9. Performance: when tellers switch into display mode

**Finding.** Bauman treats performance as a *frame*: the teller takes responsibility to an audience
for a display of skill. The frame is keyed by recognizable markers:

- special formulas ("you know what they say about...")
- appeals to tradition
- parallelism, special prosody or register
- disclaimers of performance ("I'm no good at telling this")

Bauman (1986) studied Texas tall tales, hunting yarns, and practical-joke stories. He showed how
the *narrated event*, the *text*, and the *telling event* shape each other. A practical joke is
staged partly so that it can be told later.

**Grade.** MODERATE. It is a foundational framework in folklore and linguistic anthropology, used
across many independent ethnographies, and qualitative.

**Sources.** Bauman 1986. Tannen 1989. Labov 2013, on the vernacular roots of epic style.

**On the page.**

- **Performance-keying moves**: a formulaic opener ("now this was in the dry year..."), audience
  address ("you'll like this"), a modesty disclaimer that precedes a polished telling, and a
  pause for laughter.
- **Polish signals repetition.** A performed story has rhythm and set phrases because it has been
  told many times, which makes it engaging and less reliable at once.
- **Performance changes the events.** Actions done "for the story" (a stunt, a theatrical refusal)
  in the narrated event show a person who lives with an audience in mind.

### F10. Voices and tense: constructed dialogue and the historical present

**Finding.** Tannen (1989) argues that "reported speech" in conversation is usually **constructed
dialogue**: not a recording but the teller's creation, often of things never said or said by
groups ("and everyone's like, *no way*"). It is an *involvement* strategy that animates the scene.
Holt (1996) showed that direct reported speech lets tellers present evidence and convey an
assessment of the quoted party *without stating it*, through the voice they give them.

**Tense switching.** Tellers move into the **historical present** ("so he comes in, and he says...")
in the complicating action. Wolfson (1979) argued that the *switch* between past and present, not
the present itself, does the work, marking episode boundaries. Schiffrin (1981), in 73 narratives,
found roughly 30% of the complicating-action verbs in the historical present, with switches
clustering at moments of internal evaluation.

**Grade.** STRONG for the existence and frequency of constructed dialogue and of present/past
switching in English. MODERATE for the specific functional accounts, where Wolfson's and
Schiffrin's emphases differ.

**Sources.** Tannen 1989. Holt 1996. Wolfson 1979. Schiffrin 1981.

**On the page.**

- **Quote the dialogue the teller couldn't have heard word for word.** It reads as authentic
  speech. Quoting it with perfect accuracy is the oddity: real tellers paraphrase, condense, and
  voice crowds ("and the whole yard's shouting *let him go*").
- **Mimicry as judgment.** The teller gives the enemy a whiny or pompous voice. The account's bias
  lives in the *voicing*, not in any stated opinion.
- **Tense slips at the climax.** "We came round the point and — there she *is*, broadside, and the
  captain *says*..." Present tense at the peak, past tense in the frame.
- **Constructed inner speech**: "I thought, well, that's me done." This is Labov's embedded
  evaluation, and it is the most natural way to report fear without saying "I was afraid."

### F11. Telling changes the teller's memory, and the audience changes the telling

**Finding.** People retell an event selectively, and the retelling then reshapes their memory.

- In diary data, participants judged 61% of their own real-life retellings as distorted
  (exaggerated, minimized, with omissions or additions), yet called only 42% inaccurate. They
  changed stories for different audiences: exaggerating to entertain, simplifying to inform (Marsh
  & Tversky 2004).
- In the **saying-is-believing** paradigm, speakers tune a description to the listener's known
  attitude and then remember the event *as tuned*, especially when they are motivated to create a
  shared reality with that listener (Echterhoff, Higgins & Levine 2009).
- Pasupathi (2001) reviewed how listeners' responses and conversational goals shape which past
  events are remembered and how.

**Grade.** MODERATE–STRONG for audience-dependent change in retellings. Several independent labs and
paradigms converge, with a review (Pasupathi 2001; see also Hirst & Echterhoff 2012
**[unverified]**). The *magnitude* of memory bias from audience tuning varies with the motivation
condition, so treat the size of any effect cautiously.

**Sources.** Marsh & Tversky 2004. Echterhoff, Higgins & Levine 2009. Pasupathi 2001.

**On the page.**

- **The same event, two tellings, one teller.** Lighter and funnier for friends, plainer and shorter
  for an official, bleaker for someone who shares the teller's grievance. Readers should catch the
  drift.
- **Honest distortion is common and unconscious.** A Comrade who has told a story at forty fires
  sincerely believes the polished version. Rounded numbers, a tidier sequence, and a better
  closing line are signs of age, not of lying.
- **Audience-tuned memory** is a good source of mistaken accounts: a soldier who has only told the
  battle to his own side now remembers it their way.

### F12. Stories position the teller: identity is done in the telling

**Finding.** Bamberg's **positioning** analysis works at three levels:

- how characters are positioned against each other in the story world
- how the teller positions themselves toward the listener in the telling
- how the teller positions themselves against larger cultural master narratives ("who I am")

Small-stories research shows identity work happening in fragments ("that's typical of me"), not
only in life-story interviews. Second stories (F6) and topping are positioning moves.

**Grade.** MODERATE. A widely used analytic framework with many independent applications; it is
qualitative.

**Sources.** Bamberg 1997. Bamberg & Georgakopoulou 2008. Arminen 2004.

**On the page.**

- **Self as character versus self as teller.** A teller can make their past self foolish while
  showing their present self as wise: "I was young and stupid then." That is a classic
  credibility-and-identity move.
- **Master narratives as quotation.** "Like the old saying..." "Same as every lord before him."
  The teller fits the event to a ready-made cultural story, which is where prejudice and propaganda
  enter.
- **Agency grammar.** Watch who is the grammatical subject. "The gate was opened" (no agent),
  "they opened it" (blame), "I opened it" (ownership). It is one of the cheapest bias tells to
  write.

### F13. Age, gender, and status differences: real but weaker than folklore suggests

**Finding.**

- **Age.** Kemper et al. (1990) found age-group differences in the hierarchical structure,
  syntax, and cohesion of narratives told by adults in their 60s, 70s, and 80s. Older narrators
  produced more elaborate multi-episode structure, alongside simpler syntax in the oldest group.
  James et al. (1998) found older adults more *off-topic* ("verbose") in autobiographical
  storytelling, yet judges rated their stories as more interesting and of higher quality.
- **Gender.** Johnstone (1993), studying Midwestern U.S. conversational stories, found men's
  stories more often about contests, with named place and objects, and the teller as a lone agent
  acting against challenge. Women's stories were more often about community and involved other
  people's help, with the teller acting with others. Coates (2003) reported similar patterns
  in British all-male friendship talk **[unverified]**.
- **Status.** Ochs & Taylor (1992) show status governing who *judges* stories at the family table
  (F8).

**Grade.**

- **Age:** MODERATE. There are several independent lab studies, though age-and-narrative findings
  vary with task, and "verbosity" differences are partly task-specific.
- **Gender:** CONTESTED/WEAK as a generalization. The studies are small, culture-bound, and mostly
  qualitative, and the difference is a tendency, not a rule. Treat it as a single community's
  pattern, not a law of nature.
- **Status-governs-evaluation:** MODERATE (F8).

**Sources.** Kemper et al. 1990. James et al. 1998. Johnstone 1993. Ochs & Taylor 1992.

**On the page.**

- **Elders** wander and circle back, add genealogy and side-stories, and are often the best
  tellers in the room. "That was the year the Dellen girl married, no — the year after." Don't
  write them as incoherent. Write them as *expansive*.
- **Contest-shaped and community-shaped stories.** Contest stories have one hero, a challenge, a
  win. Community stories are full of "and then Ada came with the rope." Give these to characters by
  *temperament and culture*, not strictly by sex, since the evidence doesn't support a hard gender
  line.
- **Low-status tellers** telling upward hedge more, leave evaluation to the listener ("I don't know
  what you'd make of it, sir"), and leave out what would make them look bad.

### F14. What separates truthful from deceptive telling (and why it is a weak signal)

**Finding.** The largest meta-analysis of deception cues (DePaulo et al. 2003; 158 cues, 1,338
estimates) found liars:

- are less forthcoming and tell less compelling tales
- make a more negative impression and are more tense
- produce stories with **fewer ordinary imperfections and unusual contents**, such as spontaneous
  corrections, admitted lapses of memory, and superfluous odd details

Cues were stronger when liars were highly motivated, especially for identity-relevant stakes, and
when lies concerned transgressions. A meta-analysis of computer-coded linguistic cues (Hauch et al.
2015; 44 studies) found liars use:

- more negative-emotion words
- more distancing
- fewer sensory-perceptual words
- fewer cognitive-process words ("I think," "I realized")

**Grade.** STRONG that these cues exist as averages (meta-analytic). STRONG that **effect sizes are
small** and most individual cues are unreliable; many cues showed no link to deception at all.
Untrained human accuracy at spotting lies is only slightly above chance, around 54% in Bond &
DePaulo 2006 **[unverified]**.

**Sources.** DePaulo et al. 2003. Hauch et al. 2015.

**On the page.**

- **Imperfect truth.** The honest teller self-corrects ("no — it was the second bell"), admits gaps
  ("I don't remember how I got down"), and includes useless odd details ("there was a cat on the
  rail, I don't know why I remember that").
- **The rehearsed lie** is too clean: linear, no self-correction, no irrelevant detail. It is thin on
  sensation (what it smelled and sounded like) and thin on inner process ("I didn't think, I just
  went").
- **Distancing.** "The boy was struck" for "I struck him." "That woman" for a named acquaintance.
- **Avoid making lies obvious.** The research says cues are weak, and a perceptive listener catches
  a liar through *inconsistency with known facts and sources* (F5), not body language. Let
  detection come from a source challenge or a contradiction, not from a twitching eye.

## 3. Implications by teller state

**Informed** (was there, telling in good faith):

- Primary epistemic rights and a stated basis ("I was on the wall").
- Reports access the teller actually had: their own senses, what they heard said aloud.
- Imperfections: self-corrections, admitted gaps, odd irrelevant details (F14).
- Evaluation densest at the true high point (F1).
- Tense switching at the peak (F10).
- Will upgrade against second-hand challengers ("I *saw* it") (F5).
- Can still be partial: the start point and deletions still carry their stance (F2).

**Uninformed** (telling at a remove, honestly):

- Layered evidentials ("I had it from...") and downgraded certainty ("so they say").
- A thin orientation and complicating action, with evaluation borrowed from whoever told them.
- Tends toward master narratives and ready-made shapes (F12).
- Constructed dialogue is generic and crowd-voiced (F10).
- Yields when challenged on source ("well, I wasn't there") (F5).

**Mistaken** (sincere but wrong):

- Fluent, often well-polished from many retellings (F9, F11), and confident.
- The audience-tuned version has overwritten memory (F11). It will carry *real* imperfections
  (corrections, gaps), unlike the liar's, because the teller isn't managing an impression.
- The error usually lives in sequence, numbers, or attribution: who said it, which came first.
- When confronted with a contradicting source, the mistaken teller is puzzled or argues from their
  experience. They do not dodge the source question (F5).

**Deliberately lying:**

- Over-clean and linear, with few self-corrections or superfluous details (F14).
- Thin on sensory and inner-process language (F14).
- Over-claims access: reports closed rooms and private thoughts with no basis (F5).
- The start point is pushed to the other side's provocation, and the incriminating event is
  deleted with a smoothing "anyway" (F2).
- Distancing grammar for their own acts (F12, F14).
- Under a source challenge: deflects, re-asserts, or counter-attacks rather than naming a checkable
  source (F5).
- May over-pursue affiliation ("you'd have done the same") (F4).
- Keep all of this subtle. The real signal is weak, and detection should come from inconsistency
  with facts (F14).

## 4. Implications by account type

**Protagonist series (reliable narration).** Even reliable narrators shape: the narrator chooses
the start point, the high point, and the coda (F1, F2). Use the evaluation machinery (suspension
before the climax, negatives and counterfactuals, embedded inner speech) to make the narration feel
spoken and lived rather than reported. Reliability shows as an absence of the lie signatures and the
presence of honest imperfection. Dialogue *inside* the narration still behaves conversationally:
listeners align or affiliate (F4), stories get second stories (F6), and power governs who judges
(F8).

**Comrade Account (ally, good faith, partial).** The most natural home for collaborative
narration:

- co-tellers correct each other (F8)
- insider allusions replace full tellings (F7)
- matching second stories and topping among crewmates (F6)
- polished set-piece versions of crew legends that have drifted honestly over many retellings (F9,
  F11)

The partiality is in what the Comrade *couldn't* access, which they will often mark honestly ("I
was below, so I only heard it"), and in affectionate voicing of the hero (F10). Let a Comrade end on
an open moral stance (F7).

**Adversary Account (enemy, self-interested).** Shaped by blame allocation:

- the story begins at the other side's provocation, and the self-incriminating event is deleted
  (F2)
- distancing agency grammar for their own side's acts, vivid agency for the enemy's (F12)
- mocking constructed dialogue for the enemy (F10)
- master-narrative framing ("rebels always...") (F12)

An Adversary can be *sincere and mistaken*: audience-tuned within their own camp (F11). That is
often more interesting than outright lying. Where they do lie, the cues are over-cleanliness and
over-claimed access (F14, F5).

**Dossier (institutional record).** Written institutional records strip the conversational layer.
Expect:

- no evaluation devices, or evaluation laundered into category labels
- complicating action reduced to dated, numbered clauses (F1)
- evidentials formalized ("per informant," "unconfirmed," "corroborated by two sources") (F5)

A Dossier's bias shows through what it treats as reportable (F2), what it deletes, and its agentless
passives (F12). Where it quotes testimony, the spoken account inside it should retain conversational
traces (hedges, corrections) that the clerk's frame then flattens. That contrast is itself
characterization.

**Hearsay (civilians at a remove).** Maximal evidential layering, and each relay costs detail and
gains shape (F5, F11). Hearsay drifts toward the reportable extreme (bigger numbers, cleaner hero
and villain) while losing the credibility anchors (F2). It runs on:

- crowd-voiced constructed dialogue ("and they all cried...") (F10)
- performance formulas and tradition appeals (F9)
- master narratives (F12)
- small-story allusions ("you know what happened at the ford") (F7)

When challenged ("who saw it?"), hearsay tellers retreat or name a vague authority ("everyone
knows"). Elders' hearsay is expansive and genealogical, and often the best-told (F13).
