# Rumor and Legend: How Accounts Form and Drift Person to Person

Evidence synthesis for the account-types framework (protagonist series, Comrade Account, Adversary Account, Dossier, Hearsay). This file covers what the research says about how a true event changes as it passes from mouth to mouth. Hearsay accounts depend on it most.

---

## 1. Scope and search method

**Question.** When a true event is retold by people who did not witness it, what changes, in what direction, how fast, and what stays stable? What happens when someone tries to correct it?

**Searched.** Web searches (October 2026) on: Allport & Postman's laws of rumor and the critiques of them; Bartlett's serial reproduction; transmission-chain experiments and reviews of content bias (Mesoudi, Whiten, Stubbersfield, Tehrani, Flynn, Bebbington, Kashima, Moussaïd, Xu & Griffiths); anxiety and uncertainty in rumor (Rosnow, DiFonzo & Bordia, Shibutani, Caplow, Peterson & Gist, Buckner); urban and contemporary legend (Brunvand, Fine, Heath et al.); misattribution and the Matthew effect (Merton, O'Toole); misinformation spread (Vosoughi et al., Pennycook & Rand); repetition and truth (Dechêne et al.); correction (Lewandowsky et al., Walter & Murphy, Chan et al., Ecker et al.); and how oral traditions stay stable (Rubin, Vansina).

**Sources.** 34 distinct sources. That includes 3 meta-analyses (Dechêne et al. 2010; Chan et al. 2017; Walter & Murphy 2018), 4 narrative or systematic reviews (Mesoudi & Whiten 2008; Lewandowsky et al. 2012; Ecker et al. 2022; Stubbersfield 2022), one meta-analytic review essay (Rosnow 1991), and primary experiments, field studies, and classic theory. Citations were verified by web search unless marked **[unverified]**. Unverified items are standard classics whose bibliographic details are well known, but the search budget ran out before they could be checked this session.

**Generalizing from online studies.** Findings from Twitter-scale studies are used only where they plausibly hold for face-to-face talk, criers, and broadsheets. Each one is flagged.

**Grading.**
- **STRONG:** meta-analysis, or replicated across independent groups.
- **MODERATE:** several independent studies, or a foundational, widely accepted framework.
- **CONTESTED/WEAK:** a single study, a disputed result, or a failed replication.

## 2. Findings

### F1. Stories shorten fast, then stabilize around a core

Most of the loss happens in the first one to three retellings. After that the story settles into a short, stable form that later tellers pass on with little further change. Allport & Postman called this *leveling*. In their chain demonstrations, about 70% of details were lost within five or six transmissions, and most of that loss came early. Bartlett (1932) found the same pattern of shortening and conventionalizing in serial reproduction. Modern diffusion-chain work replicates it: Moussaïd, Brighton & Gaissmaier (2015) found messages became "shorter, gradually inaccurate, and increasingly dissimilar between chains." The dissimilarity is the key point. Separate chains starting from the same event do not converge on one wrong version. Each one settles on its own short version.

**Grade: STRONG** for the shortening itself (replicated from 1932 to 2015 across independent labs). **MODERATE** for the exact numbers, which depend on the material and the setup.

**Sources:** Allport & Postman 1947; Bartlett 1932; Moussaïd et al. 2015; Mesoudi & Whiten 2008 (review).

**On the page.**
- A first-remove teller still has the scaffolding: which gate, what hour, who stood where. A third-remove teller has about one sentence and a judgment. For example: "He walked in, the guards dropped their spears, and the warden was dead by morning."
- Two Hearsay accounts from different quarters of the same city should disagree with each other, not only with the truth. A dockside version and a market version of one raid each keep a different surviving fragment.
- Connective tissue goes first: why, in what order, what came before. Short, nameable images survive: a red coat, a bell, a number.

### F2. Sharpening: the details that survive grow larger and more central

The few details that survive leveling get exaggerated, moved to the center, and given causal weight. Allport & Postman called this *sharpening*. Survivors tend to be odd or vivid items, numbers, motion, and labels. Moussaïd et al. (2015) found that while message content degraded, the *risk perception* the message carried passed on faithfully and became more extreme, because each teller reshaped the facts to fit their own concern. Bebbington et al. (2017) found that in four-person chains, ambiguous events were resolved more and more negatively at each retelling.

**Grade: MODERATE.** The direction (toward the vivid and the threatening) replicates. The specific mechanisms Allport & Postman proposed have been critiqued (see F4).

**Sources:** Allport & Postman 1947; Moussaïd et al. 2015; Bebbington et al. 2017.

**On the page.**
- "He killed a man on the pier" becomes "he killed the king's whole guard on the pier."
- A side detail (he wore no armor) becomes the explanation (he needs no armor).
- The emotional judgment travels better than the facts under it. A Hearsay teller can get every particular wrong and still be exactly right that "the warden was afraid of him." Let the feeling be accurate while the facts go wrong.

### F3. Assimilation: stories bend toward what the teller already expects

Retellings drift toward the teller's existing beliefs, stereotypes, and stock plots. Bartlett's English participants turned the Native American tale "The War of the Ghosts" into something more familiar: canoes became boats, and the supernatural logic dropped out. Kashima (2000) sent a gender-stereotype-relevant story along five-person chains. Inconsistent details were recalled well by the first person, but stereotype-consistent details came to dominate further down the chain. Lyons & Kashima (2003) showed the effect is strongest when tellers believe the stereotype is *shared* by their audience. Stubbersfield (2022) reviews this as a "stereotype-consistent content bias."

**Grade: STRONG** for chain-level assimilation to stereotypes (several independent labs). **CONTESTED** for Bartlett's exact original procedure; see F4 on replication.

**Sources:** Bartlett 1932; Kashima 2000; Lyons & Kashima 2003; Stubbersfield 2022.

**On the page.**
- Each community remakes the hero in its own image. Dockworkers make him a dockworker who "learned to fight in the yards." Nobles make him a disinherited lord. Priests give him a vision.
- An unfamiliar detail, such as an unknown weapon or a foreign custom, is quietly replaced with the nearest familiar one. "A black blade that hummed" becomes "a sword."
- The drift is strongest when the teller assumes the listener shares the frame. A tavern full of people who already hate the Trust will polish the story into "the Trust's men ran."

### F4. The classic "laws" are partly overstated: real rumors can elaborate, grow, and sometimes improve

Allport & Postman's wartime picture of rumor as steady decay is partly an artifact of their lab method. Participants passed a description of a picture, with no ability to ask questions and no stake in the outcome. Field studies disagree:
- Caplow (1947) studied rumors among soldiers in the Pacific and found many became *more* accurate as they spread. Stakes were high, and listeners checked what they heard.
- Peterson & Gist (1951) found a community rumor about a local murder grew more elaborate as it spread, rather than leveling.
- Buckner (1965) proposed that the direction of change depends on how critical the listeners are and on how the network is structured.
- Shibutani (1966) reframed rumor as *improvised news*: collective sensemaking when official channels fail.
- Bordia & DiFonzo (2004) content-analyzed 14 real rumor discussions and found a large share of statements were sensemaking and problem-solving.
- DiFonzo & Bordia (2007) review evidence that rumors in established, long-running networks (such as workplaces) are often largely accurate.
- Treadway & McCloskey (1987) showed that later textbooks *misreported the Allport–Postman study itself*. The famous "razor moved to the Black man's hand" result was much weaker and less consistent than it is usually cited. In other words, the study of distortion was itself distorted in retelling.

On Bartlett: Bergman & Roediger (1999) **[unverified]** report that his repeated-reproduction findings replicate only partly once instructions and scoring are controlled.

**Grade: MODERATE** that both decay and elaboration happen, with context deciding which. **CONTESTED** for the universality of the "basic laws."

**Sources:** Caplow 1947; Peterson & Gist 1951; Buckner 1965; Shibutani 1966 [unverified]; Bordia & DiFonzo 2004; DiFonzo & Bordia 2007; Treadway & McCloskey 1987.

**On the page.**
- Not all Hearsay should be worse than the truth. A crew that depends on knowing where the Directorate patrols are will self-correct. Each teller checks against what they have seen, and the version improves.
- A tight network (one crew, one guild, one dock) produces accurate rumor. A loose one (a whole city, a market, strangers on the road) produces decay and elaboration.
- Elaboration and leveling are different failures. The first adds invented motive and dialogue. The second deletes. A Hearsay voice can do both at once: "he never said a word the whole time, but then he turned and told the warden, 'Your ledger's short.'" The quote is invented, and it is invented to explain the silence.

### F5. Rumor rises with anxiety and ambiguity, and with how much the story matters to the teller

Allport & Postman's "basic law" says rumor intensity is roughly the importance of the subject multiplied by its ambiguity (R ~ i × a). Later work kept the core and revised the terms:
- Rosnow (1991) synthesized the evidence, including a meta-analytic summary. Personal anxiety and general uncertainty predicted transmission. So did credulity (how believable the rumor seemed). Importance, as Allport & Postman had defined it, mattered less than assumed.
- DiFonzo & Bordia (2007) organize transmission around three motives: fact-finding, relationship enhancement, and self-enhancement. All three are amplified by uncertainty.
- Shibutani (1966) [unverified]: when demand for news outruns the supply from official channels, people improvise their own.

Not everything in this area replicates. Bebbington et al. (2017) *did not* find that elevated state anxiety increased the transmission of negative content in their chains.

**Grade: MODERATE.** The anxiety and uncertainty link is supported by a meta-analytic review and by field work. Experimental manipulations of anxiety are mixed.

**Sources:** Allport & Postman 1947; Rosnow 1991; DiFonzo & Bordia 2007; Shibutani 1966 [unverified]; Bebbington et al. 2017.

**On the page.**
- Rumor peaks right after an event and right before an expected one. The night after a raid, and the week before a tax collection, are when the story multiplies.
- An official silence or a sealed notice ("By order of the Directorate, the matter is closed") produces *more* rumor, not less.
- People pass rumors to be useful ("you should know"), to bond ("did you hear"), and to look important ("my cousin was there"). Give each Hearsay voice one of these motives. It decides what they emphasize.

### F6. Content bias: social, threatening, negative, and disgusting material survives best

Some kinds of content survive transmission better than others, all else equal:
- **Social information.** Mesoudi, Whiten & Dunbar (2006) found gossip and social-interaction information was passed more accurately and more fully than equivalent non-social information.
- **Social and survival information in legends.** Stubbersfield, Tehrani & Flynn (2015) found legends with social or survival information were recalled more accurately than control material, and social and combined legends better than survival-only ones. Choosing what to receive and what to pass on showed weaker or null effects, so the bias seems to work mainly through memory.
- **Negative information.** Bebbington et al. (2017) found it survives preferentially, and ambiguous events resolve negatively.
- **Disgust.** Heath, Bell & Sternberg (2001) found people preferred to pass on the most disgusting version of a legend, and that legends with more disgust motifs spread more widely.

Stubbersfield (2022) reviews these biases across three phases: choosing to receive, encoding and recall, and choosing to transmit.

**Grade: STRONG** for the social and negativity biases in chains (several independent groups, plus a review). **MODERATE** for disgust (one multi-study paper, plus consistent online data).

**Sources:** Mesoudi, Whiten & Dunbar 2006; Stubbersfield, Tehrani & Flynn 2015; Bebbington et al. 2017; Heath, Bell & Sternberg 2001; Stubbersfield 2022.

**On the page.**
- What survives is *who did what to whom*. Who betrayed whom, who was humiliated, who knelt. Tactical and logistical detail (the tide, the angle of the gate) dies early.
- The bloodiest or most grotesque version wins. A bloodless liberation tends to grow a body count in the retelling, and a quick death grows lingering details.
- To show a Hearsay account drifting, add one more social beat and one more disgusting beat per remove. Never add a logistical one.

### F7. Numbers, names, places, and dates drift in predictable directions

Specific values regress toward what the teller expects. Xu & Griffiths (2010) model serial reproduction as Bayesian reconstruction: each person combines the noisy trace with their prior, so across a chain the values converge on the *prior distribution* rather than the original. Their experiments with simple one-dimensional stimuli confirm it. Applied to stories, this predicts:
- **Numbers** round to culturally salient values (dozens, hundreds, "a thousand").
- **Names** collapse onto more familiar names, or onto the most famous person available (see F8).
- **Places** move to better-known locations.
- **Dates** cluster on memorable anchors ("the year of the flood").

Allport & Postman's sharpening also predicts that surviving numbers inflate when they carry threat. Moussaïd et al. (2015) and Bebbington et al. (2017) support the direction toward threat.

**Grade: MODERATE.** Prior-convergence is formally modeled and experimentally supported, but mostly with simple stimuli. Its application to numbers inside narratives is an inference that fits the chain data, not a direct, replicated test.

**Sources:** Xu & Griffiths 2010; Moussaïd et al. 2015; Bebbington et al. 2017; Allport & Postman 1947.

**On the page.**
- 1,804 dead becomes "nearly two thousand," then "thousands." 5,100 freed becomes "five thousand," then "the whole Maw." 17 becomes "a score," and 3 becomes "a handful."
- A minor named participant ("Corren Halst") becomes "one of his men," and then disappears.
- An obscure place ("Killane's east sluice") becomes the nearest landmark everyone knows ("under Killane"), then just the region.
- Dates attach to memorable public events. "It was the winter the river froze" replaces "the eleventh of the second month."
- An odd, precise number in a Hearsay account is a sign the teller is close to the source, or is reading from a broadsheet. Use precision itself as a credibility clue.

### F8. Legends migrate to famous figures (the Matthew effect in attribution)

Anonymous deeds and sayings get reassigned to the most famous plausible person. Merton (1968) named the Matthew effect for scientific credit: eminent people receive disproportionate credit for shared or equal work. O'Toole (2017) documents the same pattern in quotation history. Famous figures (Lincoln, Twain, Einstein, Churchill) become "hosts" for lines they never said, often by stages: anonymous, then a minor figure, then a famous one. Folklorists describe *migratory legends*: the same plot attaches to new local figures and places in each region. Brunvand 1981 [unverified] and Fine 1992 [unverified] both document legends that recur across places with local details swapped in.

**Grade: MODERATE.** Merton's effect is well established in citation research. Quotation migration is extensively documented case by case. The migratory-legend pattern is a core, widely accepted claim of folklore scholarship.

**Sources:** Merton 1968; O'Toole 2017; Brunvand 1981 [unverified]; Fine 1992 [unverified].

**On the page.**
- Once a Kanja alias is famous, unrelated deeds collect under it. A daring escape someone else pulled off becomes "the Scourge did that." A local crew's sabotage becomes "Bane's work."
- The reverse happens too: a famous figure's real deed is credited to a better-known one.
- Sayings drift upward. A crew member's line becomes the Captain's. The Captain's line becomes Haku's. This is the in-world mechanism behind "legend's own drift."
- The same story shows up in three cities with three local heroes: "Our Ogoun did the same thing at the gate." Let readers spot the template under the local paint.

### F9. Friend-of-a-friend attribution and the credibility frame

Contemporary legends are characteristically told about someone just out of reach: a friend of a friend, a cousin's coworker. The teller is close enough to vouch for the story and far enough away that it cannot be checked. This is a central, widely accepted observation in legend scholarship: Brunvand 1981 [unverified]; Fine 1992 [unverified]. It ties to the transmission motives in DiFonzo & Bordia (2007). Relationship and self-enhancement motives reward tellers for claiming nearness to the source. Rumor scholarship also notes that tellers routinely attach credibility markers ("I heard it from someone who was there") that do not track actual access.

**Grade: MODERATE.** A foundational folklore observation, consistent with the rumor-motive literature. It is not quantified by a meta-analysis.

**Sources:** Brunvand 1981 [unverified]; Fine 1992 [unverified]; DiFonzo & Bordia 2007.

**On the page.**
- Hearsay voices nearly always claim a near-witness: "My sister's husband hauled on that line." "A boy from our street was in the pens."
- The claimed witness is never available: dead, moved away, "doesn't talk about it."
- Two removes look like one. A third-hand teller presents the story with first-hand confidence. Give the reader a way to count the real removes, such as a detail the claimed witness could not have known.

### F10. Repetition breeds belief; novelty and emotion drive spread

Hearing a claim again makes it feel truer, independent of whether it is true. Dechêne et al. (2010) meta-analyzed 51 studies of this *truth effect*: d ≈ 0.39–0.50 (a medium effect). Vosoughi, Roy & Aral (2018) studied about 126,000 rumor cascades on Twitter. False stories spread farther, faster, and more broadly than true ones. They were more novel, and they drew fear, disgust, and surprise, while true stories drew anticipation, sadness, joy, and trust. Pennycook & Rand (2019) found that belief in false headlines tracked a lack of reflective thinking more than partisan motivation. People who stopped to think were better at telling true from false, whatever their politics.

**Generalization to oral settings.** The truth effect comes from lab studies of spoken and written statements, so it transfers well. The Twitter speed asymmetry is a platform measurement. What plausibly carries over to oral settings is the *mechanism*, not the speed: novelty and fear/disgust/surprise get a story retold. The Pennycook & Rand finding implies that a listener who stops and questions a story is the natural check on it.

**Grade: STRONG** for the truth effect (meta-analysis). **MODERATE** for the novelty/emotion advantage of falsehood: one very large observational study, consistent with F6's lab results, with the platform-specific caveat. **MODERATE** for "lazy, not biased": influential, replicated in follow-ups by its authors and others, though the motivated-reasoning debate continues.

**Sources:** Dechêne et al. 2010; Vosoughi, Roy & Aral 2018; Pennycook & Rand 2019.

**On the page.**
- The version heard most often wins, whatever its source. A crier repeating "the Trust restored order at the wharf" every morning slowly overwrites what people saw.
- The surprising version beats the plausible one. "He was never wounded" travels faster than "he was cut twice and bled for a week."
- Give the skeptic in a Hearsay scene a concrete move: "How would your cousin know what he said, if he was in the pens?" Have the crowd ignore it.

### F11. Corrections help, but the original keeps working

Once a story is believed, a retraction reduces its influence without eliminating it. Lewandowsky et al. (2012) call this the *continued-influence effect*: people go on using retracted information in their reasoning, especially when the retraction leaves a gap in their causal account. Three meta-analyses agree:
- **Chan et al. (2017)**, k = 52, N = 6,878. Debunking works, but misinformation persists after it (d ≈ 0.75–1.06). Persistence is stronger when audiences have generated their own reasons for the misinformation. Debunking works better when the audience builds an explanation for the correction.
- **Walter & Murphy (2018)**, k = 65, N = 23,604. Corrections have a moderate effect. They are harder for political and real-world misinformation than for health or invented material. Rebuttals beat forewarnings, and appeals to *coherence* beat simple fact-checks and appeals to credibility.
- **Ecker et al. (2022)** review the cognitive, social, and affective drivers. A correction must supply an *alternative explanation*, not just a denial.

**Grade: STRONG** (two meta-analyses plus major reviews converge).

**Sources:** Lewandowsky et al. 2012; Chan et al. 2017; Walter & Murphy 2018; Ecker et al. 2022.

**On the page.**
- A broadsheet retraction ("The Directorate regrets the error; no prisoners were executed") rarely kills the story. People keep acting on it, and the denial becomes part of the legend: "they even had to deny it."
- Corrections that work in fiction replace the story: "There were no executions. The cells were empty because the Scourge took them all out by the sea gate." Bare denials don't.
- A listener who has argued for a version (defended it in the tavern) is the hardest to move. They have generated their own reasons.
- Corrections from the "wrong" side bounce off. An Adversary-sourced correction reaching a sympathetic audience usually makes things worse.

### F12. What keeps a tradition stable: form, constraint, and custodians

Not all oral material decays. Rubin (1995) showed that oral traditions such as ballads, epics, and counting-out rhymes stay stable over generations when several constraints overlap: theme, imagery, and sound pattern (rhyme, meter, alliteration). Each constraint limits what can be substituted. Vansina (1985) **[unverified]**, working on African oral traditions as historical sources, distinguishes kinds of tradition by their fixity. Formal, memorized texts kept by recognized custodians change slowly. Free-form accounts change fast and are rebuilt for each audience. This matches the "tight network" result in F4 (Caplow; DiFonzo & Bordia): stability comes from structure and checking.

**Grade: MODERATE.** A foundational framework built on substantial empirical work on oral-tradition stability. Not meta-analyzed.

**Sources:** Rubin 1995; Vansina 1985 [unverified]; Caplow 1947; DiFonzo & Bordia 2007.

**On the page.**
- A ballad, a chant, or a counting rhyme about an event keeps its rhymed line nearly intact for generations, while everything around it decays. "Eighteen-oh-four at the Rookery door" survives. The date in prose does not.
- The single exact detail in a decayed Hearsay account is often the one locked into a rhyme or a chant. That gives the reader a reason to trust it.
- Recognized keepers (a guild remembrancer, a temple chanter, a crew's ledger-keeper like Garren Hask) are the in-world source of stability. When the keeper dies, the account starts drifting.

## 3. Implications by teller state

**Informed** (witnessed it or holds the record):
- **Errors:** leveling of connective detail and self-serving emphasis. Precise numbers and names survive, along with odd, specific, unflattering details that a rumor would have smoothed away.
- **Strongest tell:** precision that doesn't help the teller.
- **Risk:** the informed teller assimilates too, toward their own role (F3). Even eyewitnesses enlarge their own part.

**Uninformed** (passing on what they heard, in good faith):
- **Errors:** all of F1–F8 at full strength. Round numbers, famous names, famous places. Social and emotional content kept, logistics dropped. Friend-of-a-friend sourcing.
- **Confidence:** often high (F9, F10), and unrelated to accuracy.
- **Self-correction:** possible in a tight network with stakes (F4).
- **On the page:** sincere, warm, wrong in specifics, right about the feeling.

**Mistaken** (once informed, now holding a wrong belief, often after a correction that didn't take):
- **Errors:** continued influence (F11). They may fold the retraction into the story ("they denied it, which proves it").
- **Error pattern:** confidently wrong on the one load-bearing fact, accurate on the rest, which makes them persuasive.
- **On the page:** a veteran who was really there, who swears the Captain killed the warden because that is how he resolved a gap in what he saw.

**Deliberately lying** (the Adversary, a propagandist, an official notice):
- **How:** they exploit the same biases, by design. They supply a coherent alternative explanation (F11), repeat it through criers and broadsheets (F10), fit it to audience stereotypes (F3), and add social and threat content (F6).
- **Tells:** deliberate lies are often *too* well-formed. They have causal connectives a real rumor would have lost, and they avoid odd details.
- **Precision as a tell:** they often carry *specific, unsourced* numbers meant to sound like records. Compare F7: real rumor rounds its numbers.
- **On the page:** a crier's version arrives already leveled and sharpened, as if it had been through ten tellings on its first day.

## 4. Implications by account type

**Protagonist series (reliable narration).** The baseline the others drift from. Keep it full of the details that never survive retelling: logistics, timing, the dull middle, wounds, doubt, anonymous helpers. The more of this "unrepeatable" texture it has, the more clearly readers will see the drift in every other account.

**Comrade Account (ally, good faith, partial).**
- **Informed and partial.** Expect F3 assimilation toward the comrade's own frame and role, and F8 migration of credit toward the leader ("the Captain saw it first," when the comrade did).
- **Accuracy:** mostly right on logistics they personally handled, leveled on everything else.
- **Rumor-like:** comrades in a tight crew self-correct (F4), so their shared version is close to true but has been smoothed by group retelling into a "crew legend" with agreed beats.

**Adversary Account (enemy, self-interested).**
- **Lying and motivated:** the deliberately-lying profile above. Built to replace rather than deny (F11). Uses institutional precision to look like a record. Assimilates the protagonist to the adversary's own stereotype of a criminal or monster (F3).
- **Mistaken officers:** some adversary tellers believe their own version through continued influence (F11). Let some of them be *mistaken*, not lying.
- **What leaks through:** fear and grudging respect survive even in hostile accounts (F2: the feeling travels faithfully).

**Dossier (institutional record).**
- **What it keeps:** written, so it does not level as fast as speech, and it keeps precise numbers, names, and dates.
- **How it errs:** through the selection of inputs (what got reported upward) and through assimilation to the institution's categories.
- **Sources it absorbs:** dossiers often take in rumor as intelligence. A report built on informant hearsay carries Hearsay's distortions written up as fact. This matches the SBD-error pattern already locked in canon.
- **Corrections:** in a dossier, they are usually appended, not substituted. So the original keeps its influence on later analysts (F11).

**Hearsay (civilians at a remove): the main user of this file.** A step-by-step model of how a true event degrades. Worked example: *the true event is a night liberation of a Maw pen, 312 freed, one guard killed when he came at the Captain with a crossbow, two crew wounded, the gate opened by a crew member, done before the dawn bell.*

**Retelling 1: a near-witness** (a freed prisoner, or someone who saw the aftermath).
- *What survives:* most structural facts. Leveling has only begun.
- *What's already wrong:*
  - Wrong where the teller had no line of sight.
  - Assimilated to the teller's own experience (F3).
  - Emotion strong and accurate.
  - Numbers approximately right but rounding ("three hundred, more or less").
- *Agency starts migrating:* the crew member who opened the gate becomes "one of his people," and the act may start drifting to the leader (F8).
- *Voice:* "It was dark, we heard the bar go, someone said *walk, don't run.* There was a guard who came at him and didn't get up. Three hundred of us."

**Retelling 2: the first remove** (the near-witness's neighbor, a dockhand, a barmaid).
- *Leveling is heavy (F1):* logistics gone, sequence compressed to two or three beats.
- *Sharpening (F2):* the one death becomes the center. "He killed a guard" is now the headline.
- *Social and negative content takes over (F6):* the guard gains a motive or a cruelty that justifies the kill, or the kill gains gore.
- *Numbers:* round to "hundreds" (F7).
- *Agency complete (F8):* the gate-opener is now the Captain.
- *Sourcing:* friend-of-a-friend claims appear (F9).
- *Voice:* "My cousin's lodger was in that pen. The Scourge himself broke the gate with his hands and gutted the warden's man where he stood."

**Retelling 3: the second remove and beyond** (strangers, travelers, a tavern in another district or city).
- *The story stabilizes into a short, portable form* that varies by community (F1, F3).
- *Migration (F8):* the deed may attach to a more famous alias or figure, or merge with an older story of the same shape.
- *Numbers reach mythic round values* ("a thousand," "the whole Maw").
- *Places move* to the nearest landmark everyone knows.
- *Dates attach to public anchors* ("the night before the tax bell").
- *Elaboration may add invented dialogue* that "explains" the legend's central image (F4), usually a quotable line (F12 helps it stick).
- *Confidence is now highest and least related to accuracy* (F9, F10).
- *Voice:* "That was the night the Scourge emptied the whole Maw and left the warden's head on the gate. Everyone knows he said *'your ledger's short.'*"

**Retelling 3 and beyond, with an official channel involved** (a crier or a broadsheet).
- *Repetition (F10)* pushes one version toward consensus regardless of its source.
- *Corrections:* a later correction lowers belief but leaves the core image in place (F11). Corrections that supply a vivid alternative do better than bare denials.
- *If a ballad or chant forms (F12),* one line freezes and survives generations while the rest keeps drifting. Often it is the one accurate detail, because it was set in rhyme early.

**Practical rules for writing Hearsay.**
1. Decide each teller's remove from the event and their motive: fact-finding, bonding, or status (F5).
2. Delete logistics in proportion to the remove. Add one social beat and one threat or disgust beat per remove (F6).
3. Round every number toward the cultural prior, and move every name and place toward the more famous one (F7, F8).
4. Keep the emotional judgment accurate even when the facts are wrong (F2).
5. Let exactly one detail be startlingly correct. Explain it through a rhyme, a ledger, or a tight network (F4, F12).
6. Make different communities' versions disagree with each other, not just with the truth (F1, F3).
7. When a correction enters, show it reducing belief without removing the image. Corrections that work replace the story; denials don't (F11).
