# 08 -- Adversary Accounts: How Enemies, Perpetrators, and the Defeated Tell Their Side

Research synthesis for the Adversary Account type. Real-world evidence only; no canon facts are asserted here. The "On the page" notes turn each finding into craft markers. They apply to a pre-industrial setting (commanders, enforcers, officials, slavers, assassins) and to a 1960s-90s American city (police, officials, crime bosses).

---

## 1. Scope and search method

**Question.** When someone has done harm, lost, or been opposed, how do they narrate it? What do they say, what do they leave out, and how does sincere self-deception differ from a conscious lie?

**Domains covered.** The sociology of accounts (excuses and justifications). Neutralization theory. Victim-perpetrator narrative asymmetry. Moral disengagement. Self-serving attribution. Cognitive dissonance and self-justification. Face-work and politeness. Agentive and passive language. Non-apology discourse. Perpetrator testimony from atrocity research and truth-commission-era confessions. Dehumanization. Group narratives of defeat. Partisan perception of contested events. Self-deception and cues to lying.

**Method.** Web searches confirmed each source's authors, year, title, venue, and a DOI or publisher page where available. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews came first: Mezulis et al. 2004, Gini et al. 2014, DePaulo et al. 2003, and Maruna & Copes 2005 (a five-decade review). Integrative reviews and influential primary studies came next, then classic theory. The search budget ran out before a few classic works could be re-checked. Those carry long-stable records and are marked `[details not re-verified this pass]`. One source that could not be confirmed is marked `[unverified]` and is not load-bearing for any finding.

**Grading.**
- **STRONG:** meta-analytic or convergent multi-method support.
- **MODERATE:** consistent support from several independent studies or traditions, with method limits such as recall-based narratives, qualitative samples, or single paradigms.
- **CONTESTED/WEAK:** thin, inferential, or disputed.

Every finding cites at least two independent sources or one meta-analysis/review.

**A caution on fit.** Most of this evidence comes from modern Western samples (students, offenders, officials, soldiers). The mechanisms carry over to other settings, but the surface vocabulary does not. A pre-industrial enforcer will not say "I was just doing my job." He will say "I kept the levy as it was set." The structure stays and the idiom changes.

## 2. Findings

### F1. Accounts come in two families: excuses and justifications

**Finding.** When conduct is questioned, people offer *accounts*: statements that bridge what they did and what was expected of them (Scott & Lyman 1968).
- **Excuses** admit the act was wrong but deny full responsibility: accident, defeasibility (I didn't know, I had no choice), biological drives, scapegoating.
- **Justifications** accept responsibility but deny the act was wrong: denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemning the condemners, appeal to higher loyalties, sad tales, self-fulfillment.

Sykes & Matza (1957) independently named five "techniques of neutralization": denial of responsibility, denial of injury, denial of the victim, condemnation of the condemners, and appeal to higher loyalties. Maruna & Copes (2005) reviewed fifty years of this work. They found the techniques reliably present in how offenders talk. The original causal claim, that people neutralize *before* the act and that this frees them to offend, is poorly supported. Neutralization is better read as a way of living with an act afterward, and it may help sustain persistence or desistance.

**Grade.** STRONG as a description of how wrongdoers talk. CONTESTED as a cause of the original act.

**Sources.** Scott & Lyman 1968; Sykes & Matza 1957; Maruna & Copes 2005 (review).

**On the page.**
- Decide first whether the teller is excusing ("it happened," "I had orders," "the drink," "the levy came due") or justifying ("it needed doing," "they were no innocents," "who are *you* to judge me").
- The unrepentant justify. The cornered excuse. The richest adversaries switch between the two inside a single paragraph, often without noticing.
- Condemning the condemners is the most voice-rich technique. The slaver points at the magistrate who buys his cargo. The 1970s precinct captain points at the reformer's own donors.
- Since neutralization is post-hoc, the account can sound more coherent than the act was. The smoothness itself can be a tell that the story has been rehearsed.

### F2. The magnitude gap: perpetrators shrink the act, victims enlarge it

**Finding.** Baumeister, Stillwell & Wotman (1990) had people write about angering someone and about being angered. Perpetrators described their act as meaningful, comprehensible, and provoked: a closed incident with no lasting consequences. Victims described the same kind of act as arbitrary, gratuitous, and part of a long-term grievance with continuing harm. Baumeister (1997) generalized this as the "magnitude gap." What the perpetrator loses is almost always smaller than what the victim loses, so the perpetrator honestly experiences the event as smaller and shorter.

The asymmetry also runs between groups. Noor et al. (2012) review "competitive victimhood": each side in a violent conflict argues that its own suffering is the greater, and this blocks forgiveness.

**Grade.** STRONG for the direction of the asymmetry. MODERATE for its size and for the claim that it holds outside autobiographical recall.

**Sources.** Baumeister et al. 1990; Baumeister 1997; Noor et al. 2012 (review).

**On the page.**
- The adversary dates the event narrowly ("that night," "that one season") while the victim's side runs for years.
- He uses closure language: "that was settled," "it ended there," "I paid what I owed."
- He supplies the provocation the victim never mentions: "she had been warned twice."
- He leaves out what came after for the victim: the limp, the lost trade, the child raised by an aunt.
- **Most useful single marker: what the teller never mentions.** Usually it is the victim's later life.
- At group level, the defeated faction counts its own dead precisely and the enemy's dead in round figures, or not at all.

### F3. Moral disengagement: a fixed set of mechanisms that predicts harm

**Finding.** Bandura (1999) describes how people switch off moral self-sanction without changing their moral standards. The mechanisms work on four points:
- **The conduct:** moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison.
- **The agency:** displacement and diffusion of responsibility.
- **The outcome:** minimizing, ignoring, or misconstruing consequences.
- **The victim:** dehumanization and attribution of blame.

Gini, Pozzoli & Hymel (2014) meta-analyzed 27 samples (N = 17,776, ages 8-18) and found moral disengagement reliably correlated with aggression (r = .28). Newman et al. (2020) systematically reviewed the workplace literature and found the construct predicting unethical conduct in organizations as well.

**Grade.** STRONG.

**Sources.** Bandura 1999; Gini et al. 2014 (meta-analysis); Newman et al. 2020 (systematic review).

**On the page.** Each mechanism has a recognizable voice.
- **Euphemism.** The killing is a "correction," a "clearance," a "settlement of the account." Police talk of "a use of force," "the subject." Officials "rationalize the district." Slavers "move stock."
- **Advantageous comparison.** "Compared to what the old lord did..." or "Other precincts were worse."
- **Displacement.** "The Council set the quota." "Downtown wanted numbers."
- **Diffusion.** "Forty of us held that line, and no single hand did it." Collective verbs: "we held," "it was decided."
- **Minimizing.** "A few were hurt." "Some property damage."
- **Blaming the victim.** "They knew the price of the tether when they took it."

Bandura frames these as *selective*. The same man is gentle at home. That contrast is a rich characterization tool, and it fits the evidence.

### F4. Self-serving attribution: success is mine, failure was the circumstances

**Finding.** Mezulis et al. (2004) meta-analyzed 266 studies and found a large average self-serving attributional bias (d = 0.96). People credit success to themselves and blame failure on outside causes. The bias varies across age, culture, and psychopathology, and is weaker in some East Asian samples. Lau & Russell (1980) content-analyzed sports-page quotes and found coaches and players credited wins to internal factors (effort, skill) and losses to external ones (injuries, officials, weather). Their own explanation: the bias is partly motivated and partly the honest product of different expectations.

**Grade.** STRONG.

**Sources.** Mezulis et al. 2004 (meta-analysis); Lau & Russell 1980.

**On the page.**
- A defeated commander's account names weather, the ground, late supplies, a subordinate's failure, a traitor, and the enemy's unfair method. It names his own decisions last, if at all.
- A victory, told by the same man, is all foresight.
- Read the verbs. Victory takes first-person active ("I placed the archers"). Defeat takes impersonal constructions ("the flank gave way," "the river rose").
- Culture modulates the bias. A teller from an honor-and-face culture may show it *less* in public and more in private. That is a useful contrast between the court testimony and the tavern.

### F5. Self-justification escalates after commitment

**Finding.** Festinger (1957) proposed that inconsistency between action and self-image produces discomfort, which people reduce by changing beliefs rather than admitting error. Tavris & Aronson (2007) synthesize decades of this work into the "pyramid of choice." Two people start nearly alike. After one small self-justified step, each justifies the next until they stand far apart, each sincerely convinced. Shalvi et al. (2015) review newer evidence that self-serving justifications let people break rules while still feeling moral, both before and after the act.

**Grade.** MODERATE. The core phenomenon is robust and long-studied, but several classic laboratory paradigms have contested replication and boundary conditions. The pyramid model is a well-supported synthesis, not a meta-analytic result.

**Sources.** Festinger 1957; Tavris & Aronson 2007; Shalvi et al. 2015 (review).

**On the page.**
- Escalation is the self-deceived adversary's backbone. He can tell you the first small step precisely ("I only took the ledger to protect the house"), and each later step as following from the one before.
- The more costly his commitment (a son lost to the cause, a rank bought with blood), the *more* fiercely he defends it. Pressing him makes him harder, not softer.
- Signature line: "If I was wrong about that, then everything since..." The sentence goes unfinished. He cannot follow it to its end.

### F6. Grammar of agency: "mistakes were made"

**Finding.** Grammatical agency shapes how blame is assigned. Fausey & Boroditsky (2010) found that agentive descriptions ("she ripped the costume") produced more blame and larger financial penalties than non-agentive ones ("the costume ripped"), for identical events. Kampf (2009) analyzed 354 public apologies in Israeli discourse. Public figures minimized responsibility in four ways: weakening the performative verb ("sorry," "regret" instead of "apologize"), blurring the offense, questioning who was offended, and questioning who the offender was. Tavris & Aronson's title phrase, "mistakes were made (but not by me)," names the agentless passive as a classic self-justifying move.

**Grade.** MODERATE. The experimental effect is replicated in related paradigms, and the discourse findings are convergent but descriptive.

**Sources.** Fausey & Boroditsky 2010; Kampf 2009; Tavris & Aronson 2007.

**On the page.** This is the most portable sentence-level tool for an adversary.
- **Agentless passive.** "The gate was opened." "Shots were fired." "The settlement was cleared."
- **Ergative intransitive.** "The fire spread." "The line broke." "The prisoner fell."
- **Nominalization.** "There was an escalation." "The removal took place at dawn."
- **Weakened apology.** "I regret that it came to that." "It's unfortunate that people were hurt." "If anyone was offended."
- **Craft rule.** Track the switch. The teller is active and agentive for everything up to the harm and for everything after it. *At* the harm itself the agent disappears. A reader who notices the switch has caught him.

### F7. Face-work: accounts are performed to protect a public self

**Finding.** Goffman (1955/1967) describes "face" as the positive social value a person claims in an encounter, and "face-work" as the moves that keep it intact: avoidance, corrective interchanges, deference, and poise. Brown & Levinson (1987) formalize two faces: *positive* (the wish to be approved of) and *negative* (the wish not to be imposed on). Face-threatening acts, such as admission, accusation, and apology, carry predictable mitigating strategies. Group apologies show the same dynamic. Hornsey & Wohl (2013) review evidence that collective apologies are often minimal and that their link to victims' forgiveness is weaker than assumed, partly because victims doubt the sincerity of face-saving speech.

**Grade.** MODERATE. The theory is foundational and widely used. Quantitative tests of the politeness model vary across cultures.

**Sources.** Goffman 1955/1967; Brown & Levinson 1987; Hornsey & Wohl 2013 (review); Kampf 2009.

**On the page.**
- The adversary's account depends on its audience. In public he guards his standing ("I have never broken my word in this house"). In private he guards his self-image ("I did what the others were afraid to do").
- **Poise** is face-work. The beaten officer who keeps his collar straight and speaks in measured sentences is telling the listener "I am still someone."
- **The minimal apology protects face.** A slaver's guild "regrets the losses incurred in transit." A city "acknowledges the concerns of the community."
- A respected enemy may *grant* the protagonist face ("you were the better tactician that day") precisely to keep his own: being beaten by the best costs less face.

### F8. Perpetrator testimony: ordinary men, roles, and the missing middle

**Finding.** Browning (1992) used postwar judicial interrogations of a German reserve police battalion. Most of its men were middle-aged and ordinary, and most took part in mass shootings even after being offered the chance to step aside. Their later testimony blamed conformity, career, and not wanting to leave the "dirty work" to comrades. Few gave ideological reasons. They were often vague about the killings themselves and precise about the surrounding logistics.

Huggins, Haritos-Fatouros & Zimbardo (2002) interviewed 23 Brazilian police: 14 direct perpetrators of torture and murder, plus "atrocity facilitators." Perpetrators narrated their violence through institutional role, professionalism, and specialization, and facilitators insisted on their distance from the violence. Foster, Haupt & de Beer (2005) interviewed protagonists on all sides of South Africa's apartheid-era conflict (about 300 hours). Perpetrators framed their acts through group membership, ideology, and the theater of a war. Payne (2008) studied perpetrators' public confessions in Argentina, Chile, Brazil, and South Africa. She found they rarely settle anything. Confessions come in recognizable types (remorseful, heroic, denial, sadistic, fictional, betrayal, silence), and each type provokes conflict rather than closure.

**Grade.** MODERATE. Qualitative, and the samples are self-selected or legally constrained, but the pattern converges across four countries, three decades, and different interview settings.

**Sources.** Browning 1992; Huggins et al. 2002; Foster et al. 2005; Payne 2008.

**On the page.**
- **The missing middle.** The perpetrator is vivid about the orders, the march, and the meal afterward, and fogged about the act itself: "Then it was done." "I don't remember faces." This gap reads as true. Vivid gloating detail marks a different teller type, Payne's sadistic confession.
- **Role language.** "It was my post." "I was the second man in, not the first." "Someone had to keep the list." For a 1970s cop: "I was the arresting officer, I didn't run the interview room."
- **Professionalism as pride.** The good torturer or assassin talks about technique, cleanliness, and not losing control. Huggins et al. call this a form of moral insulation.
- **Peer loyalty over ideology.** "I wasn't going to let the others carry it."
- **The facilitator's distance.** "I only signed the transfers." "I only drove."
- **Give each confession a Payne type.** It decides how the account lands with in-world listeners.

### F9. The account is built for its listener

**Finding.** Presser (2004) interviewed violent offenders and found that they used the research interview itself to claim a moral self. They solicited her judgment, invoked or rejected it, and placed themselves outside the category "violent offender." The narration *was* moral work happening in real time. Payne (2008) likewise treats confession as a performance shaped by its stage (a court, a television studio, a truth commission) and its audience. This matches Goffman's face-work (F7) and Scott & Lyman's point that the acceptability of an account depends on who receives it.

**Grade.** MODERATE.

**Sources.** Presser 2004; Payne 2008; Scott & Lyman 1968.

**On the page.**
- Every Adversary Account needs a defined listener: a tribunal, a captor, a biographer, a son, a confessor, a reporter, a rival.
- The teller watches the listener and adjusts: "You'd have done the same." "You're not like the others, you understand."
- He pre-empts the listener's judgment: "I know what you think of men like me, but..."
- A "redemption" or "I was a different man then" frame is often aimed at the listener, not at the facts.

### F10. Dehumanization in enemy talk, and its limits

**Finding.** Haslam (2006) integrates the dehumanization literature into two forms. *Animalistic* dehumanization denies uniquely human traits (refinement, civility, rationality) and casts others as beasts. *Mechanistic* dehumanization denies human-nature traits (warmth, emotion, agency) and casts others as objects or automata. Kteily et al. (2015) validated a blatant dehumanization measure. Blatant dehumanization rose after real intergroup violence and predicted support for torture and retaliation over and above subtle prejudice. Bandura (1999) lists dehumanization as a core disengagement mechanism.

Baumeister (1997) cautions that much violence is driven by idealism (higher purpose), threatened egotism, or instrumental gain. Many perpetrators recognize their victims' humanity and harm them partly *because* of it. Recent work arguing that dehumanization licenses instrumental violence more than morally motivated violence points the same way (Rai, Valdesolo & Graham 2017) `[unverified]`.

**Grade.** STRONG that dehumanizing language accompanies and predicts support for violence. CONTESTED that it is necessary for cruelty.

**Sources.** Haslam 2006 (review); Kteily et al. 2015; Bandura 1999; Baumeister 1997.

**On the page.**
- **Animalistic:** "cattle," "rats," "the herd," "they breed."
- **Mechanistic:** "units," "stock," "numbers," "the cargo," "the subject," "bodies."
- The Haslam split lets you set an institution's register. A clinical bureaucracy dehumanizes mechanistically. A raider band dehumanizes animalistically.
- For moral complexity, use Baumeister's caution. The idealist adversary does *not* dehumanize. He grants that his victims are people and still says they had to be broken "for the sake of what comes after." That is often more chilling.
- Dehumanizing language should *rise* in the account after the teller's own side has taken losses (Kteily et al.).

### F11. How the defeated narrate defeat: myths, close calls, and official stories

**Finding.** Schivelbusch (2003) compared three defeated societies: the American South after 1865, France after 1871, and Germany after 1918. He found recurring patterns:
- Defeat recast as moral or cultural superiority.
- Heroic loss myths (the plantation legend, the Lost Cause register).
- Blame displaced onto an internal betrayer: the German "stab in the back."
- A quiet urge to imitate the victor.

Tetlock (1998) found that experts protected their worldviews with close-call counterfactuals. When events contradicted a forecast, they claimed the predicted outcome *almost* happened ("I was almost right"). They rejected close-call reasoning that would have undercut their framework ("I was not almost wrong"). Bilali (2013) found that Turkish students' views on responsibility for the 1915 Armenian killings closely tracked the official national narrative. In-group glorification and perceived threat predicted less acknowledgment.

**Grade.** MODERATE. Schivelbusch is comparative history. Tetlock and Bilali are quantitative but domain-specific. They converge.

**Sources.** Schivelbusch 2003; Tetlock 1998; Bilali 2013.

**On the page.**
- **The betrayal clause.** Every defeated institution's account eventually names who "sold us out": the quartermaster, the courier, the mayor's office, the informant.
- **The close call.** "If the second wing had arrived by noon..." "One more hour and we'd have had him."
- **Moral victory.** "We lost the field and kept our honor." "They needed the whole fleet to beat us."
- **The official story as script.** Junior officers, enforcers, and clerks repeat the institution's own wording ("the incident at the ford") more consistently than any real memory would allow. Identical phrasing across several witnesses marks a received narrative, not a recalled one.

### F12. Winners and losers see and remember the same event differently

**Finding.** Hastorf & Cantril (1954) showed students at Princeton and Dartmouth the same film of a rough football game. Princeton students counted about twice as many Dartmouth infractions as Dartmouth students did. Partisanship shaped what each side *saw*, not just what it said afterward. Lau & Russell (1980) found the win/loss attribution asymmetry in coaches' own explanations (F4). Noor et al. (2012) show each side in a conflict building a victimhood narrative that exceeds the other's (F2).

**Grade.** MODERATE. The partisan-perception effect is classic and has spawned a large follow-up literature. The original is a single study, and memory-specific evidence for battles in particular is indirect.

**Sources.** Hastorf & Cantril 1954; Lau & Russell 1980; Noor et al. 2012.

**On the page.**
- Write winner and loser accounts of the same engagement so that both are *sincere* and both are wrong in opposite directions.
- The loser saw more fouls: the feint as "a trick," the ambush as "cowardice," surrender terms as "a lie."
- The winner saw fewer: the same feint is "discipline."
- Contested details (who struck first, how many, whether a flag of truce was up) should differ along the partisan line, not at random.

### F13. The respected-enemy register

**Finding.** No single study isolates "grudging respect." It is an inference from three findings:
- Attribution research lists the opponent's *ability* as an external, face-saving explanation for failure (Lau & Russell 1980; Mezulis et al. 2004). Being beaten by a superior opponent threatens self-worth less than being beaten by an equal.
- Defeated societies elevate the victor's power and method, even while mythologizing their own nobility (Schivelbusch 2003).
- Granting face to the other party can protect one's own (Goffman 1955/1967; Brown & Levinson 1987).

**Grade.** CONTESTED/WEAK. A well-grounded inference, not a measured effect.

**Sources.** Lau & Russell 1980; Mezulis et al. 2004; Schivelbusch 2003; Goffman 1955/1967.

**On the page.**
- Respect is *selective*. The defeated praises the enemy's skill, nerve, and discipline ("I've never seen a line held like that") and never his cause or his legitimacy ("a thief, but a master thief").
- Praise of the enemy lifts the speaker: "Only he could have done it. No ordinary captain would have beaten me."
- The respectful defeated often states one specific concrete detail he cannot stop thinking about: the timing of a turn, a sentence the enemy spoke. Fixation on one precise image signals real impression rather than performance.
- The respectful register can coexist with full self-justification. "He was better that day, and I would do every part of it again."

### F14. Self-deception versus lying: different speakers, different tells

**Finding.** von Hippel & Trivers (2011) argue that self-deception evolved partly to make deceiving others easier. A teller who believes his own story shows none of the cues of conscious deception, carries no cognitive load from keeping two stories, and can claim honest error if caught. DePaulo et al. (2003) meta-analyzed 158 cues across 1,338 estimates. Liars were, on average, less forthcoming, less compelling, more tense, more negative in impression, and their stories carried *fewer ordinary imperfections* (fewer spontaneous corrections, fewer admissions of not remembering). Most behavioral cues were weak or absent.

**Grade.** MODERATE. DePaulo is a strong meta-analysis, but the effects are small. von Hippel & Trivers is an influential theoretical target article with substantial critical commentary.

**Sources.** DePaulo et al. 2003 (meta-analysis); von Hippel & Trivers 2011.

**On the page.**
- **A conscious liar** tells a clean, tight, chronologically tidy story. He doesn't volunteer corrections ("no, wait, it was the second bell"), avoids "I don't remember," gives fewer unusual details, and says less than the listener expects.
- **A self-deceived teller** is relaxed and fluent, and *does* make ordinary memory corrections, because he is reconstructing, not reciting. His errors are systematic (always in his favor, always shrinking the harm), not random.
- The distinction lives in the *pattern* of error, not in nervous behavior. Avoid body-language tells such as sweating and shifty eyes. The evidence for them is weak, and readers will sense the cliché.

## 3. Implications by teller state

| State | Core mechanism | Characteristic markers | What gives it away |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Informed** (knows what happened, tells it with his own spin) | Justification and face-work (F1, F7) | Accurate facts in moral framing; owns the act and disputes its wrongness; euphemism (F3) | The facts check out and the evaluations don't. He'll say "I burned it" and call it necessary. |
| **Uninformed** (genuinely didn't see or know key parts) | Partisan perception and gaps (F12, F8) | Confident about his own sector, vague outside it; fills gaps with the institutional script (F11) | Phrases repeated word-for-word from orders or official reports; certainty out of proportion to vantage point |
| **Mistaken** (honest error, not motivated) | Partisan perception and close calls (F12, F11) | Errors that run in his side's favor yet are held sincerely; open to correction on neutral details | Corrects small things readily, resists anything touching responsibility |
| **Sincerely self-deceived** | Dissonance escalation, magnitude gap, self-serving attribution (F5, F2, F4) | Fluent, relaxed, ordinary memory corrections; agency vanishes at the moment of harm (F6); closure language; never mentions the victim's later life | Errors are *systematic*: every ambiguity resolves in his favor. Pressing hardens him. |
| **Deliberately lying** | Strategic deception (F14) | Tidy, under-detailed, no self-corrections, says less than expected; may over-use the official script | Contradicts other witnesses on checkable facts; can't supply ordinary peripheral detail when pressed; story doesn't grow when retold |

**The key craft distinction is sincere self-deception versus conscious lying.** In the evidence these are close to different speech acts (von Hippel & Trivers 2011; DePaulo et al. 2003).
- The self-deceived man's account is *richer* and *less defended* than a liar's. He has no second story to hide, so he talks freely, and the bias shows in the direction of every judgment call.
- The liar's account is thinner and more controlled. He protects a known boundary.
- The most realistic adversaries mix the two. Most of the account is self-deceived, with one consciously guarded fact at its core, usually the one thing that would collapse the self-image (a specific order he gave, a body he saw). Write him relaxed and fluent everywhere except around that one fact, where he becomes tidy and brief.

**Mixed states are the norm.** Maruna & Copes (2005) note that neutralizations can be both believed and strategic. A teller can start the account as strategy and come to believe it by the tenth retelling (Tavris & Aronson 2007). In a long-running series, the same adversary's account can drift from lie toward self-deception across eras. That is a usable arc.

## 4. Implications by account type: the Adversary Account template

Every Adversary Account should fix four things before drafting:
1. **The listener** (F9).
2. **The teller state** (Section 3).
3. **The one thing never mentioned** (F2, F8).
4. **Where agency disappears** (F6).

Then choose a register.

### (a) The unrepentant

*Mechanism:* justification, not excuse (F1). Moral disengagement through moral justification and advantageous comparison (F3). Face held through poise (F7).

**Structure.**
1. **Opening claim of standing.** Who he is and what he held. "I kept the eastern road for nineteen years." / "I ran this ward before your mayor could spell it."
2. **The order of things.** His worldview stated as obvious fact: the debt must be paid, the line must hold, the street needs a hand on it.
3. **The act, owned.** He uses an active first-person verb *for the decision*: "I gave the order." Agency still blurs at the moment of harm itself: "and the settlement was cleared."
4. **Condemning the condemners** (F1). The listener's own side did worse, or profited from his work.
5. **Advantageous comparison** (F3). "Ask what came after me."
6. **Close.** No apology. Possibly a minimal regret aimed at a technical failure, never the harm: "I regret the courier was late." Kampf's "regret, not apologize" (F6) fits exactly.

**Never mentions:** the victims' names or later lives. The victims appear only as categories or numbers (F10).

**Risk to manage:** the unrepentant can read as cartoonish. Give him one place where his account is *accurate* and the listener's side really was hypocritical. Condemning the condemners is strongest when it is partly true.

### (b) The self-deceived

*Mechanism:* dissonance escalation (F5), magnitude gap (F2), self-serving attribution (F4), sincere fluency (F14).

**Structure.**
1. **The first small step**, told precisely and sympathetically. "I only took the ledger so it wouldn't burn."
2. **Each next step as following from the last.** Connective words carry the self-justification: "so," "naturally," "after that there was no choice."
3. **Circumstance takes over the verbs** as harm approaches: "things moved quickly," "the crowd turned," "it came to a head."
4. **The act, agentless** (F6). "And then it was done." A gap.
5. **Immediate move to aftermath logistics** (F8's missing middle). The cleanup, the report, the meal.
6. **Closure language** (F2). "It ended that night." "We were square after that."
7. **An unfinished sentence** wherever the logic would reach back and condemn the first step (F5).

**Tells to plant for the reader, invisible to the teller:**
- Ordinary memory corrections on trivia ("it was the fourth day, no, the fifth"), and none on the harm.
- Every ambiguous point resolved in his favor.
- A victim mentioned once, early, as a person ("the miller's boy, I knew his father"), who becomes a category after the act ("the ones at the mill").

**Never mentions:** the first moment he could have stopped. He remembers it, and that is why it is missing.

### (c) The grudgingly respectful defeated opponent

*Mechanism:* external attribution to opponent ability (F4, F13). Face preserved by elevating the victor (F7). Partisan perception of method (F12). Defeat narrative with a close call (F11).

**Structure.**
1. **Respect for his own side first.** His men were good, his plan was sound. Moral victory (F11).
2. **The close call** (F11). "If the tide had held one hour..." / "If my lieutenant hadn't..."
3. **The turn: the enemy's quality.** One specific, concrete, fixated image (F13): the timing, the silence, the single sentence the enemy said to him.
4. **Selective praise.** Skill, nerve, discipline: yes. Cause, legitimacy, right: never, or only under protest ("a common thief, but...").
5. **The partisan residue** (F12). Even in admiration he names the enemy's method a trick, an unfairness, or an advantage he lacked.
6. **Self-elevation through the enemy.** "It took him to do it." His loss proves he was formidable.
7. **Close.** Usually justification survives. "I'd fight him again tomorrow." Or, rarely, one crack in the account (a single question he still asks himself), placed and left unexplained.

**Never mentions:** his own specific decisive error, if there was one. Or he mentions it only as "the error" in the passive, never "my error."

### Shared drafting checklist (all three registers)

- [ ] Listener named and present in the teller's address (F9).
- [ ] Excuse versus justification chosen deliberately, and the switches between them marked (F1).
- [ ] At least two Bandura mechanisms voiced in the setting's own idiom, not modern jargon (F3).
- [ ] Agency switches from active to agentless at the moment of harm (F6).
- [ ] The magnitude gap shown through closure language and the missing victim afterward (F2).
- [ ] One concrete thing the teller never mentions, identified by the writer in advance.
- [ ] If the teller is lying: tidy, thin, no self-corrections around the guarded fact (F14).
- [ ] If the teller is self-deceived: fluent, with small corrections and systematic bias (F14).
- [ ] Dehumanization register set to the institution's type: mechanistic for a bureaucracy, animalistic for a raider band, *absent* for an idealist (F10).
- [ ] The defeated party's account contains a betrayal clause or a close call (F11).
- [ ] Where a victor's account of the same event exists, the contested details diverge along the partisan line (F12).
- [ ] No body-language deception clichés (F14).
