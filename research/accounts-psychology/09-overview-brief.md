# Research Brief: How Tellers Tell. Oral Accounts, Rumor, Gossip, Deception, Memory, Settings, and Institutional Records

Prepared for writing in-world accounts: Protagonist series, Comrade, Adversary, Dossier, and Hearsay. Each section gives (i) key findings, (ii) markers a writer can put on the page, and (iii) sources. I verified specific figures by web search where flagged (✔). Items marked ⚠ are ones I'm unsure of, or ones where the research is contested.

---

## 1. Structure of conversational oral narrative

### (i) Key findings

**Labov & Waletzky's six-part model (1967; refined in Labov 1972).** A fully formed personal narrative has six parts:
- **Abstract.** What the story is about ("Did I ever tell you about the time the river came up?").
- **Orientation.** Who, when, where, and the situation. It is often in the past progressive ("We were sitting out on the pier…").
- **Complicating action.** A sequence of narrative clauses in the simple past. Their order matches the order of events, so swapping two clauses changes what happened.
- **Evaluation.** Why the story is worth telling. It is spread through the whole telling, but it clusters just before the climax and suspends the action.
- **Resolution.** What finally happened.
- **Coda.** It returns the talk to the present ("And that's why I don't go down there anymore"; "And he's still walking around, far as I know").

Only the complicating action is required. Many real stories are a minimal action sequence plus evaluation.

**Evaluation devices (Labov 1972).** These are how a teller says "this matters" without saying it:
- **External evaluation.** The teller steps out of the story: "And it was the strangest thing."
- **Embedded evaluation.** The teller quotes their own past thoughts or speech: "I said to myself, 'This is it.'" Or quotes a witness: "Even Marcus said he'd never seen anything like it."
- **Evaluative action.** "I just started shaking."
- **Intensifiers.** Gesture, expressive sounds ("BOOM"), repetition ("he hit him and hit him and hit him"), and quantifiers ("all of them," "every last one").
- **Comparators.** What did *not* happen or *could* have happened: negatives, futures, modals ("He could've killed us all"; "Nobody moved").
- **Correlatives.** Several events held at once ("while he's reloading, I'm already…").
- **Explicatives.** Clauses of reason ("because he owed my uncle").

**Reportability and credibility (Labov 1997, 2013).** Every story is built around its "most reportable event," the one that justifies the speaker holding the floor. Labov points out a paradox: the more reportable an event, the less believable it is. Tellers of extraordinary events therefore do *credibility work*, such as naming witnesses, giving objective-sounding detail, and underplaying their own role. Labov also argues that narrators construct the story backward from the most reportable event, choosing the earliest point that explains it. That choice is itself an interpretive (and potentially self-serving) decision about where "the beginning" is.

**Constructed dialogue (Tannen 1989).** Quoted speech in a story is almost never verbatim. It is constructed to dramatize and to place blame or credit. "So he goes, 'You don't scare me'" is a performance, not a recording.

**Co-narration and "Living Narrative" (Ochs & Capps 2001).** Ochs and Capps describe everyday narrative along five dimensions. Each runs between two poles:
- **Tellership:** one active teller vs. many co-tellers.
- **Tellability:** high vs. low.
- **Embeddedness:** detached from the surrounding talk vs. woven into it.
- **Linearity:** closed temporal/causal order vs. open, contested sequence.
- **Moral stance:** certain and constant vs. uncertain and fluid.

Polished literary narrative sits at the "one teller, high tellability, detached, linear, certain" end. Real conversation is mostly at the other end: it is co-told, unfinished, and people argue about what happened and what it meant. "Living narrative" is a way of figuring out an experience together, not delivering a finished verdict.

**Collaborative and retold stories (Norrick 1997, 2000; Lerner 1992; Mandelbaum 1987).**
- When two people both know a story (couples, old friends, veterans), they co-tell it. They correct each other's details ("It was Tuesday." "No, it was the day after the funeral."), finish each other's lines, and argue over the point.
- Familiar stories get retold for their *rapport* value, not their news value. The point becomes "we were there," and listeners who already know it join in.
- Norrick (2005) also describes "the dark side of tellability": stories that are too personal, too painful, or too transgressive get cut off, or told only in fragments.

**Second stories and story rounds (Sacks 1992; Ryave 1978; Jefferson 1978).**
- A recipient often answers a story with a "second story" of their own on the same theme. This shows they understood the first, and it often matches or tops it.
- Story rounds escalate: each teller ups the stakes (the bigger fish, the worse boss, the closer call).
- Jefferson (1978) shows that stories are "locally occasioned." They are triggered by something said ("Speaking of floods…") and must be brought back to the conversation at the end.

**Listener participation (Goodwin 1984; Schegloff, Jefferson & Sacks 1977).**
- Listeners are not passive. They produce continuers ("mm-hm," "yeah?"), assessments ("No way"), and, at the climax, the expected display ("Damn").
- Silence at the climax is a sanction.
- Repair is preferred when the speaker does it themselves. Other-initiated repair comes as "What?", "Who?", "Wait, which one?" and slows the teller down.

**Demanding sources (Pomerantz 1984; Heritage & Raymond 2005; Chafe 1986).**
- Speakers routinely state "how I know," and listeners challenge it ("Says who?", "You saw this?").
- Epistemic rights matter. People who were present, or who own the experience, have the right to tell it and to assess it. Others must defer or show where they got it.
- Many languages mark evidential source in the grammar: witnessed vs. reported vs. inferred (Aikhenvald 2004). English does it with phrases like "apparently," "they say," "I saw," "must have."

### (ii) Page markers
- **Abstract openers:** "You won't believe what…" / "Did I ever tell you about…" / "Remember the thing with the bridge?"
- **Orientation:** the past progressive and stage-setting. Tellers often over-specify the irrelevant (weather, what they were eating) as a realism signal.
- **Turn to the present tense at the climax** (the "conversational historical present," Wolfson 1979): "So I'm standing there and he walks in…"
- **Suspension before the climax.** Embedded evaluation and comparators slow the action right before the key event: "And I thought, this is it. Nobody's coming. I'm not getting out of this one."
- **Codas that bridge to now:** "…and that's how I got this scar" / "and nobody's seen him since" / "so don't tell me about luck."
- **Co-teller interruptions:** "Tell them about the boots." "No, no, let me tell it, you always get it wrong." "It wasn't four of them, it was three." The first teller then concedes, refuses, or absorbs the correction ("Three, four, the point is…"). How they react is characterization.
- **Second-story pivots:** "That's nothing. My cousin…" / "Same thing happened to me, except…" Topping is visible as escalation in numbers, danger, or the teller's own role.
- **Challenge sequences:** "Who told you that?" → the teller names a source ("Benny, and Benny was there"), backs down ("I'm just saying what I heard"), or doubles down ("Ask anybody").
- **Evidential phrasing** marks the type of account. Comrade: "I saw." Hearsay: "They say," "the way I heard it." Dossier: "reportedly," "it is assessed." Adversary: "it is obvious that…"

## 2. Rumor and legend transmission

### (i) Key findings

**Allport & Postman (1947).**
- **Basic law:** rumor intensity ≈ importance × ambiguity. If either one is zero, there is no rumor.
- **Serial-reproduction lab chains** (a picture described from person to person) showed three effects:
  - **Leveling.** The account gets shorter. Most details are lost within the first five or six retellings, and then it stabilizes into a short core.
  - **Sharpening.** A few details are kept and exaggerated: size, numbers, motion, labels, distinctive phrases.
  - **Assimilation.** Details shift toward the teller's expectations, prejudices, and interests. In their famous subway-scene trials, a razor held by a white man often "moved" into the hand of a Black man, a stereotype doing the editing.
- ⚠ **Caveat:** later field work (Buckner 1965; Shibutani 1966) found that real rumors among interested, anxious groups can *grow and elaborate* rather than shrink. Leveling is strongest when the chain is passive and uninvolved.

**Bartlett (1932), *Remembering*.** In "The War of the Ghosts" (a Native American tale retold by English subjects), retellings:
- dropped what made no sense to the teller (the supernatural logic),
- *rationalized* what remained (added causes to explain things),
- swapped in familiar items for unfamiliar ones ("canoe" → "boat," seal-hunting → fishing),
- and settled into a stable form. Memory is "effort after meaning" organized by schemas.

**Rosnow; DiFonzo & Bordia.**
- Rosnow (1991) models rumor generation as a function of personal anxiety, general uncertainty, credulity, and "outcome-relevant involvement."
- DiFonzo & Bordia (2007) define rumor as "unverified and instrumentally relevant information statements in circulation that arise in contexts of ambiguity, danger, or potential threat," serving *sense-making* and *threat management*.
- Credibility rises with repetition, a trusted source, fit with existing attitudes, and the absence of a rebuttal.
- Knapp (1944), from a WWII rumor collection, sorted rumors into three kinds:
  - **"pipe-dream"/wish** rumors (the war will end by Christmas),
  - **"bogie"/dread** rumors (they're poisoning the water),
  - **"wedge-driving"** rumors (that group is hoarding, shirking, profiting).

**Shibutani (1966), *Improvised News*.** Rumor is collective problem-solving when official channels fail or are distrusted. When demand for news exceeds the supply from institutions, people pool their interpretations. Under moderate tension, rumor is *deliberative*: people weigh plausibility and cite sources. Under acute tension, it turns *extemporaneous*: fast, emotional, uncritical. Rumor is a process, not a thing.

**Urban legend (Brunvand 1981; Dale 1978; Dégh 2001; Dégh & Vázsonyi 1983).**
- **The FOAF ("friend of a friend") source** (Dale 1978): close enough to vouch for, too far away to check.
- **Localization:** "it happened right over on Fifth Street."
- **Plausible circumstantial detail**, insistence that it's true, and variants that coexist.
- **"Ostension":** people act out the legend in real life, and the acting-out feeds the legend.

**Racialized and community rumor (Turner 1993; Fine & Turner 2001).** Rumor in African American communities often encoded real historical grievance (e.g., the Tuskegee study made conspiracy rumors about medicine plausible). Truth-value and *social* truth separate: a false rumor can say something true about how a group has been treated.

**Numbers, names, and dates drift.**
- **Dates move to where they make sense.** Portelli (1991) found that workers in Terni remembered steelworker Luigi Trastulli's 1949 death (at an anti-NATO rally) as happening during the 1953 layoff strikes, a more "fitting" context ✔. Memory relocates events to their meaningful frame.
- **Oral tradition compresses the middle.** Vansina (1985) describes the "floating gap" between recent remembered history (roughly three generations) and origin tradition. Genealogies telescope and the middle collapses.
- **Goody & Watt (1963):** oral tradition is "homeostatic." It quietly rewrites the past to fit present needs ("structural amnesia").
- **Numbers inflate, round off, and converge** on formulaic values (3, 7, 12, 40, 100, 1,000). Two pressures do this:
  - **Olrik's (1909) "epic laws."** Folk narrative drifts toward the law of three, two characters per scene, sharp contrast, concentration on one hero, and the "weight of the end."
  - **Mythical numbers that survive by repetition.** Singer (1971) and Best (2001) on "mutant statistics": a number gets quoted, the source drops away, and the figure hardens.

**Legends attach to famous figures.**
- Anecdotes migrate to the most famous plausible bearer. Quote researchers call this "Churchillian drift" (Rees; O'Toole 2017).
- Outlaw legends follow a template (Hobsbawm 1969, *Bandits*): the social bandit rights wrongs, takes only from the unjust, is aided by the people, is invulnerable or uncatchable, and can be defeated only by betrayal.
- The Stagolee legend grew from one real 1895 St. Louis shooting into a badman cycle with drifting details (Brown 2003).
- Fiction can become testimony. The "Angels of Mons" (1914) began as Arthur Machen's short story "The Bowmen" and came back as soldiers' eyewitness accounts (Fussell 1975).

### (ii) Page markers
- **Hearsay sourcing:** "My cousin's girl's brother was there." / "Everybody knows." / "It's true, I swear, it was in Brennan's shop." Each extra link in the chain is a tell.
- **Leveled versions:** shorter, with one vivid sharpened detail kept ("He did it with one hand"), and the context and causes gone.
- **Assimilated versions:** the enemy gets cast in the teller's stereotype of him. The hero acquires the virtues the teller's group most admires.
- **Numerical drift:** "forty men" → "a hundred" → "a whole army." Times compress ("in one night"). Ages shift to suit the role (heroes get younger at their first deed, older as sages). Dates move to the more meaningful occasion.
- **Name drift:** deeds of minor figures get credited to the famous one. Two similar events merge into one. Places shift to the teller's own neighborhood.
- **Olrik shapes appear:** three tries, three companions, the third one succeeds. One antagonist, where there were really several. The story ends on its heaviest beat.
- **Dread vs. wish:** anxious listeners pass on dread rumors fast. The hopeful pass on wish rumors. Wedge-driving rumors blame a rival group.
- **Shibutani's acute register:** short, breathless, present-tense, no sourcing. "They're coming down Fifth right now, they've got the whole block."

## 3. Gossip

### (i) Key findings
- **Dunbar (1996; 2004):** language evolved partly as "vocal grooming." Talk lets a person keep up a larger network than physical grooming allows, and it is a way to track who can be trusted. Dunbar, Marriott & Duncan (1997) found roughly two-thirds of conversation time goes to social topics.
- **Gluckman (1963):** gossip is "a hallmark of membership." Only insiders have the right to gossip about other insiders. An outsider who joins in is rebuked. Gossip maintains group norms and polices status.
- **Paine (1967), counterpoint:** gossip is also individual information management, a self-interested move.
- **Bergmann (1993), *Discreet Indiscretions*:**
  - Gossip is a triad: producer, recipient, and an *absent* subject who is known to both.
  - The producer must show they have legitimate access ("I happened to see…", "she told me herself"). Gossip comes wrapped in disclaimers ("I'm not one to talk, but…").
  - Gossip moves between morally condemning the subject and *enjoying* the condemnation.
- **Eder & Enke (1991):** the first evaluation of the absent person sets the direction. Once someone has said something negative and someone else agrees, challenges become rare. Any challenge has to come early, before agreement locks in.
- **Prosocial gossip:** Feinberg, Willer, Stellar & Keltner (2012) found people spread reputational warnings at a personal cost, and doing so lowers their frustration.
- **Gossip moves behavior:** Sommerfeld et al. (2007, PNAS) found that in indirect-reciprocity games, gossip strongly shaped cooperation, and most gossip passed on was accurate ✔. ⚠ I've seen popular summaries ("facts are no match for gossip") claim gossip overrode direct observation. I couldn't confirm that framing from the abstract, so use it cautiously.
- **Gossip as cultural learning:** Baumeister, Zhang & Vohs (2004) argue gossip teaches norms through stories of other people's violations.

### (ii) Page markers
- **Access claims:** "I'm only telling you because you know her." / "This came from his own mother." / "I was right there in the next room."
- **Disclaimers:** "God knows I don't like to say it…" / "I'm not judging, but…" / "Don't repeat this." The last one guarantees it will be repeated.
- **Who may speak:** a newcomer who offers gossip gets frozen out ("And how would *you* know?"). A long-tenured member's word sets the group's evaluation.
- **Early-evaluation lock-in:** the first person to frame the subject ("He's a snake") sets the direction. A dissenter must jump in at once ("Now hold on, that's not fair") or stay silent.
- **Gossip as currency:** whoever has fresh information gets status for a moment. Withholding can be a power move ("I could tell you, but…").
- **Warnings framed as protection:** "I'm telling you so you don't get burned."

## 4. Deception in conversation

### (i) Key findings

**Cues are weak, and people are poor detectors.**
- **DePaulo et al. (2003)** meta-analyzed 158 cues. Liars were somewhat:
  - *less forthcoming:* shorter, fewer details,
  - *less compelling:* less plausible, less logically structured, more discrepant or ambivalent, less verbally and vocally involved,
  - *more tense:* higher pitch, pupil dilation,
  - and showed *fewer "ordinary imperfections"*: fewer spontaneous corrections, fewer admitted memory gaps.
- Popular cues such as gaze aversion and fidgeting were essentially non-diagnostic. Most effects were small.
- **Bond & DePaulo (2006):** average lie-detection accuracy is about 54%, barely above chance.

**How lies are actually caught.**
- **Park, Levine et al. (2002):** most lies are discovered *after the fact*, through third-party information, physical evidence, or later confession, not through in-the-moment cues.
- **Levine's Truth-Default Theory (2014):** people presume honesty unless a "trigger" (motive, inconsistency, contradiction with known facts) knocks them out of it.
- **Serota, Levine & Boster (2010):** most people tell few lies, and a small number of prolific liars tell most of them.

**Cognitive load (Vrij 2008; Vrij et al. 2008, 2017).**
- Lying is mentally harder: the liar must build the story, monitor the listener, suppress the truth, and stay consistent. Adding load exposes liars more than truth-tellers.
- Load-raising techniques: telling the story in reverse order, unexpected questions (e.g., ask for a sketch, ask about spatial layout or minor actors), and a "model statement" that shows how much detail is expected.
- **Verifiability Approach (Nahari, Vrij & Fisher 2014):** liars avoid *checkable* details (named witnesses, recorded transactions) and lean on uncheckable ones ("I was walking alone, thinking").

**Strategic Use of Evidence (SUE) (Hartwig, Granhag, Strömwall & Kronkvist 2006).** Guilty suspects avoid mentioning, or deny, information they fear the interviewer has. Holding back evidence until late lets statement-evidence inconsistencies show.

**Liars' vs. truth-tellers' strategies (Hartwig, Granhag & Strömwall 2007; Granhag & Strömwall 1999).**
- Liars plan to keep it simple, avoid contradiction, and stick to a rehearsed script. Their repeated statements can be *more* consistent than truthful ones.
- Truth-tellers "tell it like it happened," rely on an "illusion of transparency" (Kassin 2005), and reconstruct from memory each time.
- ⚠ Inconsistency is not a reliable lie cue. Truthful repeated accounts naturally vary, especially at the edges (Fisher, Brewer & Mitchell 2009).

**Interpersonal Deception Theory (Buller & Burgoon 1996).**
- Deception is interactive. The liar watches the receiver for suspicion and adapts as the conversation goes on.
- Strategic behaviors include: uncertainty and vagueness, non-immediacy and reticence, disassociation (distancing the self from the act), and image protection.
- Non-strategic "leakage" (arousal, awkwardness) decreases as the liar adjusts.
- ⚠ DePaulo et al. criticized IDT in the same journal issue as theoretically loose.

**Content-based assessment.**
- **Criteria-Based Content Analysis (Steller & Köhnken 1989)** lists 19 criteria, grouped:
  - *General:* logical structure; *unstructured production* (truthful accounts jump around in time); quantity of detail.
  - *Specific content:* contextual embedding; descriptions of interactions; reproduced conversation; unexpected complications; unusual details; superfluous details; details misunderstood but accurately reported; related external associations; one's own mental state; the other person's mental state.
  - *Motivation-related:* spontaneous corrections; admitting lack of memory; raising doubts about one's own testimony; self-deprecation; pardoning the perpetrator.
- ⚠ CBCA was developed for child witnesses. Its evidence is mixed and its error rates are substantial (Vrij 2005 review).
- **Reality Monitoring (Johnson & Raye 1981; applied by Sporer 1997):** memories of experienced events carry more perceptual (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste), spatial, temporal, and affective information. Imagined or invented events carry more "cognitive operations" ("I must have…", "I figured…", "logically he would've…"). ⚠ Rehearsed lies can include sensory detail, and genuine old memories fade toward the "imagined" profile.

**Uninformed vs. skeptical listeners.**
- To an uninformed, trusting listener, a liar can be expansive, confident, even embellished. Truth-default does the work.
- To a skeptic or someone who may hold evidence, the liar narrows: shorter answers, more hedging, no checkable specifics, offers of "proof" that is unverifiable, counter-accusation, appeals to their own character ("Have I ever lied to you?"). They also probe what the listener knows ("Who said that?").

**Embellishment on retelling.**
- **Saying-is-believing (Higgins & Rholes 1978; Echterhoff, Higgins & Groll 2005):** tailoring a message to the audience changes the speaker's own later memory.
- **Fabrication inflation (Polage 2004, 2012):** people who repeatedly tell fabricated events later rate them as more likely to have happened.
- **Liars become less sure their lie is false (Pickel 2004).**
- Embellishments that win a reaction get kept (audience reinforcement; see Marsh & Tversky in Section 5).
- **Frankfurt's (2005) "bullshit":** indifference to whether something is true at all. It is distinct from lying, and it describes much performative talk.

### (ii) Page markers
- **Liar to a skeptic:** short, chronological, over-tidy. No spontaneous corrections. No "I don't remember." No self-deprecation. Uncheckable alibi details ("I was alone"). Answers a question with a question ("Why would I do that?"). Sudden precision about irrelevant things, vagueness about the key moment. Distancing language ("that thing that happened," dropping first-person agency: "the door got opened"). Probing ("Who've you been talking to?").
- **Liar to the uninformed:** fluent, confident, cinematic, generous with color, self-aggrandizing. Repeats set phrases verbatim across tellings (rehearsal). Tellingly, the story never contains its own uncertainty.
- **Truth-teller:** out of order ("wait, before that…"), self-corrects ("—no, it was the green one"), admits gaps ("I don't know how long, could've been a minute"), includes irrelevant and odd details (a smell, a song, someone's shoe), shows unflattering facts about themselves, attributes thoughts to others ("he looked like he'd already decided"), reconstructs dialogue roughly ("something like 'not today'"). Repeated tellings differ at the edges but keep the same core.
- **Exposure is usually later.** In a scene, the lie survives the conversation and breaks when a third party arrives, a physical object turns up, or two accounts are compared.
- **Embellished retelling:** the second telling is longer, the teller's role is bigger, the numbers are rounder, and quoted lines are crisper and wittier than any real person spoke them.

## 5. Memory and eyewitness distortion

### (i) Key findings

**Misinformation.**
- **Loftus & Palmer (1974):** the verb in the question changed speed estimates. "Smashed" produced about 41 mph, "contacted" about 32 mph. A week later, "smashed" subjects were more likely to falsely recall broken glass.
- **Loftus, Miller & Burns (1978):** misleading post-event information replaced memory of a stop sign with a yield sign.
- **Loftus & Pickrell (1995):** whole false childhood events ("lost in the mall") were implanted in some subjects.
- **Loftus (2005)** reviews thirty years of this work.

**Weapon focus.**
- **Loftus, Loftus & Messo (1987):** a visible weapon draws attention and degrades memory of the face.
- **Steblay (1992) meta-analysis:** a modest effect on identification and a larger effect on describing features.
- **Pickel (1998):** much of the effect is the *unusualness* of the object.

**Stress.**
- **Deffenbacher et al. (2004) meta-analysis:** high stress impairs face identification and event recall.
- **Morgan et al. (2004):** 509 military personnel in survival-school mock-POW interrogation. Correct identification of the interrogator was about 68% under low stress and about 29% under high stress, 24 hours later ✔.
- **"Tunnel memory" (Christianson 1992; Safer et al. 1998):** central details are kept, the periphery is lost, and the remembered frame narrows.
- **Duration overestimation (Loftus, Schooler, Boone & Kline 1987):** witnesses greatly overestimate how long a violent event lasted.

**Confidence.**
- Confidence inflates with feedback ("Good, you picked the suspect"): Wells & Bradfield 1998.
- Initial confidence under fair conditions is informative. Courtroom confidence built up later is not (Wixted & Wells 2017).

**Source-monitoring errors (Johnson, Hashtroudi & Lindsay 1993).** People remember the content but mislocate where it came from: something heard becomes something seen, something imagined becomes something done, and one person's line gets credited to another. This is the core mechanism by which hearsay becomes "eyewitness."

**Confabulation.**
- Clinical confabulation (Moscovitch 1989; Schnider 2003) is a condition seen after brain injury.
- Everyday gap-filling is normal. Nisbett & Wilson (1977), "Telling more than we can know": people readily give plausible but wrong reasons for their own behavior.

**Flashbulb memories drift but feel vivid.** Neisser & Harsch (1992) found Challenger-explosion memories changed substantially over 2.5 years, while confidence stayed high. Talarico & Rubin (2003) found the same: flashbulb memories are no more consistent than ordinary ones, only more confidently held.

**Retelling effects.**
- **Tversky & Marsh (2000):** people retold a story for a purpose (e.g., to argue for or against a character), and their later memory shifted toward that bias.
- **Marsh & Tversky (2004):** in diaries of real everyday retellings, participants themselves labeled 61% as distorted (exaggerations, minimizations, additions, omissions), yet labeled only 42% as "inaccurate" ✔. People don't count purposeful shaping as error.
- **Dudukovic, Marsh & Tversky (2004):** entertaining retellings carried more affect and exaggeration and less accuracy, and they worsened later memory.
- **Pasupathi (2001); Pasupathi, Stallworth & Murdoch (1998):** listener engagement shapes what the teller remembers. Distracted listeners produce poorer stories and poorer later recall.
- **Neisser (1981), "John Dean's memory":** Dean got the gist ("repisodic" truth) right but the specific conversations wrong. He merged repeated episodes and inflated his own role.

### (ii) Page markers
- **Stressed eyewitness:** sharp about the weapon or the central threat, blank about faces and surroundings. "I couldn't tell you what he looked like, but that blade, I can see it now." Durations stretch ("it went on forever," when it was seconds).
- **Misinformation absorbed:** the witness repeats a detail first supplied by an interrogator or a newspaper crier, using that source's exact words.
- **Source errors:** "I saw him do it." Pressed: "Well, I saw him after, and everybody said…"
- **Confident but drifted:** a vivid, certain memory of a detail other witnesses contradict, held more firmly over time.
- **Repisodic merging:** "Every time we went out it was like this: he'd say…", several events collapsed into one stylized scene.
- **Audience-tuned versions:** the same teller gives the drinking crowd the funny version, the widow the solemn one, and the magistrate the minimizing one. Each later shapes what they themselves remember.

## 6. Setting-specific talk

For each setting: who is present, the status order, turn norms, what is reportable, openers and closers, how challenge works, and typical exaggeration.

**Bar / tavern**
- **Present and status.** Regulars, a bartender, occasional strangers. The bartender is the hub and gatekeeper (Spradley & Mann 1975). Regulars outrank newcomers, and seniority means seats and "my stool." Oldenburg (1989) calls "third places" *levelers*: outside status is partly suspended, conversation is the main activity, and wit counts.
- **Openness.** Bars are open settings where strangers may start talk (Cavan 1966, drawing on Goffman's "open regions," 1963).
- **Turn norms.** Overlapping, loud, competitive. Story rounds and topping are common.
- **Reportable.** Fights, money, work grievances, sports, women and men, local scandal.
- **Openers.** "You hear about…" / "Guess who came in last night."
- **Challenge.** Ridicule, and appeals to the bartender as arbiter ("Ask Sal, Sal was here").
- **Exaggeration and its license.** Talk under drink is partly excused. MacAndrew & Edgerton (1969) describe drunkenness as a culturally bounded "time out" ⚠ (the concept is theirs, not Cavan's). So bar-claims are discounted the next day ("that was the drink talking"). Tall tales are tolerated as entertainment.

**Barbershop**
- **Present and status.** The barber owns the floor and moderates. The client in the chair is a captive participant. Waiting men are an audience that interjects. Elders and known regulars outrank the young.
- **What it is.** Harris-Lacewell (2004) describes the Black barbershop as a site of everyday political deliberation: argument as public performance, ideas tested before peers. Mills (2013) traces its history as a protected Black space.
- **Reportable.** Politics, sports, neighborhood news, women, money, who got arrested.
- **Challenge.** Explicit and theatrical ("Man, get out of here with that"). Appeal to the room ("Am I lying?") or to the elder or barber.
- **Openers.** A provocation thrown into the room ("Y'all know who's the best that ever did it…").
- **Exaggeration.** Rhetorical overstatement as a debating move.

**Street corner**
- **Whyte (1943), *Street Corner Society*.** Corner groups have stable hierarchies (Doc's gang). A leader's word carries weight. Bowling scores tracked rank. Members' accounts are weighted by rank, and a low-status man's story is checked harder.
- **Liebow (1967), *Tally's Corner*.**
  - "Public fictions" and the "theory of manly flaws": men explain failures (lost jobs, broken marriages) as the result of manly traits ("I'm too much man for one woman").
  - Friendships are idealized in talk ("going for brothers") beyond their real durability.
- **Anderson (1978), *A Place on the Corner*.** At Jelly's bar and liquor store, men sorted into "regulars," "wineheads," and "hoodlums." Status talk turns on "decency" and "having something going for yourself." Claims get tested by the group.
- **Anderson (1999), *Code of the Street*.** Respect, "juice," and reputation are built partly through stories. Backing down is costly. A man's story *is* his campaign for respect.
- **Verbal genres** (Abrahams 1964; Labov 1972; Mitchell-Kernan 1972; Smitherman 1977):
  - Ritual insult (sounding, the dozens) must be *obviously untrue* to stay play. A personal, true insult breaks the frame and can lead to violence (Labov 1972).
  - Signifying: indirect, layered meaning.
  - Toasts: long rhymed badman narratives (Stagolee, the Signifying Monkey).
- **Openers and closers.** "Let me tell you something." / "Word is…" / "And that's on everything."

**Runners and the numbers game**
- **Who.** Runners (collectors) move daily through a neighborhood, house to house and shop to shop, so they are natural news carriers and gossip conduits. Bankers sit at the top, then controllers, then runners. Players include almost everyone.
- **Sources.** Drake & Cayton (1945), *Black Metropolis* (policy wheels); Light (1977), numbers as a community financial institution; White et al. (2010), *Playing the Numbers*.
- **Talk.** Dream-books translate dreams and events into numbers. Some combinations have folk names (e.g., the "washerwoman's gig" 4-11-44 ⚠ verify). A winning number was derived from a published, hard-to-rig figure. Big-hit stories circulate and inflate.
- **Reportable.** Who hit, how much, what dream led to it, who got stiffed, which bank got raided.
- **Exaggeration.** Payout sizes and the luck of named individuals.
- **Challenge.** "Show me the slip."

**Locker room / male peer group**
- **What happens.** Competitive status display and fraternal bonding. Talk about sex, conquest, and violence works as performance for the group, and boundaries get policed through ridicule (Curry 1991; Messner 1992; Lyman 1987).
- **Men's stories (Coates 2003).** Focus on achievement and contest, single-teller performance. Women's conversational stories more often co-construct.
- **Men's gossip (Cameron 1997)** is ordinary gossip in another register.
- **Exaggeration.** Expected and *mutually understood* (Frankfurt's bullshit). Challenge is by mockery ("Yeah right"), not demand for evidence. Sincerity, vulnerability, or real fear are what break the frame.

**Sports crowd during an event**
- **Who and status.** Mixed, but loud regulars and "hard" fans hold standing.
- **What happens.** Running commentary, prediction, blame of officials, chants. Marsh, Rosser & Harré (1978) found terrace "aggro" largely ritualized. Fans' talk of violence ran far ahead of actual violence ("the illusion of violence"). Desmond Morris (1981) describes tribal chants and rituals.
- **Rumor under acute tension** spreads extemporaneously (Shibutani): "They're letting more in at the back," "someone's been stabbed."
- **Reportable.** Moments and injustices. Afterward, "I was there when…" becomes a status claim, and the numbers in attendance grow with each retelling.

**Family dinner table**
- **Ochs & Taylor (1992)**, "Family narrative as political activity" / "Father knows best." In white middle-class U.S. families:
  - Mothers often introduce children's stories ("Tell Daddy what happened at school").
  - Fathers act as primary recipients and frequent "problematizers," questioning the protagonist's actions and creating a "panoptic" regime of evaluation.
  - Children are frequent protagonists and frequent targets of problematizing.
  - ⚠ I couldn't verify exact percentages online. Describe the pattern, not numbers.
- **Ochs, Taylor, Rudolph & Smith (1992):** storytelling as joint theory-building.
- **Blum-Kulka (1997), *Dinner Talk*:** cross-cultural variation in who has tellership rights.
- **Markers.** Prompting ("Tell Dad about…"), cross-examination ("And what did *you* do?"), moral codas from parents.

**Workplace break talk (docks, factory)**
- **Roy (1959), "Banana Time":** small machine-shop groups run ritualized "times" (peach time, banana time) and recurring kidding themes that make monotony bearable. Breaking a ritual is a social offense.
- **Burawoy (1979):** shop-floor "making out" games.
- **Pilcher (1972):** longshoremen's culture.
- **Halle (1984):** working men's talk.
- **Reportable.** Foremen, injuries, the time someone told off the boss (a recurring legend), close calls, pay.
- **Who speaks.** Senior hands set the tone, and newcomers are tested with stories and pranks.
- **Exaggeration.** Of one's own defiance toward management.

**Soldiers' war stories and veterans' talk**
- **Hynes (1997):** each soldier's war is small and local. "You had to be there" is a gatekeeping move.
- **Shay (1994):** recovery needs "communalization of trauma," telling to trusted listeners who won't flinch. Many tell only to fellow veterans, or not at all.
- **Bloch (1921):** censorship and the breakdown of the press revived oral rumor among soldiers. The field kitchen was its exchange.
- **Fussell (1975):** trench legends (the Angels of Mons; the crucified soldier).
- **Bourke (1999):** killing narratives were sometimes inflated, sometimes concealed, and drew on the language of adventure stories.
- **O'Brien (1990, fiction, "How to Tell a True War Story"):** "story-truth" vs. "happening-truth." A true war story "is never moral."
- **Who stays silent.** Commonly, those with the worst experiences, or with acts they are ashamed of. The belief that "those who talk most saw least" is folk wisdom, not an established finding ⚠.
- **False veterans** (Burkett & Whitley 1998, *Stolen Valor*, a polemical source ⚠): generic, cinematic detail; claims of elite units; wrong jargon.
- **Markers.** Euphemism for killing. Dark humor. Precise, small sensory memories (a smell, a boot) amid vagueness about the big picture. Refusal: "I don't talk about that."

**Sailors' yarns**
- **Sources.** Rediker (1987) and Creighton (1995) on maritime culture and storytelling. "Scuttlebutt" comes from the water cask where crews gathered and traded rumor. "The gam" is whaleships meeting at sea to trade news and tales (Melville, *Moby-Dick*, ch. 53, a literary source).
- **Status.** Old hands outrank green hands. Tall tales are an expected genre, and the "fish story" is understood as inflated.
- **Markers.** Distances, storm heights, and catch sizes inflate. News arrives secondhand from another ship, weeks stale. Openers like "When I was on the *X*…"

**Elders telling stories to children**
- **Basso (1996), *Wisdom Sits in Places*:** Western Apache stories are tied to named places and carry moral lessons. A story is "shot" at a listener like an arrow, to correct them without naming the fault.
- **Heath (1983), *Ways with Words*:** a strong contrast for credibility norms.
  - In Roadville (white working-class), stories must be factual, moral, and Bible-like. Embellishment counts as lying.
  - In Trackton (Black working-class), stories are expected to be embellished, creatively fictionalized performances with audience participation.
- **Miller et al. (1990):** families tell young children stories about the child's own past transgressions.
- **Fivush:** "elaborative" vs. "repetitive" parental reminiscing styles shape children's memory.
- **Markers.** Formulaic openings, morals stated or implied, repetition, call-and-response, the child asking "and then?", stories that hide their target.

**Market talk**
- **Geertz (1978), "The Bazaar Economy":** information is scarce, costly, and unevenly spread. Traders build ongoing client relationships ("clientelization") to manage it. Haggling is information extraction.
- **Clark (1994), *Onions Are My Husband*:** market women's networks in Ghana.
- **Van Allen (1972):** the Igbo Women's War of 1929 mobilized through market networks.
- **Markets are rumor hubs:** news about prices, harvests, officials, and violence passes with the goods.
- **Markers.** Information traded as favor ("for you, I'll tell you what I heard"), price rumors, a vendor's standing tied to reliable news, rapid regional spread on market days.

## 7. Institutional writing (Dossiers)

### (i) Key findings

**Estimative language.**
- **Sherman Kent (1964), "Words of Estimative Probability":** analysts and readers read the same words as very different odds, so Kent proposed fixed ranges:
  - certain (100%)
  - almost certain (~93%, ±6)
  - probable (~75%, ±12)
  - chances about even (~50%, ±10)
  - probably not (~30%, ±10)
  - almost certainly not (~7%, ±5)
  - impossible (0)
- Kent also reported that some analysts ("poets") resisted numbers, unlike the "mathematicians."
- **U.S. ICD 203 (2015)** standardizes terms: almost no chance / very unlikely / unlikely / roughly even chance / likely / very likely / almost certain. It separates *likelihood* from *analytic confidence* (low / moderate / high), which reflects how good the sourcing and reasoning are.

**Source grading: the Admiralty / NATO system (STANAG 2511).**
- Source reliability, A–F: A completely reliable, B usually reliable, C fairly reliable, D not usually reliable, E unreliable, F reliability cannot be judged.
- Information credibility, 1–6: 1 confirmed by other sources, 2 probably true, 3 possibly true, 4 doubtful, 5 improbable, 6 truth cannot be judged.
- Grades read "B2," "C3," "F6," and so on. New and walk-in sources default toward F.
- ⚠ UK police used a "5×5×5" grading, revised to "3×5×2" around 2016. Verify before relying on the details.

**Analytic pathologies.**
- **Heuer (1999):** confirmation bias, mirror-imaging (assuming the adversary thinks like us), anchoring, and the remedy of Analysis of Competing Hypotheses.
- **Wohlstetter (1962):** at Pearl Harbor the signals were present but buried in noise.
- **Jervis (2010), *Why Intelligence Fails*:** Iran 1978 and Iraq WMD. Analysts treated their assumptions as evidence, absence of evidence was read as successful concealment, and the beliefs could not be falsified.
- **Wilensky (1967):** hierarchy, specialization, and centralization produce "information pathologies." Bad news gets softened as it travels up.

**How institutions misread.**
- **Guha (1983), "The Prose of Counter-Insurgency":** colonial officials' reports on peasant uprisings systematically denied rebel agency. Uprisings were cast as crime, as "spontaneous" outbreaks, or as the work of outside agitators. Official prose turns a political act into a law-and-order problem.
- **Stoler (2009), *Along the Archival Grain*:** colonial archives record their writers' anxieties and categories. They must be read *along* the grain for what worried the state.
- **Scott (1998), *Seeing Like a State*:** states need "legibility." They simplify people into categories they can count, and they act on the simplification.
- **Douglas (1986), *How Institutions Think*:** institutions confer sameness. They classify, then remember and forget by their own categories.
- **Garfinkel (1967), "Good organizational reasons for 'bad' clinic records":** records are written to be defensible (contractual accountability), not to be an accurate history. Their gaps are structural.
- **Dorothy Smith (1978), "K is mentally ill":** an account instructs its reader how to read it. It assembles selected incidents into a category, cutting out whatever doesn't fit.

**Police and incident-report voice.**
- Third person or "Reporting Officer (R/O)," military time, abbreviations, "observed," "stated," "made contact with," "the subject," "no further action."
- Agentless passives that hide agency ("shots were fired," "force was used," "the suspect was injured").
- Orwell (1946) on euphemism and passive constructions.
- **Rewritten records are real.** At Hillsborough (1989), the Independent Panel (2012) found 164 police statements significantly amended, 116 of them to remove or alter comments critical of the policing ✔. Institutional accounts get rewritten to protect the institution.
- **S.L.A. Marshall's (1947)** WWII "ratio of fire" (only ~15–25% of riflemen fired) was influential for decades. Its fieldwork basis has been seriously questioned (Spiller 1988) ⚠. It shows how an authoritative institutional figure can harden on thin evidence.

### (ii) Page markers (Dossier voice)
- **Headers:** a file number, a classification, a date-time group, "SUBJECT:," "SOURCE:," "GRADING: C3," "DISTRIBUTION:."
- **Estimative phrasing:** "It is assessed with moderate confidence that…", "Reporting suggests…", "We judge it likely…", "Cannot be confirmed at this time." The confidence and likelihood can be visibly *wrong* while sounding precise.
- **Sources at a remove:** "a source of unknown reliability reports…", "a walk-in source claims…", "an F6 report, retained for completeness." The reader sees the institution elevate weak sources when they fit its expectations.
- **Category capture:** rebels become "agitators," "criminal elements," "a contamination event." A motive is assigned from the bureau's theory (money, foreign direction) rather than what the subject says.
- **Agentless passives for the institution's own acts** ("the settlement was cleared"), active voice for the subject's ("SUBJECT incited…").
- **Mirror-imaging:** "SUBJECT will likely seek…", projecting the bureau's own logic.
- **Absence as proof:** "The lack of observable activity indicates successful concealment."
- **Defensive structure (Garfinkel):** whatever protects the officer is documented in detail, whatever doesn't is thin. Telltale gaps, such as unexplained time jumps and a missing witness.
- **Revision traces:** redactions, "amended per supervisor review," statements that read more uniform than any group of real witnesses would.
- **Bureaucratic numbers:** false precision ("approximately 1,180 persons"), figures repeated across files with the source lost.

## 8. Enemy and adversary narratives

### (i) Key findings

**Dissonance and self-justification.**
- **Festinger (1957); Festinger, Riecken & Schachter (1956), *When Prophecy Fails*:** when a commitment is contradicted, committed believers often double down and proselytize.
- **Tavris & Aronson (2007), *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)*:** the "pyramid of choice." Two people start near each other. After a small choice, each justifies it and they slide to opposite sides, each fully convinced. Self-justification is mostly *sincere*, not lying. The agentless phrase "mistakes were made" is the signature.

**Accounts: excuses and justifications (Scott & Lyman 1968).**
- **Excuses** admit the act was bad but deny full responsibility. Types: accident; defeasibility ("I didn't know," "I had no choice"); biological drives; scapegoating.
- **Justifications** accept responsibility but deny the act was bad. Types: denial of injury; denial of the victim ("they had it coming"); condemning the condemners; appeal to loyalties; sad tales; self-fulfillment.
- **Sykes & Matza's (1957) "techniques of neutralization"** overlap these.

**Moral disengagement (Bandura 1999).** Euphemistic labeling; advantageous comparison ("compared to what they did…"); displacement of responsibility (orders); diffusion of responsibility (everyone did it); distortion of consequences; dehumanization; attribution of blame to the victim.

**Perpetrator vs. victim narratives (Baumeister, Stillwell & Wotman 1990)** ✔.
- Perpetrators portray their act as meaningful and understandable, with mitigating circumstances. They cast the incident as closed and isolated, with no lasting consequences, and see the victim's anger as an overreaction.
- Victims portray the act as arbitrary or incomprehensible and as part of a long-running pattern with ongoing harm.

**Face-work (Goffman 1955).** People defend "face" (their claimed self-image) through avoidance and corrective processes. Losing face publicly prompts saving moves: humor, reframing, blaming circumstances.

**Self-serving attribution (Miller & Ross 1975).** Success is credited to oneself, failure to circumstances.

**How defeated commanders tell defeats.**
- **Germany after 1918, the "stab-in-the-back" legend (Dolchstoßlegende).** Promoted by Hindenburg's 1919 testimony and others: the army was undefeated in the field and betrayed at home.
- **The U.S. South's "Lost Cause"** (Pollard 1866; analyzed by Gallagher & Nolan 2000). Defeat recast as overwhelming numbers and resources, with superior virtue and generalship; slavery's centrality erased.
- **Common structures:** "outnumbered"; "betrayed"; "the weather / the terrain / the supplies"; "a moral victory"; "the enemy fought without honor"; "we achieved our real objective."
- **The rare acceptance of responsibility.** Lee's reported "It's all my fault" after Pickett's Charge ⚠ (wording varies across accounts) stands out for being rare.
- **Dixon (1976):** institutional causes of military incompetence and the post-hoc rationalization that follows.

### (ii) Page markers (Adversary voice)
- **Agentless passives for their own harm:** "The village burned." "Losses were sustained."
- **Enemy agency inflated:** the enemy had overwhelming numbers, sorcery, treachery, or sheer luck.
- **Displacement:** "My orders were…" / "Had the reinforcements come…" / "Command never understood the ground."
- **Closed framing (Baumeister):** "It was one night, years ago, it's done." The adversary won't connect it to a pattern.
- **Justification vocabulary:** "necessary," "regrettable," "order," "they knew the risks," "examples had to be made."
- **Selective precision:** exact numbers for the enemy's crimes, round numbers or none for their own.
- **Face-saving under challenge:** jokes, contempt for the questioner, sudden appeals to honor, the claim "you weren't there" (shared with veterans' gatekeeping).
- **Honest-but-bent accounts:** sincere conviction (Tavris & Aronson), not a lie. The adversary believes the account because they built it to live with themselves.
- **Defeat legends grow:** the enemy's numbers swell with each telling, and the betrayer becomes a named scapegoat.

## 9. Cross-cutting tools for building the account types

- **Goffman's production format (1981, *Forms of Talk*).** The *animator* utters the words, the *author* composed them, the *principal* is the one whose position they represent. Hearsay is mostly animators passing on others' authorship. A Dossier is an institution as principal speaking through a clerk-animator.
- **Footing shifts.** "Now, I'm just telling you what they said" moves the teller's footing from principal to animator, so they take less responsibility.
- **Type → dominant distortion:**
  - **Protagonist:** reliable, but selective by role and constructed backward from the reportable event (Labov).
  - **Comrade:** good faith, but bounded by what they could see (stress tunnel, weapon focus), and it drifts through rapport retellings (Norrick) and audience tuning.
  - **Adversary:** self-justification, closed framing, displacement (Scott & Lyman; Bandura; Baumeister).
  - **Dossier:** category capture, estimative overprecision, defensive record-keeping (Guha; Garfinkel; Heuer).
  - **Hearsay:** leveling, sharpening, assimilation, number and date drift, attachment to famous names (Allport & Postman; Portelli; Hobsbawm; Olrik).

## Uncertainty flags (consolidated)
- Real-world rumor does not always "level" the way Allport & Postman's lab chains did. It can snowball.
- CBCA and Reality Monitoring have modest, contested validity. Treat their cues as tendencies, not tells.
- IDT is theoretically contested.
- Exact Ochs & Taylor percentages are not quoted here because I couldn't verify them.
- The framing that gossip overrides observation in Sommerfeld et al. was not verified.
- "Time out" is MacAndrew & Edgerton's concept, not Cavan's.
- The details of the UK police 5×5×5 / 3×5×2 grading and the "4-11-44 washerwoman's gig" naming should be checked.
- Lee's exact words and S.L.A. Marshall's data are disputed.
- "Those who talk most saw least" is folk belief, not a finding.
- *Stolen Valor* is polemical.

## THE MATRIX: observable speech markers by teller state

| Marker dimension | **Informed** (was there, honest) | **Uninformed** (honest, at a remove) | **Mistaken** (honest, but wrong) | **Deliberately lying** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Source claims** | "I saw," "I was standing right there." Gives the basis without being asked. | "They say," "word is," "my cousin heard." The FOAF chain shows. | Claims to have seen what was really heard or inferred (source-monitoring error). Sincere "I saw." | To the trusting: "I saw it myself" asserted readily. To skeptics: avoids checkable sources ("I was alone"), probes ("Who told you that?"). |
| **Structure** | Out of order, doubles back ("wait, before that…"). Builds backward from the key moment. | Leveled: short, one sharpened detail, context gone. Converges on Olrik shapes (threes, one villain). | Coherent and confident. May have merged events into one vivid, stylized scene. | Over-tidy, chronological, scripted. The same phrases recur verbatim across tellings. |
| **Detail profile** | Sensory, spatial, and odd irrelevant details. Others' mental states. Rough reconstructed dialogue. | Few sensory details. Generic or cinematic imagery. Details assimilated to stereotype. | Vivid central detail (weapon focus), blank periphery. The wrong detail held firmly. | Rich in uncheckable color, thin on verifiable specifics. Vague about the key moment. Over-precise on trivia. |
| **Numbers, dates, names** | Imprecise but bounded ("a dozen, maybe fifteen"). Admits not knowing. | Rounded, inflated, formulaic (100, 1,000, "in one night"). Deeds attached to the famous name. Dates moved to the "fitting" occasion. | Specific but wrong. Durations stretched ("forever"). Faces misremembered. Confident about drifted figures. | Selective precision: exact for the other side's crimes, round or missing for their own. Numbers chosen to impress or excuse. |
| **Self-presentation** | Includes unflattering facts. Self-deprecation. Pardons others. | Little self-involvement, except "I heard it first" status. | Neutral to self-serving without knowing it (memory tuned by past retellings). | Avoids self-deprecation. Image protection. Agentless phrasing for own acts ("the door got opened"). |
| **Uncertainty** | Admits memory gaps. Raises doubts about own account. Spontaneous corrections. | Hedges by source ("could be just talk"). Under acute tension, no hedging at all. | Little hedging. High confidence, often *increased* by retellings and feedback. | Few admitted gaps (they look weak). Or a strategic "I don't recall" exactly where evidence would bite. |
| **Under challenge** | Gives a basis, adjusts edges, keeps the core. "Ask Maret, he was with me." | Backs off ("I'm just telling you what I heard") or appeals to consensus ("everybody knows"). | Defends sincerely. Bewildered when contradicted. May conflate the challenger's version into memory. | Narrows: shorter answers, counter-questions ("Why would I…?"), character appeals ("Have I ever lied to you?"), anger or contempt, deflection to the challenger's motives. |
| **Across retellings** | Core stable, edges vary naturally. Different emphasis per audience. | Each relay shorter but sharper. Drift accelerates under anxiety. | Drifts further toward the purpose of past retellings (Tversky & Marsh). Becomes surer. | Stable script early. Embellishes with an uninformed audience. Can come to half-believe it (fabrication inflation). |
| **Typical exposure** | — | Contradicted by a direct witness, or by a record. | Contradicted by physical evidence or a co-witness. Teller is genuinely surprised. | Rarely in the moment. Caught later, through a third party, an object, or comparing accounts (Park, Levine et al. 2002). |

**Two further states worth writing:**
- **The performer, Frankfurt's bullshitter** (bar, locker room, sailors' yarns). Indifferent to truth, and the audience knows it. Markers: escalation in story rounds, theatrical intensifiers, no offense taken at "yeah right." This is distinct from lying because no one is meant to be fooled.
- **The sincere self-justifier** (Adversary or Dossier). Believes the account. Agentless passives, closed framing, displacement, category labels. It reads as honest because it *is* honest, and it is still bent.
