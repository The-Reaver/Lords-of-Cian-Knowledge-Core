# Memory and Retelling: How Honest Witnesses Get It Wrong

Research brief 05 in the accounts-psychology series. It covers how memory distorts what a witness
reports, and how retelling an event changes the memory of it. It is meant to support writing
accounts that are honest but wrong, and keeping them distinct from accounts that are lies.

---

## 1. Scope and search method

**Scope.** This brief covers memory under stress, attention under threat, post-event contamination
(misinformation, other witnesses, feedback), the confidence-accuracy relationship, own-group face
bias, source confusion, audience-tuned retelling, collaborative and social remembering, flashbulb
memory, trauma and combat memory, and errors in dating, counting, and estimating time and distance.
It leaves out deception detection as a field. That field is touched only where it helps separate a
mistaken teller from a liar (Section 3).

**Method.** Meta-analyses and systematic reviews came first, then the primary studies the field
treats as landmarks. Citations were checked by web search against publisher, repository, and
university pages. Partway through the pass, the session's web-search allowance ran out, and the
network blocked direct DOI and Crossref lookups. The citations are therefore marked in two tiers:

- **[V]** The title, authors, venue, and stated finding were confirmed by a web search during this
  pass.
- **[unverified]** A standard citation in this literature, given from the established record. It
  could not be re-checked in this pass. Its existence is not in doubt, but its page numbers, DOI,
  and any quoted figures should be checked before anything is published from them. No figure from
  an [unverified] source is used as a load-bearing number below unless it is flagged as such.

**Evidence grades.**

- **STRONG:** Supported by a meta-analysis or systematic review, or by several independent
  replications, with consistent direction.
- **MODERATE:** Supported by solid primary studies or a review, but with real moderators, limited
  field data, or mostly laboratory evidence.
- **CONTESTED/WEAK:** Mixed results, open methodological disputes, or support that rests mostly on
  case studies and qualitative fieldwork.

Effect sizes are reported where a meta-analysis gives them. Every finding cites at least two
independent sources or one meta-analysis.

## 2. Findings

### F1. High stress at the time of an event makes both face identification and recall of details worse

**Grade: STRONG.**

Deffenbacher et al.'s (2004) meta-analysis pooled 27 tests of identification and 36 tests of recall
of crime details. High stress lowered both, with an overall effect of about -.31 on correct
identifications and on recall accuracy. The effect was larger for target-present lineups and for
realistic staged crimes than for laboratory face-recognition tasks **[V]**.

The best field-adjacent study is Morgan et al. (2004). It tested 509 military personnel at survival
school, each of whom was interrogated face to face, for long periods, under both low and high
stress. Correct identification of the interrogator was 68% after low stress and 29% after high
stress. That is the identification rate for a man who stood a few feet away and threatened them
for over half an hour **[V]**.

**On the page.**

- The witness was close, the encounter was long, and they are certain they "would know him
  anywhere." They are often wrong anyway. Being near the man and spending a long time with him do
  not protect against error when fear was high.
- What survives stress is the gist (*he hit the old man, he took the strongbox*). What goes is the
  periphery: what the man wore, which hand he used, what else was on the table.
- A frightened teller can be exact about a single gesture and vague about everything around it:
  "I can still see his hand coming down. I couldn't tell you what color his coat was."

### F2. A visible weapon pulls attention away from the face

**Grade: STRONG.**

Fawcett et al.'s (2013) meta-analysis found an overall weapon-presence effect of *g* = 0.53 on
memory for concurrent elements of the scene. The effect grows with threat. It shrinks with longer
exposure and with retention interval. It did not differ between laboratory, simulation, and
real-world settings **[V]**. Loftus's original weapon-focus work, and the later debate over whether
the cause is threat or simply the unusualness of the object, are summarized in the same
meta-analysis.

**On the page.**

- The witness describes the weapon with near-forensic detail and the face only in generalities:
  "A cleaver, broad, notched near the heel, with rope wound round the grip. Him? Tall. Dark. I
  don't know." In a 1970s city: "a sawed-off with tape on the stock," and nothing about the man.
- Unusual objects do the same thing as weapons. A strange mask, a brand, or an animal in the room
  will crowd out the face.
- The longer the encounter, the more the face comes back. A witness to a three-second hold-up and a
  witness to a twenty-minute standoff should not sound alike.

### F3. Information supplied after the event is absorbed into the memory (the misinformation effect)

**Grade: STRONG** for the core effect. **CONTESTED** for the strongest claims about implanting whole
false events.

In Loftus and Palmer (1974), changing a single verb in a question ("smashed" versus "hit" versus
"contacted") changed speed estimates for the same filmed collision. The verb also made people more
likely to "remember" broken glass that was never shown a week later **[unverified]**.

Loftus (2005) reviews thirty years of replications. Misleading suggestion can alter details,
introduce objects that were never present, and, in some participants, produce memories of entire
events **[unverified]**.

Morgan et al. (2013) ran the effect under real high stress at the same survival school. After
misleading information, participants reported details from their interrogation that had not
happened, including a weapon, and some picked out a face that was not their interrogator's
**[V]** (the title and finding appeared in search results).

Blank and Launay's (2014) meta-analysis covered 25 studies and 155 effect sizes. Warning people
after the fact that what they heard may be wrong reduced the misinformation effect to less than
half its size on average, at negligible cost to accurate memories. "Enlightenment" warnings, which
explain exactly how the person was misled, worked best **[V]**.

A mega-analysis of eight implantation studies (Scoboria et al., 2017) put the rate of full false
memories for whole suggested events at about 30% under its coding scheme. Critics argue that the
coding inflates the figure, which is why this part of the finding is graded CONTESTED
**[unverified]**.

**On the page.**

- The questioner's words become the witness's words. If the investigator asked about "the
  slaughter at the ford," the witness says "slaughter" now, and the numbers grow to fit the word.
- The witness reports a detail no one else mentioned, and the reader can trace it to a question
  someone asked three chapters earlier.
- A warning helps but never fully works. "The clerk said there were two of them. I know he said
  that. But I think I saw two." The doubt is real, and the borrowed detail stays half-attached.

### F4. Confidence is informative at the first test and degrades after that

**Grade: STRONG.**

Wixted and Wells (2017) synthesized the confidence-accuracy literature. Under fair, unbiased
initial testing, high-confidence first identifications are highly accurate. Low-confidence first
identifications are not. Confidence expressed later, after feedback, rehearsal, or retelling, is
largely uninformative **[unverified]**.

Douglass and Steblay's (2006) meta-analysis covered 20 experiments and more than 2,400 witnesses.
A single confirming remark ("Good, you identified the suspect") inflated retrospective certainty
with *d* of about .98, inflated self-rated "basis for identification" with *d* of about .90, and
inflated willingness to testify with *d* of about .98. Witnesses also inflated their memory of how
good their view had been **[V]**. The primary demonstration is Wells and Bradfield (1998)
**[unverified]**.

**On the page.**

- Write the witness's first statement hesitant and the courtroom (or council-hall) version certain.
  The gap between them is the tell.
- After confirmation, people rewrite how well they *saw*, not only how sure they are: "The lamp was
  right over him. I had a clear look." In the first statement the lamp was across the yard.
- For a careful investigator inside the story, the first recorded words matter more than any later
  statement. Anyone who reads only the final, polished version gets the worst evidence.

### F5. People are less accurate identifying faces from groups they have less experience with

**Grade: STRONG.**

Meissner and Brigham's (2001) meta-analysis covered 39 articles, 91 samples, and nearly 5,000
participants. It found a "mirror effect": own-race faces produced more correct identifications and
fewer false identifications than other-race faces **[V]**. The paper's abstract quantifies this as
roughly 1.4 times the correct-identification rate and roughly 1.56 times the false-identification
rate, with race of face explaining about 15% of the variability in the data. These figures are
remembered from the abstract and were not re-checked in this pass. The effect is commonly explained
by differences in contact and expertise rather than anything innate.

**On the page.**

- Apply this to any group boundary the world draws: a dockside witness picking out a highland man,
  a Vask-born observer describing lowlanders, a 1960s white beat cop describing young Black or
  Puerto Rican men.
- The mistake is confident, and it lands on the wrong person *from the right group*: "It was one of
  those salt-marsh boys. That one." Pair it with F1 and F2, and the mistake becomes very likely.
- Contact cuts the effect. A witness who grew up among the group can describe faces precisely, and
  that difference can mark who actually belongs where.

### F6. People remember what happened but lose track of where they learned it

**Grade: STRONG** for the framework.

Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay's (1993) source-monitoring framework explains a whole family of
errors. Memories carry no label saying where they came from. People infer the source from features
such as sensory detail and plausibility, and that inference fails in predictable ways. People take
something they were told for something they saw, take something they imagined for something that
happened, and take another person's story for their own **[unverified]**.

The framework underlies the misinformation effect (F3) and memory conformity (F8).

**On the page.**

- "I saw him go over the wall." Pressed, the teller admits they heard the splash, then heard the
  crier the next morning. The seeing is a later construction.
- Vivid tellings from others get absorbed. A witness who sat through three retellings around a fire
  may report a detail with the *texture* of the most vivid teller.
- Dreams, rumors, and imagined scenarios ("I kept picturing how it must have gone") turn into
  memory over the years. An older witness may sincerely describe a moment no one could have seen.

### F7. A retelling is shaped for its audience, and the shaped version replaces the memory

**Grade: STRONG.**

In Marsh and Tversky (2004), people logged their real-life retellings. They labeled 61% of them as
distorted (exaggerated, minimized, with omissions or additions) and 42% as inaccurate. They told
different versions to different listeners, exaggerating to entertain and simplifying to inform
**[V]**.

Tversky and Marsh (2000) and Dudukovic, Marsh and Tversky (2004) showed the cost. Retelling with a
slant (as a prosecutor, as an entertainer) later biased the teller's *own* memory toward that slant
**[V for existence]** (both titles appeared in search results).

Marsh (2007) reviews why retelling is not recalling: goals, audience, and genre select and
transform what is said, and what is said is what gets rehearsed **[unverified]**.

The "saying-is-believing" effect (Higgins and Rholes, 1978) shows the same thing for judgments of
people. Speakers who tuned a description to a listener's known attitude later remembered the person
the way they had described them **[unverified]**. Echterhoff, Higgins and Groll (2005) showed this
happens mainly when the speaker wants a *shared reality* with the listener, that is, when the
audience is trusted and in-group **[unverified]**.

**On the page.**

- The same veteran tells the battle three ways: to children as a story of courage, to a widow as a
  quick, painless death, and to a drinking companion as a farce. Twenty years later, the version
  most often told is the one he believes.
- An account shaped for an ally comes out cleaner, more heroic, and more morally legible than the
  event was. One shaped for a superior comes out more procedural and more defensible.
- The words of the best line harden. A phrase that got a laugh or a hush in the first telling is
  repeated almost verbatim in every later one. That fixity looks like reliability, and it isn't.

### F8. Groups that remember together drift toward one shared version

**Grade: STRONG** for the laboratory mechanisms. **MODERATE** for how they play out in real
communities.

Hirst and Echterhoff (2012) review conversational remembering. Talking reinforces what is said,
suppresses related things that are left unsaid, and spreads one speaker's version to the listeners
**[unverified]**.

In Roediger, Meade and Bergman's (2001) "social contagion" study, items a confederate falsely
recalled were later reported by participants as their own memories **[unverified]**.

In Gabbert, Memon and Allan (2003), witnesses each saw a different version of the same event. 71%
of those who discussed it went on to report details only their partner had seen, against 0% in the
no-discussion control **[V]**. Wright et al. (2009) review memory conformity among co-witnesses
**[unverified]**.

Cuc, Koppel and Hirst (2007) showed that when a speaker selectively retells part of an event,
listeners also forget the unmentioned related parts ("socially shared retrieval-induced forgetting")
**[unverified]**.

Marion and Thorley's (2016) meta-analysis covered 64 studies and 75 effect sizes. Groups recall less
in total than the same number of individuals recalling separately. This "collaborative inhibition"
is stronger in larger groups, among strangers, and in free-flowing discussion. Individuals' later
recall is often improved by having collaborated, but it is improved toward what the group said
**[V]**.

Oral historians document the same convergence in the field. Thomson's (1994) *Anzac Memories* shows
Australian veterans reshaping their personal war stories to fit the national legend over decades
**[unverified]**. Portelli's (1991) oral histories in Terni show a community collectively
re-dating and re-situating a 1949 death so it fits a more meaningful later struggle **[unverified]**.

**On the page.**

- Survivors of the same fight who have drunk together for years tell it *in step*: the same order
  of beats, the same named moments, the same joke at the same place. Where the group version is
  thin, each one goes vague in the same place.
- The loudest or highest-status teller's details win. A quiet witness who saw something different
  will often drop it, or say "I thought I saw... but Corran was closer."
- What the group never says fades for everyone. An embarrassing retreat, a dead friend nobody
  names, a prisoner nobody mentions — these thin out of every member's memory, not just the
  speaker's.
- A newcomer who wasn't there can absorb the group version so completely that they tell it in the
  first person.

### F9. Flashbulb memories feel vivid and certain without being more accurate

**Grade: STRONG.**

Talarico and Rubin (2003) tested students the day after September 11, 2001, then later. Their
memories of where they were when they heard declined in consistency at the same rate as ordinary
everyday memories. Their vividness and confidence, however, stayed high **[unverified]**.

Hirst et al. (2009, 2015) followed more than 3,000 respondents for ten years. Memories of how they
heard the news, and of facts about the attack, were forgotten quickly within the first year and
then stabilized. Confidence stayed high throughout. By ten years, none of the measured factors
(media attention, discussion, personal loss, emotional intensity) predicted consistency **[V]**.
The once-changed version tends to be the one that sticks.

**On the page.**

- "I'll never forget where I was when they told me the King was dead." The details of that scene
  (who told them, what they were holding, the weather) change between tellings. The certainty does
  not.
- Being able to see it clearly is the teller's proof that it's accurate. For the reader, that is no
  proof at all.
- A teller corrected with their own earlier account ("you said it was at the market") will often
  insist that the *earlier* version was the mistake.

### F10. Trauma and combat memory: the gist holds, the details move, and they grow toward the teller's current state

**Grade: MODERATE.** The field data are solid but limited, and theories of trauma memory remain
debated.

Southwick, Morgan, Nicolaou and Charney (1997) questioned 59 Gulf War reservists one month and two
years after their return about objective combat events, such as seeing someone killed or being
under fire. 88% changed at least one answer, and 61% changed two or more. The number of events
changed from "no" to "yes" rose with PTSD symptoms at two years: memories *amplified* as distress
grew **[V]**.

Brewin, Dalgleish and Joseph's (1996) dual-representation theory describes a contested split.
Trauma memory has a sensory, involuntary intrusion component (flashback fragments) and a verbal,
narrative component, and the narrative can be disorganized while the fragments are vivid
**[unverified]**.

Together with Morgan et al. (2004) under F1, the pattern is that combat memory is neither indelible
nor erased. It is selectively vivid and progressively rebuilt.

**On the page.**

- A veteran's account at one month is sparse and literal. At ten years it holds more incidents,
  more danger, and more death, sincerely. It grows most in those who suffered most afterward.
- Narrate fragments where the teller can't hold the order: a smell, a sound, one face, delivered
  out of sequence. The full, well-ordered story usually belongs to the teller who has told it often,
  not to the one who was most hurt.
- A teller who is still affected may *undercount* aloud and *overcount* in private, or the reverse.
  The direction depends on the audience (see F7).

### F11. Time, distance, and number estimates fail in predictable directions under stress

**Grade: MODERATE** for duration and dating. **CONTESTED/WEAK** for crowd size and distance.

**Duration.** In Stetson, Fiesta and Eagleman's (2007) free-fall study, participants judged their own
fall to have lasted about 36% longer than other people's falls, with no increase in perceptual
temporal resolution. The "slow motion" is built in memory, not perceived in the moment **[V]**.
Loftus, Schooler, Boone and Kline (1987) found that witnesses overestimate the duration of brief,
stressful events, often substantially **[unverified]**.

**Dating.** Survey methodology has documented "telescoping" since Neter and Waksberg (1964): people
place distant events as more recent than they were, and more recent events as further back. Rubin
and Baddeley (1989) modeled telescoping as a product of uncertainty in dating, not of time being
compressed. Error grows with distance in time and is pulled toward the middle of the period being
asked about **[both unverified]**.

**Numbers and distance.** Crowd and force sizes are routinely misjudged, and the misjudgments follow
motive and emotion more than perception. Laboratory numerosity research shows large sets being
underestimated without feedback (Izard and Dehaene, 2008 **[unverified]**). Field estimates of
crowds and enemy forces are shaped by stakes and by the audience, which ties back to F3 and F7.
Threat increases how close a feared object appears (Cole, Balcetis and Dunning, 2013
**[unverified]**). The evidence here is thinner and less consistent, which is why it is graded
weaker.

**On the page.**

- "It went on forever" about a fight that lasted under a minute. "Half the night" about an
  hour-long siege. The teller's duration is a measure of fear, not of time.
- "Six years ago, maybe five." The event was nine years ago. A distant event pulled forward into a
  more meaningful period is especially common for older tellers.
- "There were hundreds of them." Count the boats, the cookfires, or the ration chits, and there
  were sixty. A teller who wants to justify the outcome (the retreat, the surrender, the
  heroism) inflates the enemy. A teller who wants to belittle the enemy shrinks them.
- "He was right on top of me." He was across the yard. Pair it with F4: once the teller is
  confirmed, they're certain the distance was small.

### F12. Older tellers: the reminiscence bump, and errors that are less likely to be noticed

**Grade: STRONG** for the reminiscence bump. **MODERATE** for age-related error patterns.

Autobiographical memory is unevenly distributed. People recall disproportionately many events from
roughly ages 10 to 30 (Rubin, Wetzler and Nebes, 1986; systematic review in Munawar, Kuhn and
Haque, 2018) **[both unverified]**.

Ageing tends to preserve gist while losing source detail (see F6). Older adults are more prone to
high-confidence errors (Dodson, Bawa and Krueger, 2007) **[unverified]**. Repeated retelling
across decades (F7, F8) amplifies all of this.

Note that in a world where lifespans run to hundreds or thousands of years, "the bump" would be
tied to formative years, not to a human age band. The analogue for an ancient teller is a dense,
vivid, frequently retold early period, with long stretches of later centuries remembered thinly
and by summary.

**On the page.**

- The old teller's youth is rich and specific. Their middle years are summary: "those were busy
  decades."
- They are fluent and sure in the formative period, and they confuse who told them what in the
  later ones.
- Their story has a set shape, polished by long use. Ask a question from outside that shape and the
  teller grows uncertain, or improvises within the shape's own logic.

## 3. Implications by teller state

**Informed** (was present, attended to the relevant thing, has not been heavily contaminated):

- Their first statement is the strongest evidence anyone has (F4). It is often *less* confident and
  less vivid than later tellings, with gaps they admit to.
- Even they are vulnerable to F1, F2, and F5 at the moment of encoding. "Informed" means they had
  access, not that they were accurate.
- Mark them on the page by restraint: specific about what they could see, open about what they
  couldn't, and consistent in the core while unstable in the periphery.

**Uninformed** (wasn't present, or present but not attending):

- Reports what the community says (F8), often in the first person (F6).
- Fills gaps with the most plausible schema: what usually happens in a raid, a strike, a trial.
- Mark them by generic detail, borrowed phrasing that matches other tellers word for word, and
  confidence that does not change when they're asked what they personally saw.

**Mistaken** (honest, present, and wrong):

- This is the most common and most useful teller for fiction. Their errors follow from the
  findings above: inflated numbers and durations (F10, F11), weapon over face (F2), the wrong
  person from the right group (F5), details absorbed from questions and companions (F3, F8), and
  certainty that has grown through confirmation and retelling (F4, F9).
- Their errors are *motivated without being deliberate*. They lean toward the version that has been
  rewarded, comforted, or agreed with (F7).
- When challenged, they defend the memory itself ("I saw it"), not a rationale for it. Shown a
  contradiction, they're bewildered rather than evasive. They may accept a correction and slide back
  to the old version later (F3's partial warning effect).

**Deliberately lying:**

- Telling a lying account from a mistaken one cannot rest on the usual cues. Deception research
  finds that observers judge lies at little better than chance, roughly 54% (Bond and DePaulo,
  2006), and that behavioral cues to lying are weak and inconsistent (DePaulo et al., 2003)
  **[both unverified]**.
- Inconsistency is *not* a reliable sign of lying. Honest memory changes between tellings in its
  details (F7, F9, F10), and consistency of peripheral detail is a weak predictor of accuracy
  (Brewer, Potter, Fisher, Bond and Luszcz, 1999 **[unverified]**). A liar who has rehearsed can be
  *more* consistent than an honest witness.

**Distinguishing the mistaken teller from the liar.** These are writing heuristics drawn from the
findings above, not validated detection rules:

1. **Direction of error.** The mistaken teller errs in the directions memory errs: toward gist,
   toward the vivid, toward bigger numbers and longer time, toward the group version. The liar errs
   toward what *serves them*, even when that runs against memory's grain. An oddly precise
   peripheral detail, a convenient gap exactly where the guilt lies, or an unusually exact
   timeline under high stress all point to construction rather than memory.
2. **Pattern of change across tellings.** Honest change drifts slowly along the audience and group
   lines in F7 and F8. Lying change jumps when the teller's interests change, such as after
   learning what someone else knows.
3. **Response to contradiction.** The mistaken teller defends the experience ("I *saw* it"), may
   produce a source error on reflection ("maybe I heard it from Vel"), and seems genuinely unsettled.
   The liar defends the story's logic, adjusts the narrative to fit the new fact, and rarely
   volunteers how they came to know something.
4. **Admitted gaps.** Honest tellers, especially early on, admit what they didn't see. Liars tend to
   cover everything that matters to them and to admit gaps only where those gaps are harmless.
5. **Both at once.** The richest teller is a liar who has begun to believe the lie. Rehearsing a
   chosen version produces saying-is-believing (F7) in the liar too. Decades on, a lie told often
   enough can be reported sincerely.

## 4. Implications by account type

These are suggestions for how the findings bear on each account type the project uses. They are
not canon rules.

**Protagonist series** (the protagonist's own close perspective, or their narrator's):

- Even a reliable, close narrator is subject to F1, F2, and F10 in the moment. Use sensory narrowing
  in combat scenes: the weapon, the hands, a single sound, a lost sense of time.
- When the series moves into the protagonist's retrospection, especially an account narrated long
  after the fact, apply F7, F9, and F12. The protagonist's own memory of their early years can be
  the most vivid and the most rehearsed part of the account.
- If a narrator records rather than remembers (an exact log, an instrument, a non-human recorder),
  contrast that exactness with the human tellers' drift. That's strong material, as long as the
  recording narrator's limits (what it cannot perceive) are respected as consistently as human
  memory's limits are.

**Comrade Account** (an ally, in good faith, with a partial view; the main consumer of this brief):

- This is where F7 and F8 do the most work. The comrade's version is tuned to the shared reality of
  the crew: more heroic, more morally clear, numbers inflated in the crew's favor, the crew's own
  costly mistakes thinned out (F8, retrieval-induced forgetting).
- The comrade's details will often match those of other crew members' accounts word for word.
  That's the group version, not independent corroboration.
- Their limited vantage point shows in confident first-person reports of things they could only
  have heard about (F6). A good way to plant a discrepancy is to have a comrade "see" something
  from a position where it could not have been seen.
- Over a long-running series, the comrade's account of an event should *drift* between appearances,
  slowly and in the direction of the group's preferred meaning, with the best lines repeated
  verbatim.

**Adversary Account** (an enemy, in good or bad faith):

- An honest adversary shows the mirror image: the protagonist's force inflated (justifying a defeat)
  or shrunk (belittling a loss), the adversary's own conduct cleaned up, and own-group face bias
  against the protagonist's people (F5).
- An adversary's account can be honest *and* wrong in a way that is more useful than a lie. A
  defeated commander's certainty that "there were hundreds of them" is sincere and serves him at the
  same time.
- Institutional adversaries pass their accounts through debriefings with leading questions (F3) and
  confirming feedback (F4), which produces confident, wrong, consistent official versions.

**Dossier** (an institutional record compiled from statements):

- A dossier is the end product of every contamination channel at once. It is built from interviews
  with leading questions (F3), confirmed and re-confirmed statements (F4), co-witnesses who
  discussed the event (F8), and witnesses who identified across group lines (F5).
- The clearest marker of a well-made in-world dossier is that it preserves the *first* statement
  and dates every later one. The clearest marker of a corrupted one is a single smooth narrative
  with no record of who said what first.
- Precise numbers in a dossier often carry false authority: a witness's guess, written down,
  becomes a fact.
- A dossier's errors propagate. Later investigators read it, and later witnesses are questioned
  with its terms.

**Hearsay** (a story that has passed through several tellers):

- Each link adds F7's audience tuning and F6's source loss. The story converges on the shape the
  culture finds meaningful: clear villains, round numbers, a memorable line.
- Hearsay keeps the vivid and the narratively useful, and loses the hedges. "He might have been
  wounded" becomes "he was wounded," then "he lost the arm."
- Dates in hearsay telescope toward meaningful events (F11, and Portelli's example under F8). An
  incident migrates in memory to the year of a famous battle or strike.
- Hearsay most often reaches a reader through a teller who is sincere. The error is in the chain,
  not the person speaking.
