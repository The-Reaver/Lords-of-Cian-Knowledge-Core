# Gossip and Reputation Talk: Evidence Synthesis for Account Writing

Research note 03 in the accounts-psychology series. Real-world research only. Nothing here is canon; it is craft evidence for drafting Comrade Accounts, Adversary Accounts, Dossiers, and Hearsay under VB-067, and for the dialogue inside any Series entry.

---

## 1. Scope and search method

**Question.** How does everyday talk about absent third parties work: who starts it, who is allowed to take part, what it does for groups, how accurate it is, how it differs when the target is powerful or low-status, and what changes when the talk is malicious or collected by an institution?

**Working definition.** Following Foster (2004) and Dores Cruz et al. (2021), gossip is evaluative or descriptive talk between a sender and a receiver about a third party who is absent. It can be positive, negative, or neutral. Rumor (unverified claims about events) overlaps with gossip but is a different thing, and this note keeps the two apart where the evidence allows.

**Sources searched.** Web searches confirmed authors, years, venues, and DOIs or URLs. Every source marked [unverified] could not be confirmed in this session. Those sources are used only as supplements and never as the only support for a finding. Evidence was ranked in this order:
1. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses: Card et al. 2008; Wu, Balliet & Van Lange 2016; Foster 2004; Dores Cruz et al. 2021; the Giardini & Wittek 2019 handbook.
2. Naturalistic observation: Dunbar et al. 1997; Szala et al. 2024; Robbins & Karan 2020, a pooled analysis of five recorded-audio samples.
3. Experiments: Feinberg et al. 2012, 2014; Beersma & Van Kleef 2011, 2012; Sommerfeld et al. 2007; Fonseca & Peters 2018; McAndrew et al. 2007.
4. Ethnography and conversation analysis: Gluckman 1963; Eder & Enke 1991; Bergmann 1993; Kniffin & Wilson 2005.
5. Historical scholarship on denunciation: Fitzpatrick & Gellately 1996.

**Evidence grades.**
- **STRONG:** a meta-analysis, or replication across independent groups.
- **MODERATE:** several independent studies, or a foundational framework that is widely accepted.
- **CONTESTED/WEAK:** a single study, a disputed result, or a failed replication.

**Limits.**
- Most quantitative work uses WEIRD student samples (Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic) in lab games. The economic-game literature shows that gossip changes behavior in lab groups. It does not show how villages or crews actually talk.
- The ethnographies cover small, bounded groups: a Makah-type community in Gluckman, a middle school in Eder & Enke, a rowing team in Kniffin & Wilson. They transfer well to crews, neighborhoods, and holds. They transfer less well to anonymous cities.
- Whether a finding holds in a pre-industrial setting is a judgment call, flagged where it matters.

## 2. Findings

### Finding 1. Most relaxed conversation is social, but "gossip" in the strict, judging sense is a much smaller share, and most of it is neutral.

**Evidence grade:** STRONG for "social topics dominate" (replicated). MODERATE for the exact proportions, which depend on how gossip is defined.

**Sources.**
- **Dunbar et al. (1997).** Observed conversations in relaxed settings and found about two-thirds of conversation time was spent on social topics: relationships, personal experience, third parties. Critical comment and advice-giving were rare.
- **Szala et al. (2024), Dunbar as co-author.** Revisited this with a finer coding scheme and found an even larger share, about 85%, was social.
- **Robbins & Karan (2020).** Pooled five samples, 467 people recorded by the Electronically Activated Recorder (EAR) in daily life. They used a narrower definition: talk about an absent person. Under that definition, gossip was about 14% of conversation, roughly 52 minutes a day. About 74% of it was neutral, about 15% negative, and about 9% positive. Targets were overwhelmingly acquaintances; celebrities were rare.
- **Dunbar (2004).** Frames this as the social-bonding function: conversation does for human networks what grooming does for primate ones.

**On the page.**
- Most talk about absent people should be ordinary: who married whom, whose boat is laid up, who moved to which block, who is ill. A teller who only ever reports scandal reads as a tabloid, not a person.
- Let the neutral register carry information sideways. "She's back at the forge on the east quay" tells the reader a location, a trade, and a fact about the talker's network all at once.
- Negative gossip should stand out against that baseline. When a neighbor who usually reports weather and weddings lowers his voice, the shift is the signal.

---

### Finding 2. Gossip is an access right that marks membership. Outsiders who try to gossip are rebuffed, and who may say what about whom tracks the group's boundary.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. This is a foundational ethnographic framework, corroborated in conversation analysis and observation.

**Sources.**
- **Gluckman (1963).** Argued that the right to gossip about someone is a privilege of membership. Newcomers must learn, often over years, the histories that let them join the talk. Gossip both maintains a group's values and keeps the group exclusive.
- **Bergmann (1993).** Found a structural rule in conversation: the gossiper and the recipient must both know the absent subject. The recipient should not be so close to the subject (a spouse, a parent) that the talk becomes an attack on them.
- **Eder & Enke (1991).** Showed in adolescent groups that who can start gossip, and who can challenge it, depends on standing in the group.
- **Paine (1967) [unverified].** Offered the classic counter-reading: gossip serves individual interests and information management more than group unity. The two readings are usually treated as complementary, not exclusive.

**On the page.**
- **Membership tests.** A stranger in a dockside bar who asks "what's the story with that one?" gets silence, a joke, or "you'd have to ask him." The same question from a man who worked the cranes ten years gets an answer.
- **Inheritance of context.** Insiders gossip in shorthand ("it's the Brennan business again"). Outsiders hear the words and miss the meaning. An outsider teller can show the reader a closed group without breaking it open.
- **The close-relation rule.** People stop gossiping when the subject's kin walks in, and the conversation visibly changes register. Use this as a tell that a newcomer has a hidden connection: the talk dies when he arrives, and he doesn't know why.

---

### Finding 3. Gossip opens with a sequence: check what the listener knows, mark the talk as confidential, distance yourself morally, and only then name or describe the absent person.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. This rests on conversation-analytic and ethnographic work that is widely cited. It is detailed observation, not a quantified effect.

**Sources.**
- **Bergmann (1993).** Describes gossip as "discreet indiscretion." The gossiper performs an ambivalent moral position: disclosing while signalling reluctance or disapproval of disclosing. Opening moves check whether the recipient knows the subject. The gossiper also displays how they came by the information, so as not to look like a snoop.
- **Eder & Enke (1991).** Showed that the opening is a test. The first evaluative remark is an offer, and the listener's response decides whether gossip proceeds.
- **Foster (2004).** Treats this ambivalence, and the pressure to deny that one is gossiping, as a defining feature of the genre.

**On the page.** These markers work in a fishing hold, a guild hall, a tenement stairwell, or a 1970s precinct house.
- **Checking knowledge:** "You know Tamsin? From the ropewalk?"
- **Lowering the voice and closing the circle:** a glance at the door, a lean in, a hand on the forearm.
- **Moral distancing:** "I'm not one to talk, but..." / "It's none of my business..." / "I only say it because I care about her."
- **Source-laundering:** "I didn't hear it from me" / "you didn't get this here" / "my cousin's wife saw it, not me."
- **Displaying innocent access:** "I was only there to drop off the mending."
- **Naming strategies:**
  - full name when the subject is a rival or the speaker wants a clear verdict
  - a role ("the new overseer," "her upstairs")
  - a pronoun with a nod ("you-know-who")
  - no name at all when the subject is dangerous to name

Leaving the name out is itself evidence of fear.

---

### Finding 4. The listener's first response decides whether gossip goes ahead. Agreement leads to escalation; a well-placed early challenge redirects it. Challenges come more easily from high-status members.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. Eder & Enke's structural finding is foundational and fits Bergmann's sequence analysis. Robbins & Karan's in-the-wild data are consistent with it but do not test it directly.

**Sources.**
- **Eder & Enke (1991).** Over three years of lunch-table observation, once an initial negative evaluation got a supportive response, further negative contributions followed and challenges became rare. An early challenge could turn the talk. Low-status members seldom challenged, and higher-status members could do so at less cost.
- **Bergmann (1993).** Gossip is co-produced. The recipient's uptake ("Really? What did she do?") licenses the gossiper to go on.

**On the page.**
- Give every gossip scene a hinge where the listener either takes the bait or doesn't. "Really?" and "Go on" escalate. "That's not what I heard," "she's always been decent to me," or plain silence and a change of subject stop it.
- **Status shows in who can say no.** A crew elder can say "leave it" and the talk stops. A new hand who says the same gets frozen out.
- An unreliable teller can reveal their own standing by reporting how their gossip was received: "Nobody said a word after that," versus "Corwin laughed, so I went on."

---

### Finding 5. Gossip is a low-cost way to enforce norms. People who expect to be talked about behave more cooperatively; free riders are named and avoided; targets often reform.

**Evidence grade:** STRONG. Independent lab groups have replicated it, a narrative review supports it, and field ethnography matches it.

**Sources.**
- **Beersma & Van Kleef (2011).** People contributed more to a group when they expected others to gossip about them and their choices were identifiable.
- **Feinberg, Willer & Schultz (2014).** Members readily passed on reputational information, used it to exclude selfish players, and the excluded players then cooperated at normal levels.
- **Sommerfeld et al. (2007).** In indirect-reciprocity games, gossip carried social information that recipients acted on, standing in for direct observation.
- **Wu, Balliet & Van Lange (2016).** Review the evidence that monitoring, spreading, and managing reputations sustains cooperation among unrelated people.
- **Kniffin & Wilson (2005).** In an 18-month ethnography of a rowing team, gossip targeted a member seen as slacking and enforced group expectations.
- **Gluckman (1963).** Placed the same function at the center of small-community life.
- **Baumeister et al. (2004).** Add a related function: gossip stories teach the culture's rules in narrative form, so a listener learns from someone else's misfortune without suffering it.

**On the page.**
- Show the policing working before anyone says anything. A hand who has been shirking hears the talk stop when he enters, notices that no one passes him the jug, and works harder the next morning.
- Gossip as instruction: an old stevedore tells a new hire about the man who skimmed from the tally and "was never trusted with a crate again." The story is the rule.
- **Reform closes the loop.** When the target changes, the talk changes ("he's come round"). A teller who keeps repeating old gossip after the target has reformed is revealing a grudge.

---

### Finding 6. Much negative gossip is protective: it warns others away from someone who exploits. The urge to warn is felt, and passing on the warning relieves it.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. One multi-study paper, consistent with the motive research below and the group-protection literature.

**Sources.**
- **Feinberg, Willer, Stellar & Keltner (2012).** Across four studies, observers who saw someone cheat felt frustrated. The more prosocial the observer, the stronger this was. Observers chose to warn the next player even at a cost to themselves, and passing the warning eased their frustration.
- **Beersma & Van Kleef (2012).** Identified "group protection" as a distinct motive. It became salient when someone had broken a norm, and gossip framed as protection was judged acceptable rather than petty.

**On the page.**
- **The warning register.** "Don't lend him anything." / "Count your change with that one." / "Don't be alone with the foreman." The listener is told what to do, not just what happened.
- **Physical relief.** The teller who has been carrying a warning speaks fast, then exhales: "There. Now you know."
- **Cost accepted.** A warner who risks something (the target is powerful, or a friend of the listener) and says it anyway is marked as a prosocial gossiper, not a malicious one. This is the cleanest way to signal a reliable Comrade Account voice.

---

### Finding 7. Motives are mixed and measurable: information gathering and validation, social enjoyment, group protection, and negative influence. Information seeking is the most common.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. A validated questionnaire, extended later, plus convergent reviews.

**Sources.**
- **Beersma & Van Kleef (2012).** Developed the Motives to Gossip Questionnaire. Four motives separated empirically, and the informational motive was the most prevalent reason people gave for starting gossip.
- **Foster (2004).** Proposed overlapping functions: information, entertainment, intimacy/friendship, and influence.
- **Wert & Salovey (2004).** Argued that all gossip involves social comparison: people use talk about others to learn where they stand.

**On the page.**
- **Information-seeking gossip asks questions:** "Is it true she's leaving the co-op?"
- **Enjoyment gossip has rhythm and laughter**, and the details are embellished for effect.
- **Intimacy gossip is a gift.** "I've not told anyone else this" binds teller and listener.
- **Influence gossip has a target audience,** not just a target. It is said in front of the right person so it will be carried to the right ears.

A single teller can shift motives within one conversation, and the shift is characterization.

---

### Finding 8. Who gets talked about: mostly acquaintances; same-sex peers and rivals draw the most interest; damaging news about rivals and good news about allies is the most prized.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. Converging survey/vignette and naturalistic findings.

**Sources.**
- **McAndrew, Bell & Garcia (2007).** Negative news about rivals and positive news about friends and partners were rated the most interesting and most likely to be passed on. Both sexes were more interested in same-sex others.
- **Robbins & Karan (2020).** Real conversations were overwhelmingly about acquaintances, not distant celebrities.
- **Wert & Salovey (2004).** Predict that comparison targets, especially those just above oneself, draw the sharpest talk.

**On the page.**
- People in a crew gossip about the next crew over and about the person who got the job they wanted, more than about distant lords.
- Good news travels too, but selectively. A teller who spreads a friend's promotion is building an ally's reputation as deliberately as a malicious gossip tears one down.
- A teller's gossip shows the reader who that teller measures himself against.

---

### Finding 9. Gossip about the powerful runs differently from gossip about peers: it goes underground, relies on euphemism, and serves those with less formal power. Its prevalence and effects are less well measured.

**Evidence grade:** CONTESTED/WEAK as a quantitative claim; MODERATE as a theoretical framework.

**Sources.**
- **Wert & Salovey (2004).** Identify powerlessness and perceived injustice as triggers of malicious gossip, with upward comparison as a driver.
- **Kniffin & Wilson (2005).** Found that gossip's function shifted across organizational levels, from teammates to coaches and rival teams.
- **Kurland & Pelled (2000) [unverified].** Modeled gossip as a source of informal power in workplaces.
- **Scott (1985) [unverified].** Documented peasant gossip and character assassination of wealthy villagers as an everyday form of resistance.
- **Gap.** The upward direction is far less measured than peer gossip. Naturalistic samples (Robbins & Karan 2020) mostly capture talk about peers and acquaintances.

**On the page.**
- **Euphemism and indirection.** Nobody names the magistrate; they say "the big house," "upstairs," "certain parties." A joke carries the criticism so it can be denied.
- **Rhythm of risk.** Talk about the powerful happens in places they don't go: the laundry line, the back table, the night watch. It stops dead when an outsider of unknown loyalty arrives.
- **Gossip as the only weapon.** Where the low-status cannot act openly, they can make a powerful man's name smell. Show the cost to the powerful as slow (doors closing, servants leaving), not dramatic.
- **Downward gossip is different.** The powerful gossip about subordinates as assessment ("he's soft," "she'll do"), in plain voice, without lowering it. The absence of caution is itself a power marker.

---

### Finding 10. Gossip is usually roughly accurate but not perfectly so. Senders bend it deliberately, often to punish. Moderate inaccuracy doesn't destroy its usefulness, but heavy distortion does.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. Several independent experimental studies. The field accuracy of gossip outside the lab is poorly measured.

**Sources.**
- **Sommerfeld et al. (2007).** Gossip in their groups largely reflected the target's actual behavior, and recipients acted on it.
- **Fonseca & Peters (2018).** Found spontaneous inaccuracy at non-trivial rates, much of it from gossipers wanting to punish untrustworthy players. That self-generated inaccuracy did not reduce trust. High levels of externally imposed noise did, though even very noisy gossip beat no communication.
- **Feinberg et al. (2014).** Senders passed reputational information readily, which is exactly what lets errors travel too.

**On the page.**
- **Distortion is directional.** A teller who exaggerates usually exaggerates against the person they already disapprove of: "He didn't just short the tally, he's been doing it for years." The exaggeration fits the grudge.
- **Accuracy in the core, drift at the edges.** The who and the what survive retelling; numbers, dates, and motives grow. Give mistaken tellers inflated figures, compressed timelines, and confident motives.
- **Self-correction from lived contact.** A listener who has dealt with the target directly weighs gossip against their own experience: "That doesn't sound like him." That is a reliability tell.

---

### Finding 11. Gossip passing through several mouths changes predictably: details level, the core sharpens, and the story bends toward what listeners already believe. Each teller's own stake shapes what survives.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE as applied here. This rests on the accuracy findings (Finding 10), the motive findings (Finding 7), and Eder & Enke's collective-evaluation dynamic. It is not a single measured effect of gossip chains in the field.

**Sources.**
- **Eder & Enke (1991).** Showed evaluations converging once uptake begins.
- **Fonseca & Peters (2018).** Showed senders adding punitive distortion.
- **Baumeister et al. (2004).** Gossip is transmitted as a story with a moral, which favors the parts that teach the rule.

**On the page.**
- **Hearsay at three removes** keeps the outline and the moral: a man defied the overseer and the overseer fell. It loses the mechanism, and the name may slip.
- **Chain markers:** "the way I heard it," "they say," "my mother used to tell."
- **Legend's drift.** The numbers grow (eight men become forty), the timeline compresses, the motive simplifies to virtue or villainy. Each drift should trace to a teller's stake or to the story's moral.

---

### Finding 12. Sex differences: women and men spend similar shares of talk on social topics. Where women gossip more, it is neutral, information-sharing talk, not more negative talk. The "malicious female gossip" stereotype is not supported.

**Evidence grade:** STRONG against the stereotype. MODERATE for the specific neutral-gossip difference.

**Sources.**
- **Card, Stucky, Sawalani & Little (2008).** A meta-analysis of childhood and adolescent aggression found boys substantially more directly aggressive. The difference in indirect aggression (which includes spreading rumors and social exclusion) was trivial.
- **Robbins & Karan (2020).** Women gossiped more than men only in neutral, information-sharing gossip. There was no sex difference in negative gossip. Younger people gossiped more negatively than older people. Extraverts gossiped more. Poorer or less-educated people did not gossip more than wealthier ones.
- **Dunbar et al. (1997).** Reported broadly similar shares of social talk across men and women.
- **McAndrew et al. (2007).** Found both sexes most interested in same-sex others, with some differences in who they confide in.
- **Hess & Hagen (2006) [unverified].** Reported some sex differences in self-reported informational aggression. It is not used as the basis for any claim here.

**On the page.**
- Write male gossip as gossip, not "talk." A crew of men trading news about who is shirking, who is sleeping where, and whose ship is unlucky is gossiping.
- Avoid giving negative gossip disproportionately to female characters. If a woman is the malicious gossip, make it her character, not her sex.
- Use age and temperament instead: the young and the talkative are the readier gossips. The old often hold their tongues, and their silence carries more weight.

---

### Finding 13. Institutions that collect gossip as intelligence receive mostly private grievance dressed as public concern. Informant reports carry the informant's motive and the institution's categories.

**Evidence grade:** MODERATE. Historical scholarship across several regimes, synthesized in a widely cited collection.

**Sources.**
- **Fitzpatrick & Gellately (1996).** Showed that denunciations reaching the state were often driven by private disputes (neighbors, spouses, housing, rivals), recast in the institution's preferred language.
- **Wert & Salovey (2004).** Predict this: envy, resentment, and injustice-feelings drive malicious talk.
- **Fonseca & Peters (2018).** Show senders shading information to punish.
- **Giardini & Wittek (2019).** Treat the movement of gossip into formal reputation systems (markets, organizations, states) as a core research problem.

**On the page.**
- **The informant's tell.** A report that is precise about the target's moral failings and vague about operations, and that names the informant's own rival as a suspect.
- **Institutional translation.** A neighbor's complaint ("she has men in at all hours") becomes a file entry ("subject receives unidentified male visitors; possible courier activity"). The reader sees both the gossip and the institution's misreading.
- **Payment distorts.** An informant paid per report produces more reports, and a handler under pressure files them anyway.

## 3. Implications by teller state

**Informed teller.** Knows the subject directly and has reason to report accurately.
- **Markers:** specific, checkable detail; plain naming; few hedges; willing to say what they don't know ("I can't speak to why"). Offers both good and bad. Corrects others' exaggerations.
- **Prosocial warning register (Finding 6):** instructs the listener what to do.
- **Lowers the voice for danger, not for drama.**

**Uninformed teller.** Relaying what they heard, without direct knowledge.
- **Markers:** source-chain hedges ("they say," "the way I heard it"), vague timelines, generic motives. Shows Finding 11's chain effects: a clear moral, a blurred mechanism.
- **Honest in good faith, but bounded.** What they relay is locked only as said (VB-067).

**Mistaken teller.** Believes something false.
- **Markers:** the same confidence as an informed teller. The error usually sits in a number, a date, a motive, or a misattributed act, and bends toward what the teller already believes (Findings 10-11).
- **Give the error a source:** a trusted cousin, an overheard fragment, an assumption filled in.
- **Don't telegraph it with hedges.** Mistaken people sound sure. The reader learns of the error from a locked rule or a reliable account, not from the teller's tone.

**Deliberately lying teller (malicious gossip, reputation attack).** Knows or suspects the truth and bends it to harm.
- **Markers:**
  - over-specific moral detail, thin operational detail
  - insistence on secrecy that also ensures the story spreads ("don't tell anyone, but...")
  - targets a rival or someone above them (Findings 8-9)
  - picks the audience for what it will repeat, not for who it will protect
  - cannot tolerate challenge: answers "that doesn't sound like him" with escalation, not evidence
  - recruits agreement early (Finding 4) to make the lie collective
- **The craft trap.** A lie that isn't sometimes believed is no threat. Show it working on someone who lacks direct contact with the target (Finding 10).
- **Under VB-067,** every point where the lie departs from the truth must trace to a locked rule or reliable account.

## 4. Implications by account type (VB-067)

**The Series (protagonist series).** Reliable narration, but characters inside it gossip, and what they say is locked only as said.
- Use gossip scenes to show a protagonist's standing. Is the talk freely shared with them, does it stop when they enter, are they the subject?
- A protagonist who refuses to take up gossip (Finding 4) is making a character choice that costs them insider access.

**Comrade Account.** Good faith, bounded by what the teller saw.
- **Strongest register:** prosocial warning and protective gossip (Finding 6), plus insider shorthand (Finding 2).
- **Natural failure modes:** loyalty-driven positive distortion about friends and negative distortion about the comrade's own rivals (Finding 8), and relayed secondhand material (Finding 11).
- Mark relayed material with source-chain hedges so its "as said" status is clear on the page.

**Adversary Account.** Unreliable by interest.
- Enemies gossip about a protagonist the way people gossip about rivals: hunting for damaging detail, exaggerating along the grudge (Finding 10), and in some cases deliberately lying.
- Adversaries also report downward assessments in plain voice when they hold power (Finding 9).
- An adversary's account of what "everyone says" about the protagonist is evidence of the adversary's own network, not of the protagonist.

**Dossier.** Institutional record built from informant gossip (Finding 13).
- Write the layers: the informant's motive, the handler's filter, the institution's categories.
- Typical misreadings:
  - private grievance logged as political threat
  - inflated or compressed numbers
  - confident motive attributions
  - the informant's rival named as a suspect
- The file's clinical register hides the gossip underneath. A reader who has seen the gossip scene elsewhere recognizes it.
- Consistent with VB-067, a Dossier may not add new facts about its own institution.

**Hearsay.** Unreliable by vantage. Gossip several removes from the event.
- Keeps outline and moral, loses mechanism, drifts in numbers and names (Finding 11). Carried by chain markers.
- Suits bystanders who never met the subject and gossip about the powerful in euphemism (Finding 9).
- Hearsay may repeat a deliberately open question only as VB-067 and the relevant reserved rule permit.
- Neutral gossip is the most common kind (Finding 1). Hearsay can be mostly harmless news and still carry one damaging error.

**Cross-setting note.** Every marker above was chosen to work both in a pre-industrial hold or harbor and in a 1960s-1990s American city:
- lowered voices
- glances at doors
- source-laundering
- euphemisms for the powerful
- the talk stopping when someone arrives

What changes is the venue (well, quay, washhouse versus stoop, barbershop, precinct break room) and the medium of escalation (criers and pamphlets versus phone calls and flyers). The world's tech constraints govern the medium; the social mechanics hold regardless.
