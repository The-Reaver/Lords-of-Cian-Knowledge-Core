# Settings of Talk: How Stories Move in Specific Social Places

Research file 06 in the accounts-psychology series. This is evidence synthesis for fiction craft. It is not canon and adds nothing to `canon-ledger.json`.

## 1. Scope and search method

**Question.** Who talks, in what order, and under what truth rules, in the places where stories about people get told? For each setting the file covers who is present and how they rank; who holds the floor and how it passes; what is worth telling and what is off-limits; how talk opens, closes, and gets challenged; how exaggeration shows itself; how outsiders are handled; and how a liar or a bragger is checked.

**Settings covered.** Bars and taverns; street corners and the numbers game; barbershops and salons; locker rooms and male peer groups (with barracks); sports crowds; family dinner tables; work breaks, docks, and ships; soldiers' and veterans' war stories; elders telling stories to children; wakes and funerals; markets.

**Method.** Each anchor source in the brief was checked by web search for author, year, title, and publisher, and further sources were added where a setting needed a second independent ethnography. Only sources whose bibliographic details could be confirmed are given unmarked. One detail I could not confirm is marked [unverified]. Findings summarize what the ethnographies report. Where two ethnographies point the same way from different places or decades, that agreement is the basis for the grade.

**Grades.**
- **STRONG:** the same pattern appears in several independent ethnographies or studies.
- **MODERATE:** one major ethnography plus at least one independent supporting source.
- **CONTESTED/WEAK:** the evidence is thin, comes from a single study, or the sources disagree.

**Source window and limits.** Most sources are American or British studies from 1943 to 2015. Three widen the base: Geertz's Moroccan bazaar, Glassie's Ulster ceili, and Rediker's eighteenth-century seamen. The settings are therefore overwhelmingly modern and Western. Treating them as models for a pre-industrial world is a craft choice, not a research finding. The "On the page" markers are chosen to work in both a pre-industrial fantasy setting and a 1960s-90s American city.

**One cross-setting principle used throughout.** Labov (1997) states an inverse relation between reportability and credibility: the more an event is worth telling, the less believable it is. Every setting below manages this tension in its own way. Some reward the incredible tale and openly enjoy it as performance. Others punish any departure from fact. That difference is the main variable in the comparison table in section 3.

## 2. Settings

### 2.1 Bars, taverns, pubs

**Participants and status order.** Regulars outrank newcomers everywhere the studies look. Cavan (1966), a Goffman student working in San Francisco bars, separates "home territory" bars, where a core of regulars treats the room as theirs, from open bars, where strangers may approach one another. In LeMasters' (1975) Wisconsin tavern "The Oasis," rank among construction tradesmen came from skill, pay, and long attendance. The bartender and owner sit outside that ladder: they know everyone and hold the information. Spradley and Mann (1975) show that the women serving drinks are ranked below every customer. Men's joking at waitresses' expense is a large part of the room's talk, and the waitresses run counter-rituals of their own.

**Turn-taking and floor.** Oldenburg (1989) argues that talk is the main activity of "third places," and that levelling is one of their features: rank from outside is set aside for a while. In practice the floor goes to the wittiest or most senior regular. Fox (2004) describes pub talk in England as built on banter and irony. Earnestness is penalized, and the bar counter is the zone where strangers are allowed to talk to each other.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: work, sport, women and men, local scandal, absent people's foolishness. Taboo: real self-disclosure of weakness (LeMasters; Coates 2003, whose pub-recorded carpenters avoid personal vulnerability), money owed between regulars, and a regular's serious private trouble, which is talked about only behind his back.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** The opening is a ritual greeting and an order, often an offer of a drink. Fox describes round-buying as almost sacred: not buying your round counts as heresy. Closers are the last round and a departure announced to the room. Challenges come through mockery rather than direct contradiction. A claim gets "taken the piss out of" (Fox), and a man who cannot laugh at it loses standing.

**Exaggeration and its signals.** Exaggeration is tolerated and expected in comic or sporting stories, and is signaled by laughter, rounder numbers, and the teller's own grin. LeMasters records tavern talk about sex and fighting that everyone understands as partly performance.

**Outsiders.** In home-territory bars the stranger is watched, given short answers, and made to show that he understands the norms before he is admitted to talk (Cavan; Spradley & Mann on the college bar's insiders).

**Checking the liar or bragger.** The regulars' shared memory checks him ("you said it was four last year"). He is also made the butt of the room, and the bartender, who has heard every version, has the last word.

**Grade: STRONG** (Cavan 1966; LeMasters 1975; Spradley & Mann 1975; Oldenburg 1989; Fox 2004; Coates 2003).

**On the page.**
- Name the regulars' seats.
- Let the barkeep correct a number.
- Show a stranger answered in single words until he buys a round or takes a joke.
- Let laughter, not denial, deflate a brag.
- Works in a dockside alehouse or a corner bar in 1970s Detroit alike.

### 2.2 Street corners, stoops, runners, the numbers game

**Participants and status order.** Every corner ethnography finds a visible pecking order, and finds that talk is a main way that order is made and remade.
- **Whyte (1943), Boston's North End.** The rank of Doc's corner boys showed even in their bowling scores. A leader's suggestion was taken up where a follower's was ignored.
- **Anderson (1978), Jelly's, Chicago.** The men sort into "regulars," "wineheads," and "hoodlums." Claims to being "down" with someone, or to having a certain status, are made in talk and checked by others in front of the group.
- **Liebow (1967), Tally's Corner, Washington, D.C.** The corner's talk covers repeated failure at work and in marriage with a shared "theory of manly flaws."
- **Duneier (1999), Sixth Avenue sidewalk vendors.** Talk and mutual surveillance organize a working community.
- **Venkatesh (2006), "Off the Books."** Disputes in a Chicago shadow economy are settled by informal adjudicators: a pastor, a gang leader, a respected hustler.

**Turn-taking and floor.** Talk runs as long, open sessions. Anyone may speak, but leaders and people with fresh news hold the floor. Among adolescents, Goodwin (1990) shows that in Philadelphia children's "he-said-she-said" disputes, telling who said what about whom is itself the act that starts a confrontation. On the corner, reporting talk is a form of action.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: who got over on whom, who is "lame," money made, police, women. Taboo: open admission of dependence or fear (Liebow; Anderson 1999 on the "code of the street," where showing weakness invites predation; Bourgois 1995 on East Harlem crack dealers' constant "respect" work). Naming an informant or a business arrangement to outsiders is also taboo.

**Ritual insult.** Labov (1972, "Rules for Ritual Insults") and Abrahams (1962, the dozens) show that sounding works only because the insult is understood to be untrue. "Your mother's so..." is a contest of wit. A *personal* insult that could be true breaks the frame and becomes a real fight. This gives fiction a precise mechanism for marking exaggeration: obvious falsity is the license.

**Numbers and policy talk.** White, Robertson, Garton and White (2010) show that in interwar Harlem the numbers were everyday talk. Dream books, hunches, the day's number, and who "hit" were common news, and runners were trusted public figures carrying information as well as slips. A runner's credibility was his business.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** Openings are greetings that claim a relationship ("what's happening," a nickname). Closers are an excuse tied to a mission ("I got to see a man"). Challenges are public: "you lying," "prove it," a demand for a witness. Anderson (1978) shows a claim that cannot be backed by someone present being laughed off.

**Exaggeration signals.** Sexual conquests, fights won, money made (Liebow; Bourgois). The group lets them pass when they are entertaining and costless. They are called when the claim would change someone's standing.

**Outsiders.** Watched, tested, and categorized: police, social worker, mark, or someone who can be "down" (Whyte's and Liebow's own accounts of their entry; Duneier's dependence on Hakim Hasan as sponsor). Entry comes through a sponsor.

**Checking.** Through cross-witnessing, the presence of the person named, and the long memory of the corner.

**Grade: STRONG** (Whyte 1943; Liebow 1967; Anderson 1978, 1999; Duneier 1999; Venkatesh 2006; Bourgois 1995; Labov 1972; Abrahams 1962; Goodwin 1990; White et al. 2010).

**On the page.**
- A boast the whole group knows is false, allowed because it is funny.
- A second boast, almost identical, called instantly because it claims rank.
- The runner who knows everyone's number and everyone's debts.
- An outsider who can speak only once someone vouches for him.
- Pre-industrial equivalents: the well-steps, the gate of a quarter, the lottery-crier or tally-man.

### 2.3 Barbershops and salons

**Participants and status order.** The barber or stylist owns the space and runs the conversation. Elders and long-standing customers come next, then the young, then walk-ins. Mills (2013) traces Black barbershops in America as businesses that gave barbers the financial independence to keep a public space for civil-rights talk. Harris-Lacewell (2004) recorded Black men in a barbershop on Saturday mornings and argues that this everyday talk is real political deliberation, with a range of ideologies argued out in it.

**Turn-taking and floor.** It is a long-session, multi-party room. Waiting customers form an audience, the barber feeds topics, and an argument can be taken up and dropped as chairs empty. Majors (2015) describes a Black women's salon as a cross-generational teaching space ("shoptalk") where older women model problem-solving through narrative, and younger ones learn by contributing.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: politics, sport, neighborhood news, relationships, advice. Heated disagreement is welcome. Taboo: matters that would shame a specific present person, and disclosure to outsiders of what was said inside. The shop's value depends on talk staying there.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** Opening: a greeting, a place in the queue, a quick catch-up. Closer: paying, the mirror, a parting jab. Challenge is open argument, often loud, settled by the barber's or an elder's word, or left as an ongoing feud carried over to next week.

**Exaggeration signals.** Sport and personal exploits are overstated for laughs. Political claims are argued on their merits, and an audience heckles an obvious stretch.

**Outsiders.** Welcomed as customers, kept at the edge of the talk until they show familiarity. Harris-Lacewell notes the space's value as a Black space; a white or official outsider changes what is said.

**Checking.** The audience is large and continuous, so a brag is tested against what others in the room know. The barber's knowledge of every customer is the final memory.

**Grade: MODERATE** (Harris-Lacewell 2004 and Mills 2013 are the major sources; Majors 2015 supports for salons; Oldenburg 1989 lists hair salons among third places).

**On the page.**
- The keeper of the chair as the town's archivist.
- An argument picked up where it left off last week.
- A customer told "not in here" when talk turns to someone's child.
- Pre-industrial equivalents: the barber-surgeon, the bathhouse, the women's washing place.

### 2.4 Locker rooms, male peer groups, barracks

**Participants and status order.** Status comes from performance (starters over benches, veterans over rookies) and from the ability to give and take verbal abuse. Curry (1991), working in college locker rooms, recorded collective talk about competition and women. The talk was sexist and homophobic, and it worked as bonding and as a display of heterosexual masculinity. Kiesling (2005), studying an American fraternity, shows men building closeness indirectly, through insult, competition, and talk about women, because direct affection is unavailable under dominant scripts of masculinity. Messner (1992, "Power at Play") documents how athletic men's identities are built on public performance.

**Turn-taking and floor.** It is group performance. Short turns, interruptions, and topping (each story bigger than the last) are the norm. Coates (2003) finds men's group stories centered on achievement and conflict, with minimal self-disclosure. Stories also arrive in sequence, each matching or topping the one before.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: athletic feats, sexual claims, humiliating others, injury endured. Taboo: fear, tenderness, doubt in the group's values. Curry notes that players' private one-on-one talk was more honest than the public locker-room register. The same man says different things depending on audience size.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** Openings are insults that mean welcome. Closers are dispersal to the field or showers. Challenge is ridicule and the counter-boast.

**Exaggeration signals.** Sexual and physical claims are inflated and partly understood to be inflated. Topping sequences are visibly competitive.

**Outsiders.** Newcomers are hazed into the register. Women and reporters are outside it, and their presence changes the talk entirely.

**Checking.** Teammates witnessed the performance, so on-field claims are policed hard. Off-field claims are policed only by mockery. Barracks talk follows the same structure, with rank and time served added as status markers. Hynes (1997) notes the gap between soldiers' bravado among themselves and their testimony afterwards (see 2.8).

**Grade: STRONG** for the public-performance and bonding pattern (Curry 1991; Kiesling 2005; Coates 2003; Messner 1992). **CONTESTED/WEAK** for how far the locker-room pattern transfers to barracks, which is supported here only by Hynes's memoir-based evidence.

**On the page.**
- A topping sequence: three men each enlarging the last one's story.
- The same character saying something different alone than in the group.
- Insult as welcome.
- Crew quarters, a sparring yard, a garrison mess hall.

### 2.5 Sports crowds during an event

**Participants and status order.** Status comes from identification and knowledge. Long-time season-ticket fans and lifelong supporters outrank fair-weather ones. Wann, Melnick, Russell and Pease (2001) synthesize research showing that team identification shapes fans' emotion, aggression, and self-esteem. Cialdini et al. (1976) documented "basking in reflected glory": students wore school colors more often and said "we won" (but "they lost") after victories.

**Turn-taking and floor.** During the event there is no orderly floor. Talk is shouting and chanting, and outcomes drive brief exchanges. Collins (2004) builds on Durkheim's "collective effervescence." When a crowd shares rhythm and focus, emotional energy and symbols of membership are produced, which is why crowd chants and the stories told about "being there" become identity markers.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: the play, the referee, the enemy, "I was there when." Taboo: supporting the other side, criticizing your own side in a way that sounds disloyal.

**Exaggeration signals.** "We" for the team, numbers growing in memory, "the greatest ever." The truth norm is loose because loyalty, not accuracy, is being performed.

**Outsiders.** An opposing fan is tolerated, jeered, or threatened depending on the stakes.

**Checking.** Almost none during the event. Afterwards a shared record (the score, other witnesses) checks claims about the play, while claims about the crowd's own feelings go unchecked.

**Grade: MODERATE** (Wann et al. 2001; Cialdini et al. 1976; Collins 2004 for the ritual mechanism).

**On the page.**
- "We" when they win, "they" when they lose.
- A crowd count that doubles with each retelling.
- Pre-industrial equivalents: a duel's crowd, an arena, a race, a public execution.
- A crowd's account of a fight is rich in feeling and poor in mechanics.

### 2.6 Family dinner tables

**Participants and status order.** Parents outrank children, and Ochs and Taylor (1992) found a gendered structure in the American families they recorded: a "Father knows best" pattern. Mothers often introduce children's stories ("tell Daddy what happened"). Fathers are frequent primary recipients and evaluators, and mothers and children are more often the protagonists whose conduct gets judged. Blum-Kulka (1997), comparing Israeli and Jewish American families, shows that who may tell, how topics start, and how children are drawn in vary sharply by culture even among families of common background.

**Turn-taking and floor.** Adults allocate turns, and children win the floor by being invited or by producing a story worth hearing. Stories are co-narrated: listeners add, correct, and question (Ochs & Taylor; Blum-Kulka).

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: the day's events, children's achievements and problems, plans. Taboo: adult money trouble and adult sexuality in front of children, and in many families open criticism of the father.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** Opening: prompt questions ("how was school?"). Closer: dismissal, dessert, washing up. Challenge is direct and frequent. Parents question details, especially of children's accounts, and the table works as a small court.

**Exaggeration signals.** A child's exaggeration is corrected openly. Adult exaggeration is tolerated when comic and corrected by the other parent when it bears on family facts.

**Outsiders.** Guests receive a performance version of the family. Blum-Kulka notes how guest presence changes sociability.

**Checking.** By co-narration: someone else at the table was there, or knows.

**Grade: MODERATE** (Ochs & Taylor 1992; Blum-Kulka 1997; Heath 1983 on household storytelling, see 2.9). The father-as-judge pattern is from middle-class American samples and is not universal.

**On the page.**
- "Tell your father what you told me" as a trap.
- The other parent quietly correcting a number.
- A family's set version of an event, retold with fixed lines.
- Guests hearing the polished version.

### 2.7 Work breaks: factory floors, docks, ships

**Participants and status order.** Seniority and skill. Roy (1959), working as a machine operator, describes a small group whose day was broken by ritual "times" such as banana time, peach time, and coke time. Each was a repeated, predictable exchange of kidding with fixed roles, and the oldest worker was the anchor. LeMasters (1975) shows tradesmen's status talk carrying from site to tavern. Coates (2003) recorded garage mechanics on break. Rediker (1987), on eighteenth-century merchant seamen, shows a strong oral culture: yarns, songs, shared complaint about masters and pay, and a sharp sense of who among the crew could be trusted.

**Turn-taking and floor.** Talk follows the rhythm of the work. Short exchanges are fitted around tasks, longer sessions come at meals and on watches below. The same jokes and themes recur, and their predictability is the point (Roy).

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: the boss's failings, pay, past jobs, dangerous incidents, sex, home. Taboo: informing to management, and in Rediker's sailors anything that broke solidarity against officers.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** Openings are ritual teasing. Closers are the return to work. Challenge comes from experience: a man who claims a skill is tested at the next task.

**Exaggeration signals.** The yarn is a recognized genre of tall telling among sailors (Rediker), as the tall tale is in Bauman's (1986) Texas material. Everyone knows the yarn is a yarn. Claims about skill or about the job itself are policed, because the work would expose them.

**Outsiders.** New hands are watched, given dirty work, and teased into the group (Roy describes his own slow entry).

**Checking.** The work itself checks claims about work. Recounted pasts go unchecked unless someone else was there.

**Grade: MODERATE** (Roy 1959; Rediker 1987; LeMasters 1975; Coates 2003).

**On the page.**
- The same joke every day at the same hour.
- The yarn told on watch, which nobody believes and everybody repeats.
- The man who says he can splice, given the line to splice.
- Smithies, rope-walks, foundries, galleys.

### 2.8 Soldiers' and veterans' war stories

**Participants and status order.** Combat experience outranks everything, then the severity of the fighting seen. Rear-echelon troops and civilians stand outside. Hynes (1997), surveying memoirs and testimony from both World Wars and Vietnam, describes the soldiers' tale as a genre defined by "what it was like." Its tellers distrust any version not from someone who was there.

**Turn-taking and floor.** Among veterans, the floor goes to the one who was closest to the worst. Many who saw the most say the least. Shay (1994), a VA psychiatrist, argues that recovery from combat trauma requires telling to a listener who can bear the story without flinching or moralizing (he calls this the communalization of trauma). Most listeners cannot, so the stories are held back.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable to civilians: comic incidents, hardship, the sanitized arc. Reportable among veterans: specifics, failures, the dead by name. Commonly withheld: one's own killing in detail, atrocity, cowardice (one's own or a friend's). Shay's patients describe betrayal by command as the deepest wound and the hardest to speak.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** Openings ask for place and unit ("where were you?"), which establishes the right to speak. Closers are silence, a toast, a change of subject. Challenge: a claim of combat that does not match place, date, or unit details is quietly or bluntly exposed by those who were there.

**Exaggeration signals.** Two kinds. Bravado among peers, and inflated stories told to civilians by men who were not in combat. Shared detail checks the second. Portelli (1991) shows that oral memory systematically reshapes events, even moving a 1949 killing into a 1953 strike in workers' memory, and argues that these "errors" carry meaning rather than being mere noise.

**Outsiders.** Kept at a distance by the "you had to be there" barrier (Hynes; Shay).

**Checking.** Unit and veteran memory, and the details only a participant would know.

**Grade: STRONG** for the reticence-among-survivors and "had to be there" patterns (Hynes 1997; Shay 1994; Portelli 1991 on oral reshaping). **MODERATE** for any specific claim about who tells and who stays silent.

**On the page.**
- The quiet man at the table who was there.
- The loud man who wasn't, caught on a wrong place-name.
- The detail no outsider would know.
- A story that is truer in its feeling than in its date.

### 2.9 Elders telling stories to children

**Participants and status order.** Elders hold the floor, but communities differ sharply in what children may do with it. Heath (1983) compared two Carolina mill communities. In Black working-class Trackton, storytelling was performance. Children had to break into adult talk and win the floor, and imaginative, exaggerated tales were admired. In white working-class Roadville, stories had to be true and morally pointed, and a child's fanciful story could be rebuked as a lie. Glassie (1982), in an Ulster community, documents old storytellers at the evening ceili who hold a large repertoire, distinguishing historical stories told as true from comic and fantastic tales enjoyed as such.

**Turn-taking and floor.** In the ceili, the established teller holds long turns and is fed cues by listeners. Children listen, and their own turns come later in life (Glassie). In Trackton, children compete for the floor (Heath).

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: local history, ancestry, the dead, ghosts, moral lessons, comic disasters. Taboo varies: open shame of living families, sexual matter in front of children.

**Exaggeration signals.** Genre labels carry it. A community knows which stories are "history" and which are entertainment (Glassie; Bauman 1986 on Texas tall tales and "expressive lying," where the lie is a declared art and everyone shares the frame).

**Checking.** Other elders correct genealogy and historical stories. Tall tales are not checked, since checking would break the frame.

**Grade: STRONG** for the genre-frame mechanism, where communities mark which stories are held to fact (Heath 1983; Glassie 1982; Bauman 1986). **MODERATE** for any single community's specific rules.

**On the page.**
- A grandmother who says "this one is true" before one story and nothing before the next.
- A child scolded for "telling stories."
- An old man corrected on a great-uncle's name by his sister.

### 2.10 Wakes and funerals

**Participants and status order.** Family first, then the dead person's closest friends, then the community. Clergy or the officiant hold the formal floor at the funeral, and the floor is open at the wake.

**Talk norms.** Harlow (1997) documents Irish wakes in the first half of the twentieth century as social, even festive, with practical jokes that "revived" the corpse fitting the wake's norms rather than violating them. Glassie (1982) records wake gatherings as a major occasion for storytelling in the community he studied. The general norm is that the dead are remembered through anecdote. The best-received stories show the dead as recognizably themselves, faults included, told with affection.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: characteristic anecdotes, sayings, the dead person's jokes. Taboo: open accusation of the dead in front of family, and settling the dead person's debts or feuds at the wake itself. These are deferred, not abandoned.

**Openings, closers, challenge.** Opening: condolence to the nearest kin. Closer: departure after paying respects. Challenge is muted. A disputed story is corrected gently by family ("that was his brother, actually").

**Exaggeration signals.** Virtues are enlarged. Comic anecdotes are allowed to grow.

**Outsiders.** Welcomed as mourners and introduced by their link to the dead.

**Grade: MODERATE** (Harlow 1997; Glassie 1982). The "speak no ill of the dead" norm is widely asserted but thinly documented in the sources checked here, so treat specific taboos as **CONTESTED/WEAK** outside the Irish material.

**On the page.**
- The story about the dead man that everyone has heard and asks for again.
- A widow correcting a name.
- A grievance that waits for the week after the burial.

### 2.11 Markets

**Participants and status order.** Established sellers with regular customers outrank itinerant sellers. Buyers with standing relationships outrank strangers. Geertz (1978), studying the bazaar of Sefrou, Morocco, argues that bazaar life centers on the search for information and its protection, through two procedures. *Clientelization* is building lasting buyer-seller ties so that information is trustworthy. *Bargaining* is intensive and multi-dimensional, covering quality and quantity as well as price.

**Talk norms.** Bauman (1986) studied dog traders at a Texas trade day ("any man who keeps more'n one hound'll lie to you"). Trade talk mixed real dealing with expressive lying, and participants judged one another's skill at both. Duneier (1999) shows sidewalk vendors' talk policing who belongs on a block and maintaining informal order.

**Reportable and taboo.** Reportable: prices, quality, who cheated whom, news from elsewhere. Taboo: revealing a regular's price to another customer.

**Challenge.** Testing the goods, walking away, bringing in a second opinion.

**Exaggeration.** The seller's praise is understood as a genre. Claims about quality are what get checked.

**Outsiders.** Strangers pay more and get less information until they become regulars (Geertz).

**Grade: MODERATE** (Geertz 1978; Bauman 1986; Duneier 1999).

**On the page.**
- The seller who praises everything and is believed about nothing.
- The regular whispered the real price.
- A stranger overcharged and gossiped about after.
- A trader's lie admired as craft.

| Wake/funeral | 10-100 | Moderate-high at funeral, low at wake | Kindly exaggeration tolerated; accusation deferred | Family and close friends first | Gentle family correction |
| Market | 2-5 per exchange; crowd around | Low-moderate; bargaining ritual | Praise discounted as genre; quality claims tested | Sellers, regulars; strangers least informed | Testing goods, walking away, second opinion |

**Patterns across the table (STRONG, supported by all the ethnographies above).**
1. Every setting separates two zones: **brag zones**, where exaggeration is a shared performance, and **fact zones**, where a false claim costs the teller. The boundary falls wherever a claim would change someone's standing, money, or safety.
2. Every setting has a **memory-holder** who checks claims: barkeep, barber, corner elder, the other parent, the senior hand, the veteran who was there, the elder sister, the regular trader.
3. **Audience size changes candor.** Larger audiences push talk toward performance and smaller ones toward disclosure (Curry; Coates; Shay).
4. **Outsiders get the performance version** until vouched for.

## 4. Implications by account type

These notes apply the findings to the account types used for writing about Kanja and the other protagonists. They are craft suggestions, not rules.

**Protagonist series (the protagonist's own Chronicle, close or narrated).** The protagonist's own speech should change with the setting he is in. He gives short answers at a bar where he is unknown, frank talk in a small room with comrades, and the performance register in front of a crowd. The memory-holder role is useful: a barkeep, quartermaster, or elder who remembers his earlier claims can quietly measure him. A protagonist who stays silent where others brag reads, by the veteran pattern in 2.8, as someone who was there.

**Comrade Account (a friend or crew member telling).** Comrades tell in setting-shaped ways. At a work break the story is a repeated in-joke. At a wake it is a characteristic anecdote with faults included. In a barracks-style group it is a topping sequence. The richest comrade stories carry insider detail no outsider would know, which is also the veteran's credibility test. A comrade may also withhold, telling the comic version and keeping back the part that only another witness could bear (Shay).

**Adversary Account (an enemy telling).** Adversaries often speak in fact-policed settings where saving face is at stake: a commander reporting to superiors, a defeated man in his own bar. Expect strategic minimizing ("we withdrew"), the "had to be there" defense, and the crowd pattern of claiming the win and disowning the loss (Cialdini et al.). An adversary telling among his own peers may grant the enemy more credit than he would in public, since the public-private split in Curry is a useful lever.

**Dossier (institutional record).** A dossier draws on settings it was not part of: informants' corner talk, bar gossip, market rumor. It inherits their genre markers without understanding them. A dossier that records a tall tale or a ritual insult as fact is a realistic error, the outsider misreading the frame. It can also miss the fact zone because its informant was only allowed the performance version. Labov's reportability-credibility inverse fits here: the most dramatic entries are the least reliable.

**Hearsay (secondhand, repeated).** Hearsay carries the drift of each setting it passes through. Crowd retellings inflate numbers. Wakes soften faults. Markets carry news with sellers' interest attached. Portelli's finding that oral memory reshapes dates and places to fit meaning is the key pattern: hearsay should be wrong in ways that reveal what the teller's community needed the story to mean. Labelling which setting a piece of hearsay came from ("it was told at the docks," "a crier had it") lets readers weigh it as people in those settings would.
