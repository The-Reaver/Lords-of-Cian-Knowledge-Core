A debt that isn't collected doesn't disappear. It compounds, silently, in the one ledger no one
else is ever allowed to read.

Eri Kotoko had kept that ledger for six years before the bargain that required it finally ended,
and he had not spent one entry of it early. Every insult swallowed whole. Every provocation met
with the same level, unreadable calm that had made half of Guanín call him patient and the other
half call him hollow, neither of them understanding that patience and hollowness look identical
from outside a man who has simply decided the accounting will happen on his own schedule and no
one else's.

The stranger found him on the evening the bargain's final year closed — not a celebration, Eri
Kotoko had never much cared for those, just a man alone on a dockside bench doing the last of his
arithmetic in silence before he let himself do it out loud.

"You're not from Guanín," Eri Kotoko said, without turning.

"No."

"Then you've come a long way to watch a man sit on a bench."

"I heard there was someone here who'd spent six years refusing to answer anything, and that when he
finally did, it would land exactly once and exactly where it needed to." The stranger sat at the
bench's far end, unhurried, the way a man sits when he has already decided he isn't the one this
evening belongs to. "I wanted to see if six years of restraint actually adds up to something, or
just teaches a man to stay quiet forever."

"Which would you rather it be."

"Doesn't matter to me. Only to you, and to whoever's been counting on the second answer."

Eri Kotoko almost smiled at that. Almost. "There's one man who's been counting on it more than
anyone. He measured me every day of those six years — not to see whether I'd crack, he never
believed I would. To see whether I'd forget. He thought if he pushed long enough, carefully enough,
the ledger itself would go stale in my hands, the way a debt goes uncollectible if the man who's
owed it simply stops remembering the sum." He stood, unhurried, the way he did everything. "He's
wrong about a great many things. That one most of all."

He found the man an hour later, in a counting-house that had profited, in some part, from every
year Eri Kotoko had spent unable to answer for himself — the specific kind of man who mistakes a
target's silence for a permanent condition rather than a temporary one, because the alternative
would have required him to be afraid the entire time, and men like that do not enjoy being afraid.

"You're free of it now," the man said, already reaching for the easy warmth of a peace offered too
late to mean anything. "No hard feelings, I trust. Business is business."

"It was never business. You made it personal every single day for six years, in ways small enough
that no one else in the room ever saw them land." Eri Kotoko's voice did not rise. It had never
needed to. "I saw. I counted. I have counted every day since the first one."

"And now?"

"Now the ledger closes."

What happened next took less time than the man had spent congratulating himself on having gotten
away with any of it. Eri Kotoko did not raise his voice, did not raise a fist in anger, did not
allow the moment even the small mercy of looking like rage — six years of banked weight arrived at
once, precise, deliberate, aimed with a total, surgical accuracy that left everyone else in the
counting-house untouched and the man himself understanding, in the single instant before he
understood nothing else at all, exactly which of his six years of small cruelties had finally come
due. It was over before the room had finished registering that anything had begun.

Eri Kotoko did not look at the stranger on his way out. He didn't need to; he had felt him there the
entire time, at the doorway, the same unmoving presence he'd carried since the dockside bench, adding
nothing, taking nothing, simply confirming by his stillness that what had happened had happened
exactly the way Eri Kotoko meant it to.

"You could have warned him," the stranger said, once they were both back in the open air.

"I did. Six years of not answering was the warning. He mistook it for surrender because he wanted
to." Eri Kotoko looked back once at the counting-house, then didn't again. "The men who taught me to
wait this long thought they were teaching me mercy. They were teaching me arithmetic instead. I
learned it well enough that I only ever need to be right once."

"And the rest of Guanín. The ones who called you hollow."

"They'll hear about tonight by morning. Let them decide for themselves which word fit better." A
pause, the first real weight he let show all evening. "I've spent six years being told my silence
was wisdom. I'd rather they understood, finally, that it was only ever a debt with the interest
still running."

The stranger was gone before the counting-house's night watch arrived to find what they found. He
left nothing behind that Eri Kotoko could point to and name — only, later, when Eri Kotoko told the
story to the handful of people he ever told it to in full, one sentence he found himself repeating
without remembering exactly where he'd first heard it phrased that way: *a debt that isn't collected
doesn't disappear. It only waits for the day someone finally decides to be paid.*
