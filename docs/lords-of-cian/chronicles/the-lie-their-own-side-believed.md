# The Lie Their Own Side Believed

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Crow King Alias Chronicle LIV, wave 18, closing.
Rebellion era. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named
characters.*

---

The false signal had been built, as always, for the Directorate — a fabricated troop-movement pattern
meant to pull a garrison's attention east while the real crossing happened west. It worked exactly as
intended on the garrison it was built for.

It also reached an allied militia company, operating in loose, imperfect coordination with Kanja's
crew but under its own separate command, whose own scouts intercepted the false pattern, took it for
genuine intelligence about an approaching Directorate column, and nearly moved to intercept what they
believed was an enemy force — which was, in truth, a detachment of Kanja's own people crossing the
same stretch of ground the false signal had been built to clear.

Only a last-moment recognition, one militia scout close enough to see faces rather than hear signals,
stopped an allied unit from attacking friendly forces based on a lie the crew itself had built and
aimed at someone else entirely.

"We've spent this whole craft worrying about who catches the lie," the apprentice said, once the
near-disaster had been walked back and both units stood down, shaken. "We never once built anything
for who believes it that we didn't mean to reach."

"Because it never mattered before," Kanja said. "One patrol here, one garrison there. We weren't
running signals through a stretch of country with other allied forces moving through it at the same
time. That's new. The war's gotten bigger than the craft was built for."

The militia commander, once he'd recovered his composure, put it more bluntly than either of them had
allowed themselves to. "You nearly got my people to kill your own. Not through treachery. Through
being good at your job in a direction you weren't watching."

What came out of it, over the following days, was the same instinct that had produced the
authentication phrase for imitators, turned now toward friends rather than enemies: a second,
distinct marker — quiet, unmistakable to anyone briefed to listen for it — woven into any pattern
built near ground where allied forces might also be operating, so a genuine ally intercepting a false
signal aimed at someone else would recognize it as the crew's own work and hold rather than act.

"It won't stop every mistake," Kanja said, reviewing the new protocol with the apprentice a final
time. "It'll stop this particular one."
