# The Man Who Didn't Flinch

*Locked canon, Batch 196, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-688`). Bane Alias Chronicle XXIII, wave 8 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The garrison commander at Voss Hollow had built his entire command philosophy, in the two years
since the Black Trench, around a single conviction he repeated to every officer under him: that Bane
was a story soldiers told each other to excuse losing, and that a disciplined enough command,
unwilling to believe the story, would find an ordinary man on the other end of it.

He was, in that narrow sense, not entirely wrong. He was also about to be the first commander in the
alias's run for whom the presence itself simply didn't work.

Bane came to the Hollow's gate the way he'd come to a dozen others — alone, unhurried, offering the
garrison a chance to stand down and walk rather than fight and lose. The commander met him at the
gate personally, which no one else had done, and listened to the offer without the fear that
usually crept into a man's voice as he weighed it.

"You want me to believe you've already decided how this ends," the commander said, unmoved. "I've
heard that about you. Officers report feeling it in rooms with you — that you've already finished
whatever conversation you're having somewhere they weren't invited. I don't feel that. I feel a man
at my gate making an offer I can refuse."

"You can refuse it." Bane didn't press the point, didn't raise his voice, didn't do anything that
had worked on every commander before this one. "I'd rather you didn't. Not because I need you to
believe something about me. Because refusing costs your garrison more than accepting does, and
you're the one who has to explain that cost afterward to men who trusted your judgment."

The commander refused it anyway, on principle, precisely because accepting would have meant
admitting the story worked on him the way it worked on everyone else. The garrison fought. It lost —
faster and more completely than a garrison that surrendered would have, exactly as Bane had warned —
and the commander survived it, disciplined to the last, still unconvinced afterward that anything
supernatural had happened to him at the gate. He had simply made a bad tactical decision, he
maintained, against a dangerous but ordinary opponent, and he would go on maintaining that for the
rest of his career.

"He never flinched," the officer escorting the captured garrison said afterward, still shaken
himself despite surrendering along with everyone else. "Not once. I don't understand how."

"He didn't need to believe it for it to cost him." Bane looked back once at the commander being led
away, unbroken in his own account of events even in defeat. "Some people would rather lose the fight
than admit the fight was already over before it started. I can live with that. I'd have rather he
walked his men out instead."
