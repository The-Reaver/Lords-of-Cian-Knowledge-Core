# Uhuru Chronicle II: What He Finished First

*DRAFT -- pending Abad's approval, not yet locked canon. Batch 95 candidate (`MCD-358`
candidate). Second entry in Uhuru's own Chronicles, its protagonist Ofin (`PH2-044`), not a Kanja
Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present in City Hall's late-hour orbit throughout the
night but absent from the room itself at the moment of Ofin's death -- matching `PH2-044`'s own
explicit instruction that this is "the one death in the Chicago build kept as-built, not flipped,"
and the real historical record of Harold Washington being found by his own staff. No intervention,
no resolution authorship, nothing prevented or changed. Narration is close-third on Ofin throughout,
not Onyx of Oblivion. Directly pays off `PH2-044`'s own stated capstone cost, explicitly flagged as

---

The last holdout alderman switched his vote a little past nine that night, and Ofin heard about it
the way he heard about everything now — not from the man himself, who had not spoken to him directly
in four years, but from an aide who came into the office at a half-run and then, seeing Ofin's face,
slowed to a walk for the last few steps, because some news deserved to arrive without anyone rushing
it.

"It's done," the aide said. "All of it. Every seat on that committee. He just told his own people he
was tired of losing."

Ofin sat with that for a long moment, the way he had learned, over four years of Council Wars, to sit
with almost everything — not celebrating, not yet, the way a man doesn't trust a door until he has
actually walked through it and felt the air change on the other side. "Tired of losing," he said,
finally, something that was almost a laugh living somewhere underneath the words. "Took him four
years to get tired of it. I've been tired of it since the first vote."

"You don't look tired tonight."

"I don't feel tired tonight. Ask me again in the morning." He looked at the appointment papers still
spread across the desk, years of nominations blocked and reblocked, names he had fought for one
alderman at a time until the arithmetic itself finally gave out from under the men holding it
together. "Send everyone home. Tell them it can wait until morning to be official. I just want to
sit with it a while, the way it actually is, before it becomes the way it's written down."

The stranger had been in and out of the building all night, the way he'd been in and out of most of
Ofin's nights for longer than either of them had ever remarked on — never in the room for the votes
themselves, always somewhere in the orbit of it, a quiet presence in an outer office or a hallway
that the staff had long since stopped asking about.

"You should go home too," Ofin told him, when the aide had gone and the office had finally emptied
out around the two of them. "Whatever this is between us, it doesn't need an audience for the parts
that are just paperwork."

"I don't mind the paperwork."

"I know you don't. That's not the same as it needing you here for it." Ofin was already pulling a
clean sheet toward himself, the particular unhurried motion of a man who had decided, tonight of all
nights, to write something in his own hand rather than dictate it. "Go on. I'll lock up myself. I do
most nights."

The stranger stood a moment longer than he needed to, the way he sometimes did when something in a
room asked more of his attention than the words in it accounted for, and then did as he was told, the
way he generally did with the people whose calls he'd decided, somewhere along the way, were actually
theirs to make.

Ofin wrote for a while after that — not the letter such a night deserved, nothing that grand, mostly
just names: the appointments finally freed to move, the order he wanted them announced in, a note in
the margin about the fair-housing board's funding, secure now for a second year running, that he
wanted someone to check on personally rather than trust to the usual channels. He set the pen down at
some point without finishing the last line, the way a man sets a pen down when he has simply run out
of the particular kind of tiredness that keeps a hand moving, and did not pick it back up.

His wife had been asking him for months to see a physician about the sleep, about the color he'd lost
across a year of appointment fights that should have taken half the time and hadn't, about a fatigue
that had stopped announcing itself and simply become the water he swam in. He had told her, more than
once, that he would, once the wall came down. The wall had come down four hours before he stopped
writing.

He was found at his own desk the next morning by the first aide to arrive, the appointment papers
still in order beside the unfinished note, the pen a few inches from his hand.

The stranger heard within the hour, the way news like that moves through a city's orbit whether or
not anyone thinks to send for the people who ought to hear it, and stood for a long while outside the
building he had spent so many quiet nights inside of, saying nothing to anyone who might have
recognized him, because there was nothing in him that night that belonged in a room with people who
had actually known the man by name.

Kasa heard it in Kwan before noon. Omoba heard it in Jibaro not long after. Both would say, in the
years that followed, the same thing in slightly different words: that the wall he'd spent four years
breaking had finally, completely come down, exactly the way his gift had always promised it would —
and that the engine which broke it had never once, in all those years, promised to spare the man
running it.
