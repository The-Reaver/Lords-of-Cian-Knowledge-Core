# Two Old Men and One Yard

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1823`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCIV, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2. Set strictly pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's later
adolescence (roughly age sixteen), years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard
alive, Ozmund still the House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from accounts given separately by Armsmaster Berrin Hollis (Chronicle XLI) and Sergeant
Oswin Kade (Chronicle XLV), each account contradicting the other on small points in ways Red Beard
notes rather than resolves. Gives the two men their first extended, deliberately friction-first shared
scene in the strand, following Chronicle XCI's crisis-forged cooperation with an ordinary-day account
of the institutional overlap between their two roles. No new named characters.*

---

I should say plainly that this account came to me in two versions, from two men who had by then known
each other the better part of thirty years, and that where they disagreed I've kept both disagreements
rather than pick a winner, because I came to suspect the disagreement itself was closer to the truth of
the two of them than either man's preferred version alone.

Hollis's version began with a complaint. He said the House Guard, under whichever commander preceded
Draconis at the time, had taken to pulling young guardsmen out of his own weapons instruction early —
before he considered their forms reliable — to fill out patrol rosters that were, in his professional
opinion, chronically understaffed by poor planning rather than genuine need. He said he'd raised it with
Kade directly, more than once, and gotten nothing but the same answer: that a guardsman learned more
about real combat standing a cold watch at two in the morning than he ever would in a drill yard, and
that Hollis's precious extra months of polish counted for less than Kade seemed willing to admit against
a man's actual survival in the field.

Kade's version began, tellingly, with the same complaint reversed. He said Hollis had never once served
a watch in his life, had never stood a gate at the hour when exhaustion made a man's hands slow and his
judgment worse, and had no standing, in Kade's view, to tell him how much polish a guardsman needed
before real duty taught him the rest faster and harder than any drill ever could. He said the argument
had gone on, on and off, for the better part of two years, loud enough more than once that the yard
itself went quiet around them.

The General was sixteen the year it came to a head, old enough by then to sit in on the House's own
minor governance without anyone thinking twice about it, and both men agreed, independently, that it was
his intervention that settled it — though they disagreed, characteristically, on what exactly he said.
Hollis remembered him asking a single question: how many of the guardsmen pulled early from instruction
had since been injured on duty in ways more training might plausibly have prevented. Kade remembered him
asking the mirror question: how many guardsmen kept back for extra polish had missed real duty their
fellows had to cover instead, at real cost to those fellows. Both men agreed the answer to each question,
once actually counted rather than argued from memory, turned out to be smaller than either of them had
believed.

What came out of it, by both accounts, wasn't a victory for either man. It was a staged rotation neither
had proposed on his own — new guardsmen split their early service between Hollis's yard and Kade's
actual rosters in alternating months, rather than finishing one before beginning the other, so that a
man learned the forms and the cold watch close enough together that neither could claim the other had
made him. Hollis told me he'd resented it for exactly as long as it took him to watch the first cohort
trained that way outperform every prior class he'd sent through on his own schedule. Kade told me almost
the identical thing, in almost the identical words, which struck me enough that I asked each of them,
separately, whether they'd ever noticed how alike their two accounts actually were. Both men, separately,
said no — and both, separately, seemed genuinely pleased to be told it.

Neither man ever fully stopped arguing, by their own admission. Kade said that was rather the point —
that an institution built by two men who agreed on everything would have blind spots neither of them
could see, and that thirty years of disagreeing with Hollis in that yard had made the Guard stronger
than either of their individual instincts would have, left alone. Hollis, for his part, told me only
that he'd trained a great many men over his career and argued with exactly one of them long enough to
call it something close to friendship, and that he supposed, at his age, that counted for something.
