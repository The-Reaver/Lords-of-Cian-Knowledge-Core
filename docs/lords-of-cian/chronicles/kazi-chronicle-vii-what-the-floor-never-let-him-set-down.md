# Kazi Chronicle VII: What the Floor Never Let Him Set Down

*Locked canon, Batch 288, 2026-09-12 (`MCD-1526`). Seventh entry in Kazi's own Chronicles, its fourth
with Tunji (`PH2-065`) as protagonist. Kanja's presence is minimal and peripheral, matching the
established convention — no command, no intervention, no resolution credit. Narration is close-third
on Tunji throughout. A quiet personal/domestic register showing who Tunji is outside the plant, and
the toll of a method built entirely on reading other people. Slots into no existing mainline battle —
original homage-era material set in Kazi itself.*

---

Tunji's aunt Adaeze had raised him from the age of nine, after his mother's death, in a house crowded
enough that reading a room had never been a craft he'd chosen so much as one the house itself had
required of him — six children under one roof, only one of them hers by blood, and someone had to know,
before it turned into shouting, which fight was about to matter and which would burn itself out on its
own inside the hour.

He was thirty-one now, and he still could not sit in a room without doing it.

"You're doing the thing," Adaeze said, from her chair by the window, not looking up from the mending in
her lap. She was seventy-one and her hands still moved faster than his did at anything requiring that
kind of patience.

"What thing."

"Watching me to see if I'm about to ask you for something before I've decided whether I'm going to ask
it." She set the mending down, finally, and looked at him directly, which she did rarely enough that it
still made him sit up a little straighter, thirty-one years old or not. "I raised six of you in four
rooms. I know what a person watching for weather looks like. You've had that look on your face since
you were nine years old, and you've never once, in twenty-two years, let me see you set it down."

"It's not something I know how to set down. It's just how I see a room."

"It's how you see every room. That's not the same thing." She picked the mending back up, but slower
now, giving the words room to sit before the next stitch. "You read that whole plant floor for the
company of it, and I understand why — it matters, what you do there, I've never once doubted that. But
you come home and you read this room too, the exact same way, like I might be one more grievance
you've got to decide is ripe or green before you can rest in your own chair."

Tunji said nothing for a while, because there was nothing in him ready to argue with it.

"I don't know how not to," he said finally. "I've tried. It's not a thing I do on purpose anymore. I
watch your hands when you set the mending down too fast. I watch which of my cousins goes quiet at
dinner instead of loud. I don't decide to do it. It's just what's under everything else by the time I
notice I'm doing it at all."

"I'm not asking you to stop reading me, Tunji. I don't think you could, and I'm not sure I'd want you
to — it's kept this family together longer than I managed to on my own, some years." Adaeze's voice
softened, the way it rarely did outside this one room, this one chair. "I'm asking you to notice that
it costs you something, every single time, whether the room needs it or not. You've spent your whole
life being the one who has to know first. Nobody ever asked whether that was a fair thing to ask of a
nine-year-old boy. I certainly didn't, not then. I'm asking it of you now, only because I don't think
anyone else in your life ever will."

A neighbor's boy, hired for the week to fix a step that had been loose since spring, worked quietly at
the bottom of the porch through most of the conversation, near enough to have heard all of it and near
enough that his continued, careful hammering was its own kind of answer — the sound of someone doing an
unglamorous thing well, without being watched for it, without needing to be.

"I don't know how to just sit in a room," Tunji admitted, eventually, more to himself than to her.

"Then start with this one." Adaeze nodded toward the window, the ordinary evening light through it,
nothing in the room requiring anything of him at all. "Just this one chair. Just tonight. The floor
will still be there tomorrow, needing exactly what it needs of you. It can survive one evening without
you reading it first."

He stayed in the chair a long while after that, not entirely at ease in it, but staying anyway, which
was, for a man built the way he was built, very nearly the same thing.
