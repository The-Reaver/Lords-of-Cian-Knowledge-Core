# The Toll They Couldn't Agree On

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1818`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXIX, House politics strand wave 3, tenth entry.*

---

The General told me he thought of this one, more than most of the political stories he gave me, as a
lesson he'd had to learn twice — once in the room where it happened, and a second time, years later,
turning it back over in his own memory and realizing he'd understood only half of what he'd actually
done right.

House Ashworth held no land at all in the ordinary sense, its standing built entirely on a chain of
river barges and warehouse rights along the waterway that carried a substantial share of the
Verehimu lowlands' grain to market each autumn. The relationship between the two Houses was, by long
custom, mutually necessary and mutually irritating in roughly equal measure — Verehimu needed
Ashworth's barges to move its harvest, and Ashworth needed Verehimu's harvest to justify running
them, and the toll each autumn's shipping contract set was renegotiated, with some friction, nearly
every year as river conditions, grain prices, and each House's sense of its own leverage shifted.

That year's negotiation had stalled badly before it reached Ozmund's involvement, twenty-seven by
then and increasingly trusted with matters his father judged too tedious or too fraught for his own
patience. Factor Yewen Ashworth, who conducted the House's trade dealings personally rather than
through an intermediary, had opened the season's talks with a toll increase considerably steeper than
recent years' pattern would have suggested, and House Verehimu's own negotiators — experienced men,
the General said, who'd handled a decade of these talks without serious trouble — had responded with
the kind of firm, practiced counter-pressure that had always worked before and, this year, for
reasons no one on the Verehimu side could quite identify, simply wasn't working.

The General said his father, growing impatient with the stalemate, sent him to observe a session
directly rather than resolve it, expecting little beyond a lesson in how tedious such negotiations
could become. What he noticed instead, sitting quietly through an hour of increasingly circular
argument, was that Ashworth himself said remarkably little once the opening positions had been
stated — he let his own negotiators do most of the talking, watched the Verehimu side closely, and
seemed, the General thought, less interested in winning any particular exchange than in something
else entirely, something the General couldn't yet name.

He asked, at the session's next sitting, for a private word with Ashworth alone before the formal
talks resumed — an irregular request, and one his own negotiators plainly disapproved of, but one
Ashworth accepted readily enough. Rather than open with a counter-position of his own, Ozmund did
something he told me felt, at the time, almost too simple to count as strategy at all: he asked
Ashworth directly what had actually changed since last year's contract, and then said nothing further,
and waited.

It took Ashworth some time to answer honestly, the General said, and when he finally did, the
explanation had nothing to do with river conditions or grain prices at all. Two of Ashworth's older
barges had failed structural inspection that spring, expensive enough to replace that the House's own
finances were considerably more strained than its public bearing let on, and the steep toll increase
had been Ashworth's attempt to recover that cost quietly, through a single hard negotiating position,
rather than admit the weakness directly to a trading partner he worried would use it against him in
every future season's talks. The General said he understood, hearing it, why a decade of practiced
counter-pressure from Verehimu's own side had failed to move the man: pressure only works against a
position someone is willing to abandon, and Ashworth had nowhere left to retreat to that didn't mean
admitting the truth he'd been trying to avoid.

What Ozmund offered instead wasn't the counter-pressure his own negotiators had been applying, but a
structured solution to the actual problem — a modest toll increase, smaller than Ashworth had opened
with but real, paired with a multi-year shipping commitment that let Ashworth finance the barge
replacements against guaranteed future contracts rather than a single desperate season's toll. Ashworth
accepted within the hour, visibly relieved in a way that told the General, more clearly than any words
could have, how much the man had been carrying into that negotiation that had nothing to do with
grain at all.

The General said the lesson he took from it at the time was straightforward enough: that a stalled
negotiation is very often not actually about the terms on the table, and that asking a direct question
plainly can accomplish what pressure never will. It was only years later, he told me, turning the
memory over again, that he understood the fuller version of the lesson — that Ashworth had needed, as
much as he'd needed the money, someone willing to let him admit weakness without immediately using it
against him, and that the willingness to offer that, rather than the cleverness of the eventual terms,
was the actual thing that had ended the stalemate.

addressing the counterpart's real, undisclosed motivation rather than the stated terms — a distinct
register from Chronicle XXXVIII's Dellark negotiation (`MCD-1767`), extending the "listening" lesson
established there into active, deliberate inquiry rather than passive observation. Set age
twenty-seven, strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`). Introduces two new minor named entities,
