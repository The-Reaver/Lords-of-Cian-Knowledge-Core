# The Line No Deed Could Settle

*Locked canon, Batch 257, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1179`). Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LXVIII, wave 23. A property-dispute entry -- Kanja mediates a land-boundary conflict between two civilian families through an honest survey rather than authority.*

---

Two families had claimed the same reclaimed lot above the eastern gallery since the tunnels beneath it were cleared, each holding a deed that predated the siege and each certain the other's paperwork was the forgery. It wasn't a fight between tunnel crews, and it wasn't a fight with the Directorate. It was two households who'd lost almost everything else, down to arguing over forty feet of ground neither had the money to properly build on yet, and it had already turned ugly enough that someone had put a fist through a door.

"They both want you to just tell them who's right," Garren Hask said, laying both deeds side by side on a crate. "Whichever way you say it, half the district will call it favoritism."

"Then I'm not going to say it." Kanja looked at the deeds a long moment, then set them aside entirely. "I'm going to read the ground instead. The ground doesn't lie the way paper can."

He spent two days doing what he did underground applied to open sky -- surveying old foundation lines, mortar seams, drainage cuts that predated either family's claimed ownership by decades, cross-referencing them against the district's own pre-siege registry, which neither family had thought to consult because neither trusted the registry any more than they trusted each other. What the ground actually showed was neither deed was outright false. Both had been drawn honestly, at different times, against boundary markers that had shifted twice during construction work nobody living remembered.

He didn't rule for either side. He presented the survey to both families together, plainly, and let the physical evidence do what his own judgment refused to do on its own -- split the disputed strip along the boundary the oldest verifiable foundation line actually supported, with a formal correction filed against both deeds so the next generation wouldn't inherit the same argument.

Neither family walked away fully satisfied. One got less ground than they'd hoped; the other got confirmation their neighbors hadn't lied, which cost them the grievance they'd been nursing for months.

"You didn't give either of them what they wanted," Hask said afterward.

"I gave them something true instead," Kanja said. "It doesn't feel as good in the moment. It lasts longer."
