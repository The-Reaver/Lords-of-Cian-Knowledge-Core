# The Merchant Who Changed His Trade

*Locked canon, Batch 200, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-824`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle XXXIX, wave 13 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The merchant had never once faced the Scourge directly, had never lost a ship to a raid or a hold to
a boarding party, and yet found himself, across a slow decade, entirely unable to keep his own trade
in chained cargo profitable — buyers grown skittish, insurers refusing to underwrite the risk,
partners quietly withdrawing rather than be caught associated with a business the whole strait's
economics had turned against by inches rather than force.

He requested the meeting himself, through a go-between neither side fully trusted, and arrived at the
neutral dockside tavern with none of the bravado the stories had led him to expect from anyone
desperate enough to want an audience with the man ruining him.

"I'm not here to fight you," the merchant said, hands visibly empty on the table between them. "I
don't think I could, and I don't think it would fix anything if I somehow did. I'm here because
you've made this trade too expensive to survive in, one lost buyer and one nervous partner at a time,
and I'd rather ask you what comes next than wait to find out the hard way."

"What makes you think I have an answer for you."

"Because everyone who's tried to keep doing what I do is failing the same slow way, and everyone
who's quietly switched to something else seems to be doing fine. That's not coincidence. That's a
trade you're strangling on purpose, patiently, and I'd rather learn to read the pattern than keep
losing to it."

He studied the man a long moment, genuinely uncertain, at first, whether this was sincerity or a
longer game — and found, weighing the merchant's account against everything the crew's own ledgers
already knew of him, no evidence of the latter.

"Dyed cloth," he said finally. "The demand's real and growing, three ports along this strait can't
keep pace with orders, and none of your old buyers would think twice about the switch. You already
have the ships and the crews. You'd just be moving cargo that doesn't need chains to be worth
carrying."

The merchant took the advice, and within two years had rebuilt a fortune on dyed cloth larger than
the one chained cargo had ever given him — not converted by force or confrontation, but by economics
that had made his old trade unsustainable long before any of them ever sat down together, and a
willingness, rare among his former peers, to read the writing honestly rather than fight it to the
end.
