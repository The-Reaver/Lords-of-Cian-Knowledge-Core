# The Exhibition They Wanted Him to Perform

*Locked canon, Batch 257, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1186`). Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LXXV, wave 25, closing the wave. Kanja refuses a staged morale demonstration and replaces it with a real repair job performed publicly instead -- authenticity against propaganda.*

---

The resistance's own information office wanted a show. Recruitment had slowed in the eastern districts, and someone with a printing press and good intentions had decided that a public demonstration of the Blue-Collar Titan's full Trinity capability -- something dramatic, something people could tell their neighbors about -- would move more names onto the enlistment rolls than another pamphlet ever would.

"We'll stage it against something safe," the office's coordinator explained, spreading a proposed script across the table. "A rock face nobody needs cleared. Obsidian Malice at full discharge. It photographs well."

"It's a lie dressed as a demonstration," Kanja said, not unkindly, but flatly enough that the coordinator stopped talking. "I've spent this entire war refusing to fabricate things for causes I actually believe in. I'm not starting now because the cause is my own reputation instead of someone else's."

The coordinator pushed harder -- people needed to believe the war could be won, and belief sometimes needed spectacle more than it needed accuracy. Kanja didn't dispute that people needed hope. He disputed that hope required a performance built on nothing real.

So he offered a different show entirely. A collapsed aqueduct feeding drinking water to four thousand people sat unrepaired three streets from where the exhibition was supposed to happen, genuinely broken, genuinely useful to fix, genuinely difficult enough that failure was possible and visible if it came. He did the actual repair in public, at the hour the staged demonstration had been scheduled for, with no rehearsal and no guaranteed outcome written into anyone's script.

It took longer than a staged strike against an empty rock face would have. It also produced clean water running through four thousand households within the hour, watched by a crowd that had come expecting theater and left having seen labor instead.

"That wasn't the spectacle we asked for," the coordinator admitted afterward, watching people fill jugs at a spigot that hadn't worked in weeks.

"No," Kanja agreed. "It was better. Nobody has to take my word that it happened. They're drinking the proof."

Enlistment in the eastern districts rose that week regardless -- not because anyone had been dazzled, people said later, but because the water finally ran.
