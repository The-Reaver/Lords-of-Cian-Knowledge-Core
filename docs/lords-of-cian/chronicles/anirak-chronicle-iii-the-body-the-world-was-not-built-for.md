# Anirak Chronicle III: The Body the World Was Not Built For

*UNLOCKED DRAFT, PENDING ABAD'S APPROVAL, 2026-10-04. Third entry of Anirak's Character Chronicle
series (Game Plan pitch 1, locked at Batch 372), closing the launch wave. Close-third on Anirak per
`VB-065`. The late Long Mask, roughly Kanja 300. Ren (Abyss, Ren Oshaal) comes aboard an adult,
already recruited by Sephtis, and she takes him as her charge unasked, the undated origin of their
pairing (`CC-114`). Notable register, no kill, no fight. No new named characters. Anirak, Ren, Edda,
Hamund, and Odile reused; Sephtis and the Captain referred to, not on the page. No child-safety

He came aboard on the morning launch, in fog.

She felt the launch before she saw it. A small hull riding low. Lower than six oarsmen and a cargo of
water casks should ride it. As if one more cask sat in the bottom of it, a cask of lead.

The launch came alongside. The oarsmen shipped their oars fast and kept to the far gunwale, all six of
them, crowded together on the one side so the boat listed. In the stern, a young man sat alone with a
sea-bag on his knees.

Hamund, at the rail beside her, said it under his breath. "Sephtis's."

Word had come the week before. Sephtis had found someone on the shelf islands. Sephtis was sending him
out.

The young man came up the side ladder. The rungs creaked under him. Every one. The ladder was oak and
had held men in full plate. It creaked under him as if he were three.

He stepped onto the deck.

The planks groaned. A long, low sound, deep in the wood, the sound a hull makes in a heavy sea. The
deck was flat calm.

The men nearest the ladder stepped back. Not far. A few paces. They did not mean anything by it. Their
bodies did it for them. Something in the air around him leaned on them, and they leaned away.

He saw them do it. His face did not change. He had seen it before.

• • •

They gave him a berth in the forward hold, alone, near the keel where the timbers were thickest. The
bosun did that. Nobody told him to.

By the end of the first day there was a shape on the deck that followed the young man everywhere.

A ring. Ten paces across. Empty.

Where he walked, the ring walked with him. Men coiling line drifted out of it. Men at the pumps found
reasons to stand at the other pump. At the evening meal the crew sat close along the benches amidships,
elbow to elbow, and he took his bowl to the bow and sat on the anchor housing with the ring around him
and the dark coming on.

The planks under the anchor housing groaned all through the meal. Not loud. Steady. A ship talking to
itself about a weight.

His name was Ren. Somebody had heard it from the launch crew.

• • •

She watched him for three days.

The young man ate alone. Slept alone. Worked the pumps alone, at the hours nobody else took. He
moved through the ship slowly and set his feet down carefully, the way a man walks on ice. He never
went below the waist deck. Somebody had told him the lower timbers were older.

He did not complain. He did not ask anyone to stand nearer. He did not ask for anything.

Her three watched her watch him. Edda said nothing. Odile said nothing. Hamund said, on the second
night, "They're afraid of him."

"No. They're heavy near him."

"Same thing, to a body."

She had no answer to that.

On the third evening he looked up from the bow. Across the waist of the ship, across the benches and
the crew between them, to where she stood at the mainmast.

His face turned to her and stayed.

The same as every face. The pull went out of her eyes and into him, and the little that came back came
back the way it always did, and she could not tell what in it was his.

He looked away. It took him a moment. She watched him do it.

• • •

She walked across the deck to the bow.

The crew on the benches turned to watch her go, all of them, the way they always did. She went past
the end of the benches. Past the foremast. To the edge of the ring.

She stepped into it.

The weight came down on her at once. A load, flat and even. As if the air had grown a hand and set it flat
on her shoulders and pressed. Her knees took it. Her spine took it. The chains on her forearms went
heavier by a third and hung straight down, swinging less. The plank under her boot gave a groan of its
own.

She took another step. Then another. Each one set down harder than the one before it. Her heart rose a
little with the work of it.

He was watching her come. She saw his face. She saw the moment she crossed into the weight, because
something in his face went.

The pull went out of it.

It was the only word she had. He had been looking at her the way everyone looked at her. Fixed. Held.
Then she was inside the ring, under the weight, three paces from him, and he was only looking at her.
His eyes moved. Down to the chains. To her boots on the groaning planks. Back to her face. Off to the
sea, once, and back again, because he chose to bring them back.

She stood very still a moment and watched him do that.

Nobody had ever looked away from her and then come back.

• • •

"You'll want to step back," he said. Quiet. He did not get up. "It is heavy, this close."

"Yes."

"Most people step back."

"I saw."

She sat down on the deck beside the anchor housing, an arm's length from him. The weight sat down with
her. It pressed her into the planks. The planks groaned under the two of them together, one long low
note.

He looked at her for a while. She let him. She had nothing to measure it against.

"Does it hurt you?" he said.

"It's heavy."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the answer."

He almost smiled. Then didn't. He looked down at the bowl in his hands.

"At home they built the walls thicker for me," he said. "Then thicker again. Then there wasn't any wall
thick enough."

She said nothing. She did not ask where home was. He did not ask her anything at all.

Out on the benches, the crew had gone back to their bowls. Her three sat at the end of the nearest
bench, outside the ring, the way everyone sat outside the ring. Hamund was watching. He caught her eye
and held it, and his face did what every face did, and she looked away from him first.

• • •

She ate at the bow after that.

Every evening. Inside the ring. The weight on her shoulders through the whole meal, and the planks
groaning their low note under two bowls instead of one. She did not explain it to anyone. Nobody asked
her to.

In the mornings she worked the pumps at his hours. She walked the waist deck with him when he walked
it, on the side nearest the crew, so that the men coiling line looked at her instead of at the empty
space around him. Faces turned to her and stayed. That was the use of it. A ring that walks with a
woman everyone watches is a ring nobody counts.

On the fifth day a deckhand came too close behind him with a cask on his shoulder, misjudged the
weight, and went to one knee in it, the cask rolling. The man swore at Ren. Loud. The whole waist heard.

She was there before the cask stopped rolling. She did not touch the deckhand. She stood between them,
inside the weight, with the chains hanging heavy at her knees, and looked at him.

His face turned to her and stayed.

"Go round," she said.

He went round. He did it wide.

Ren had not moved. He was looking at her. She knew the difference now. She could feel it, the way she
felt a hull in the dark. Inside the ring, his look was only his.

"I didn't ask you to do that," he said.

"No."

He waited. She did not give him more.

• • •

On the ninth day, word came forward from the stern. The Captain wanted the young man's measure. Feet.
Chest. The weight of him. There would be boots. A vest.

Ren listened to the bosun say it from the edge of the ring. Then he looked at her.

"Boots," he said.

"He builds for the body you have," she said.

He thought about that for a long time, in the stillness that she was learning was the way he counted.

"How do you know?"

She held up one wrist. The chain ran from the cuff to the Fang that hung at her knee, and the links
were warm from her pulse, and heavier than they should be, inside the weight.

He looked at it. Then at her face. Then away to the sea, and back.

That night she lay in her hammock and listened to the ship. The groan in the forward timbers went on
past midnight, low and even. A weight the hull had learned to carry.

She fell asleep counting it.
