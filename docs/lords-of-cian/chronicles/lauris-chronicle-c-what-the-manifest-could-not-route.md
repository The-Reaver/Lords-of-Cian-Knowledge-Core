# Lauris Chronicle C: What the Manifest Could Not Route

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1717`). One-hundredth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, Strand W. A stakes-free entry pairing Lauris with Cooper (Ronan Kellsward,
`CC-103`), the crew's logistics specialist -- she helps him solve a supply-cache routing problem
the Manifest could not close through arithmetic alone, using her own capacity rather than a tally,
a deliberate variation on Chronicle LV's ledger-reconciliation evening with Garren Hask: a physical
problem rather than a bookkeeping one. Puts `CC-134`'s combat-joy trait on the page as satisfaction
in a hard, practical puzzle solved by testing rather than counting. No new named characters.*

-----

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Ronan had a route that would not close on
paper. I offered to close it with my own back instead. He looked at me as though I had misunderstood
the problem. I had not."*

I mark this entry's number with a certain private amusement, since Ronan Kellsward is, of every
member of this crew, the one whose own professional instinct is most naturally suited to appreciate
a round figure, and I suspect he would tell me, if asked, that the hundredth entry landing on him
specifically is either a fine coincidence or proof that someone somewhere keeps a better count than
even he does. I have not told him it was neither, precisely, only the order in which these accounts
happened to fall.

The Manifest -- the crew's master supply ledger, which Ronan maintains with the same close attention
his own biology gives to absorbed mass, is not a record so much as a living architecture, and I am
told it had, for the better part of a week before this account, refused to close on a single problem:
a cache route through a stretch of collapsed terrain where every calculated path required either a
load-bearing capacity the terrain itself could not support, or a detour long enough to spoil the
perishable third of the cargo before it arrived. He had recalculated the route eleven times. It had
failed to close eleven times.

Lauris found him at the twelfth attempt, staring at a diagram of the terrain with the specific,
weary stillness of a man whose own considerable gift -- absorbing external mass through sustained
contact, raising his density well past anything his resting frame would suggest -- was, for once, not
the relevant tool for the problem in front of him.

"You are trying to solve this with numbers," she observed.

"It is a numbers problem," he said, not unkindly, in the tone of a man explaining something to
someone he assumes has not looked closely enough at it.

"It is a numbers problem you have already solved eleven times," she said. "It has failed eleven
times regardless. I would suggest, respectfully, that the twelfth attempt at the same method is
unlikely to succeed where the first eleven did not."

What she proposed instead, and what he initially regarded, by his own later account, as her having
simply misunderstood the terrain diagram, was considerably more direct: rather than calculate whether
a given point on the collapsed ground could bear a laden cart's weight, she would carry the load
across the weakest sections herself, on her own back, and let the route be defined by where a body
capable of six thousand years' accumulated certainty about her own limits could actually stand,
rather than by what an engineer's formula, built for ordinary loads, presumed the ground could bear.

It took him, he told me afterward, a full minute to understand she meant this literally, and a
further several seconds to decide he had no actual objection to it beyond the mild professional
indignity of watching a routing problem solved by main strength rather than by the Manifest's own
elegant machinery. She spent the better part of an afternoon crossing and recrossing the collapsed
stretch with increasing loads, testing each questionable span herself before committing the route to
his ledger, and by evening the twelfth attempt closed cleanly -- not because the numbers had finally
cooperated, but because the numbers had, in the end, never been the actual obstacle.

"You enjoyed that," he said, watching her set down the final load without so much as a change in her
breathing, in the tone of a man noticing something he had not expected to notice.

"I did," she said. "I do not often get to solve a problem with my own hands rather than an
argument. I have found I miss it, when I go too long without one."

He told me later, once the route was recorded and the cargo safely moving along it, that he had
recruited to this crew specifically because his own gift for logistics was worth more to Anansi than
his gift for combat, and that he had spent a great many years since quietly proud of being useful in
a register no one particularly celebrates. He said he had not expected, watching her cross that
ground a dozen times without complaint or hurry, to feel that pride sharpen rather than diminish --
that watching someone else take genuine, unhurried satisfaction in solving his own stubborn problem
made the problem, and the solving of it, feel less like a chore and more, for one afternoon, like
the kind of work worth doing for its own sake.

The route remains in the Manifest, marked in his own hand: *Closed by testing, not by arithmetic.
Recommend this method whenever arithmetic alone will not.*
