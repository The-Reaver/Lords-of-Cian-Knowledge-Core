# The Frequency He Read Wrong

*Locked canon, Batch 158, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-497`). The Iron Bastard Alias Chronicle X, first entry
in the fourth wave. Rebellion era, age 25. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material — the
first genuine misdiagnosis entry in this alias's run. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new
named characters.*

---

The support tower's resonance read clean on Kanja's first diagnostic pass, the pattern matching
every structure he'd correctly diagnosed before it — except a hidden repair, an old patch job buried
beneath decades of later construction, carried a second, competing tension the diagnostic listening
hadn't accounted for. When Obsidian Malice discharged against what should have been a controlled,
targeted collapse, the tower came down wrong — a wider, less contained failure than intended,
catching two Directorate engineers who hadn't been the target at all.

Both survived. Neither escaped without injury.

"The doctrine's never failed like this before," the crew's own field medic said, treating the
engineers alongside Directorate personnel who'd arrived to do the same, an uneasy shared effort in
the wreckage's aftermath. "What happened?"

"I read the tower's surface tension correctly. I didn't account for what was buried underneath it —
an old repair nobody currently working the structure would have known was there." Kanja didn't excuse
the outcome, didn't minimize what the misdiagnosis had cost. "I've built this entire doctrine on the
claim that listening carefully enough tells you the truth about a structure. Today it told me a
partial truth, and I treated it as the whole truth. Two people are hurt because of that gap."

"Every method has a margin of error. This is well within what anyone would call an acceptable
failure rate for something this precise."

"Maybe. That doesn't make the margin easier to accept when it lands on two specific people instead of
staying an abstract statistic." Kanja stayed to help with the wounded personally rather than
withdraw once the engagement's tactical purpose was achieved, an unusual choice that both sides
noted. "I intend to change how I diagnose from here forward — deeper listening, more time spent before
any discharge, even when time is exactly what a battlefield rarely offers. I'd rather be slower and
more certain than fast and wrong again."

The correction cost him speed in every subsequent engagement, a real and lasting trade against the
doctrine's own effectiveness — accepted deliberately, the price of having been wrong once in a way
that mattered.
