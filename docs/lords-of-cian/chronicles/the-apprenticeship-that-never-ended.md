# The Apprenticeship That Never Ended

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1149`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXV, wave 22. The method's first apprenticeship-contract case, a legal category distinct from simple wage labor -- a bonded craft apprentice kept years past any craft still being taught.*

---

The boy — not a boy any longer, though the contract still called him one — had signed his apprenticeship papers at eleven, thumbprinted by a father who couldn't read them, for a seven-year term learning wheelwright's work under a master who took government subsidy for every year he kept an apprentice on the books. He was nineteen now. He hadn't been taught anything new in four years. He hauled stock, swept the yard, and ran the master's personal errands, and every year the master renewed the apprenticeship on paper rather than converting him to a paid journeyman, because an apprentice cost nothing but his keep and a journeyman cost wages.

Ezio had never worked a case like it. Wage theft had a number attached — hours, rate, shortfall, a subtraction anyone could check. This wasn't theft in that shape. Every year of the boy's contract was technically legal, technically renewable at the master's discretion, technically defensible in front of any magistrate who only read the paper and not the yard. What made it theft was invisible on the page: four years of a craft not being taught, measured only in what the boy still couldn't do that a real four-year apprentice would have learned twice over.

"The contract's clean," Ezio said, laying it flat. "There's no number on this page that's wrong."

"Then the number's not on that page." Kanja had him bring in three other wheelwrights from neighboring yards, working masters with nothing to gain either way, and asked each the same question separately: what should a boy four years into a wheelwright apprenticeship be able to build unsupervised. All three answered within the same narrow range. None of it matched what the boy could actually do, which the boy demonstrated himself, honestly, in front of all three — a spoke he set wrong, a hub he couldn't true without correction, work four years behind where it should have stood.

That comparison became the finding: not a wage figure, but a craft-progress deficit, cross-referenced against the government subsidy record itself, which had paid the master for four straight years of instruction a competent tribunal of his own peers said plainly hadn't happened. The subsidy fraud alone was enough to end the arrangement outright — the master lost his license to hold apprentices at all, a penalty with more teeth than any back-wage settlement Ezio had ever written. The boy left with something the ledger had never had to hand anyone before: not owed coin, but a formal release from a contract that no longer bound him, and a signed account from three working masters attesting to exactly what craft he'd need to catch up before any guild would call him competent.

"We've never fixed a case that didn't end in a number," Ezio said afterward, still turning the unfamiliar shape of it over.

"It ended in a number," Kanja said. "Just not one you write in coin. Four years is four years, whichever way you were owed them."
