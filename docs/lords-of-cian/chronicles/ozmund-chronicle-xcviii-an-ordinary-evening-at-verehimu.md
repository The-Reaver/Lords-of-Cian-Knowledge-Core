# An Ordinary Evening at Verehimu

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1827`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCVIII, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2. Set strictly pre-Book-1, spanning no single

Not everything I have of those years came to me as a single story with a single shape, and I've come to
think this one — if it can even be called one — is worth setting down exactly as it arrived: not as an
event, because nothing in it happened, but as a kind of composite evening built from five people's
separate, unremarkable habits, none of whom thought to connect their own small routine to anyone else's
until I asked them to, decades later, in five separate rooms.

Hollis walked the training yard alone most evenings after the last student had gone, not drilling,
simply checking the ground the way a man checks a thing he's responsible for whether or not anyone's
watching him do it — a fence post worked loose, a patch of earth gone soft with the last rain, small
maintenances that kept the next morning's session from starting with a surprise. He told me he couldn't
say how many thousand evenings he'd spent that way over a career, only that he didn't remember any single
one of them, which he said was itself the point of a well-run yard — that the evenings worth remembering
were the ones where something had gone wrong.

Fairweather closed the mews at roughly the same hour, further across the grounds, checking each bird
down for the night in an order she'd long since stopped having to think about — weight, temperament,
whichever bird had had the harder day getting settled last. She told me she used to be able to hear
Hollis's own boots on the yard gravel from the mews door on a still evening, and that she'd come, without
ever remarking on it to him directly, to find the sound of it faintly reassuring, proof the House was
being looked after by someone even when nothing needed looking after at all.

Fenmoor worked later than either of them, by candlelight in a study that never seemed to run short of
unfinished translations, and told me he'd long ago stopped noticing what hour it actually was until his
own eyes told him plainly to stop. He said he liked working in that particular stretch of the evening,
after the household's daytime noise had settled and before anyone needed anything urgent from him,
because it was the one part of his day that belonged entirely to the work itself rather than to whoever's
question he was answering.

Kade stood or sat near the gate most such evenings, not always on formal duty, sometimes simply present
the way an old soldier is present near the thing he's spent his life guarding even on the nights no one
requires it of him. He told me he liked that hour for the same reason Fairweather liked the sound of
Hollis's boots — it told him, without anyone having to report it to him directly, that the House was
settled for the night.

Cobb, in the years before the courier post took him elsewhere, finished bedding the last horses down at
roughly the same hour the others were finishing their own small rounds, and told the General once,
which the General in turn told me, that he liked the stable at that particular time of evening better
than at any other — quiet enough to hear each horse settle, dark enough that the work became mostly
feel rather than sight, and near enough to the House proper that he could sometimes hear, faintly, five
different people going about five different unremarkable routines, none of them thinking of it as
anything but an ordinary end to an ordinary day.

I never found a single account of all five of them in the same place on any one such evening, and I
don't believe there ever was one — that isn't the shape this particular truth takes. What I found
instead, piecing five separate memories together, was a House that held itself together not through any
single dramatic act any of them could point to, but through an unremarkable, overlapping, almost
accidental rhythm of ordinary people quietly finishing their own small piece of the day's work, each
one unaware how much the others' small pieces mattered to his own. I put it to the General once, much
later, whether he'd ever thought of those years that way — as a kind of chorus he'd grown up inside
without ever quite hearing it as one. He was quiet a long while before he answered, and when he did, he
said only that he supposed that was as good a description as any of what he'd spent the rest of his life
trying, in far harder circumstances, to build again.
