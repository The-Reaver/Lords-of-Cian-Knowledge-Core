# The Cousin Who Smiled Too Easily

The General told this one the way a man tells a story he's a little embarrassed still remembers. Not
grimly. Almost fondly, the way you'd talk about a splinter you dug out of your palm as a boy and can
still, if you press the old scar, feel exactly where it went in.

It was the confirmation of the Aldenmoor holding — a strip of upland pasture and one drafty hunting
lodge, worth less in Scrip than the feast House Verehimu threw to mark it changing hands officially
from a life-tenancy into Ozmund's own name. The land itself had never been in serious dispute. It had
sat, for three generations, under an informal understanding that the junior branch — Cassius's
branch — would hold it in trust until the senior line had an heir old enough to be given something of
his own to manage. Ozmund was that heir now, seventeen and newly trusted with his first real
administrative charge, and the lodge was simply the House's way of saying so out loud, with witnesses,
over wine.

Cassius came to the feast smiling, which the General said was the thing to understand about him
before anything else: he was, in every room, the most agreeable man in it. He toasted his cousin
first and loudest. He told a story about Aldenmoor's terrible plumbing that made the whole hall laugh,
including Ozmund, and he meant every word of the affection in it, or near enough that no one present
could have told the difference, least of all Ozmund at seventeen.

It was afterward, in the low half hour when the formal part of the evening had ended and people had
started drifting toward the doors, that Cassius found him again — alone this time, a cup of something
stronger than wine in his hand — and said, still smiling, still perfectly pleasant, that it must be a
fine thing, to be handed a lodge at seventeen for doing nothing but being born in the right room.
He said it lightly. He said it the way a man says something he has decided in advance he will be
allowed to deny meaning, if it's ever repeated back to him. And then he laughed, and clapped Ozmund
on the shoulder, and asked after his father's health, and by the time he'd finished the second
question it was as though the first had never been spoken.

The General said what stayed with him wasn't the remark itself — he'd have forgotten a plainer insult
inside the week. It was that Cassius had managed, in one breath, to be entirely truthful and entirely
unfair at the same time. Ozmund *had* been born in the right room. He knew that better than anyone
alive, having spent every year since infancy learning exactly what else he'd been born with that no
one else in that hall carried, and no ledger of land or lodges would ever come close to balancing it.
Cassius wasn't wrong. He was simply pointing at the one door in the whole House that had, in fact,
opened for Ozmund without him lifting a hand — the door Cassius could see — while having no idea at
all what stood behind the doors he couldn't.

There was no confrontation. The General was careful to tell me that, more than once, as if he worried
I'd shape it into something it wasn't. He didn't correct his cousin. He didn't defend himself, or
explain, or even privately resent the man, not in any way he could still name decades later. What he
remembered instead — what he said actually sat with him, on the ride home and for longer than he
expected — was smaller and stranger than resentment: the particular discomfort of being envied for
the wrong reason, by a man who would never once guess the right one, and of understanding, dimly, at
seventeen, that some kinds of loneliness don't come from what people take from you. They come from
what they assume about you, cheerfully, while smiling, and never think to ask.

He said his father noticed nothing wrong that night, because there was nothing to notice. Aethelgard
thanked Cassius warmly for his toast on the ride home and called him, not for the first time, "the
easiest man in the family to have in a room." The General agreed with him. He still agreed, telling
it to me all those years later — Cassius was easy. That was never the trouble. The trouble, if it was
trouble at all and not simply the ordinary weather of a large family with an unequal history, was that
easy and honest had turned out, that one evening, not to be the same thing, and no one but Ozmund had
any particular reason to have noticed the difference.
