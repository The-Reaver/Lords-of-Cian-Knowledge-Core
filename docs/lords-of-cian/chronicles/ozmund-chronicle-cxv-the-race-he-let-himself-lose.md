# The Race He Let Himself Lose

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1844`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CXV, second entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave, set strictly pre-
Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. A rare entry of genuine, unguarded joy
— young Ozmund and stable boy Cobb, a real peer without power (`MCD-1772`), in an ordinary afternoon
that has nothing to do with discipline, inheritance, or restraint at all. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn
Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. Reuses Cobb, already-locked minor named character. No new named
characters.*

---

Not every story he gave me needed excavating for its meaning, and I want this one to stand as exactly
what it was, because I think the General would have wanted that too — he told it to me laughing, which
was rare enough on its own that I remember the sound of it more clearly than half the words.

Cobb was a stable boy at the House seat, a year or so younger than Ozmund and entirely unimpressed by
whatever a lord's son was supposed to be, which the General told me was precisely why he'd liked him
from the first week they'd known each other. Cobb did not know how to be careful around him, because
Cobb did not know there was anything about him that required care. He knew a boy who was, by his own
account, a fair hand with a currycomb and a genuinely terrible judge of horses, and who could be talked,
with almost no effort at all, into racing the length of the lower paddock any time the grooms weren't
looking.

The General told me he lost that race more often than he won it, and that for a long while he assumed
this was simply the truth of things — that Cobb, half his weight and built low and quick, was faster
over open ground than he was, and there was no more to it than that. It was some years before he admitted
to me, still half-laughing at his own younger self, that this had never entirely been true. He had held
back. Of course he had held back — holding back was, by then, so constant a habit that he had stopped
noticing himself doing it in half the places it mattered, and this was one of them: a boy who had spent
years learning never to be certain how much of himself he was withholding in a wrestling match had
carried the same caution, without meaning to, into a footrace that could not possibly have hurt anyone
no matter how hard he ran it.

What changed it, he told me, was an afternoon in high summer when Cobb beat him for the fourth time
running and declared himself, with enormous and entirely unearned confidence, the fastest boy at the
House seat and possibly in the whole of the Wetlands besides — and something about the sheer, cheerful
absurdity of the claim undid, for exactly the length of one race, every careful habit he'd built around
himself. He told me he simply ran. Not carefully, not with any part of his attention spent monitoring
what he was or wasn't allowing through — he ran the way Cobb ran, without calculation, because for once
the only thing at stake was whether a boastful stable hand would have to eat his own words in front of
two grooms who'd stopped pretending not to watch.

He won by a margin that made Cobb laugh so hard he had to sit down in the paddock grass, and the General
told me that the laughing was the part he remembered best of all — not his own laughter, though he'd had
that too, but Cobb's, doubled over and wheezing and entirely without malice, already composing the story
of his own defeat into something he'd apparently be telling for weeks. Neither of them made anything of
the margin. That, the General said, was the whole gift of it: Cobb had no framework in which a lord's
son suddenly running faster than expected meant anything at all beyond a lucky day and a longer stride,
and so it cost Ozmund nothing to have let it show. There was no secret in that paddock large enough for
anyone watching to notice, because no one watching was looking for one.

He told me he went back to holding himself carefully the very next race, and every race after that, for
years — that this was not a lesson that undid the discipline, and he was careful that I understand it
that way, because it would be easy to tell the story as though one afternoon of running free had taught
him something he then carried forward. It hadn't, not directly. What it had taught him, he said, only
became clear much later: that the careful version of himself was a choice he was making, over and over,
and not simply the only shape available to him — because he had, once, for the length of one footrace
in the summer grass, chosen otherwise, and the world had not ended, and a stable boy had laughed himself
breathless in the dirt, and nothing at all had been lost by it. He told me it was good, sometimes, simply
to know that the door was there. He did not have to walk through it often for it to matter that it opened.
