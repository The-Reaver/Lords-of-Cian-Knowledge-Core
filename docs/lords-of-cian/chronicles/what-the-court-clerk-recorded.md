# What the Court Clerk Recorded

*Locked canon, Batch 194, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-647`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle XLII, wave 14 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The Canal House inquest's official record, filed and sealed within the Trust's own archive, ran to
several hundred pages of formal transcription — every question, every figure entered into evidence,
every non-committal pause from the three adjudicators recorded in the flat, procedural language the
Trust required of its clerks.

It was not the clerk's job to editorialize. She did it anyway, in a margin nobody but a future
archivist would ever likely read, in handwriting small enough that a casual review would miss it
entirely.

*The figures held up under six hours of hostile examination without a single discrepancy surfacing.
I have transcribed inquests into genuine fraud before. They do not sound like this one sounded. A
guilty man's numbers develop small inconsistencies under sustained pressure, minor evasions in
phrasing, moments where the questioning finds an edge and the answer shifts to avoid it. This
inquest found no edge to find, six hours running, and I do not believe that was luck.*

She had never spoken to Kanja directly, would likely never have reason to, and had no obligation
whatsoever to record anything beyond the transcript itself. She did it, she would have said if
anyone had asked, because the formal record — accurate in every particular, careful to preserve
exactly what was said and by whom — nonetheless left out the thing she had actually noticed sitting
in that room for six hours: that whatever the tribunal officially concluded, every person present
had watched something considerably more convincing than a non-finding, and none of the official
language would ever capture that it had happened.

The margin note was never meant to leave the archive. It sat, sealed with the rest of the inquest
record, unread by anyone beyond the archivists who would eventually catalog it, a small, private
crack in an otherwise perfectly neutral institutional document — one clerk's honest observation,
kept quietly, that even the machinery built to find fault with him had come away, in its own
unofficial corners, convinced.
