# The Grain That Never Reached the Garrison

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Bane Alias Chronicle XXXV, second entry in the
twelfth wave. Rebellion era, age 19-20, roughly a year after the Black Trench. Not a territory
Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The Kessic Wardline garrison held four hundred men on a supply schedule that left no room for
interruption, and Bane had learned enough, by then, not to spend lives storming a position that
could simply be starved into irrelevance instead. The operation that broke it took eleven weeks and
never involved a single engagement.

It started with the grain convoys themselves — three routes, all of them running through territory
where the rebellion had quiet sympathy among the farmers and haulers who worked the roads. Bane
didn't seize the grain, didn't burn it, didn't do anything that would have registered as sabotage
worth reporting up a chain of command. He simply arranged, through patient, unhurried conversation
with people who had their own long-standing grievances against the requisition rates the garrison's
supply officers had been quietly padding for years, for the convoys to run slower. A broken axle
here. A dispute over payment there. A route "closed" for repairs that took a week longer than any
repair should have.

"None of it's an attack," Garren Hask observed, tracking the convoy delays against the garrison's
own increasingly desperate requisition orders. "They can't even report it as one. Every single delay
has an honest excuse behind it."

"That's the whole point. An attack gives them something to respond to. This gives them nothing to
respond to at all — just a slow, ordinary, deniable erosion of a supply line that was never as secure
as they assumed it was." Bane reviewed the latest intercepted requisition order, its tone shifting
from routine to visibly anxious over eleven weeks of documented paperwork. "Four hundred men don't
need to be beaten. They need to run out of grain slowly enough that surrendering starts looking like
the reasonable choice instead of the humiliating one."

It worked exactly that way. The garrison's commander opened negotiations himself, in the ninth week,
without a shot fired or a wall breached, offering terms that would have cost the rebellion
considerably more to win by force. Four hundred men walked out under their own power, fed one last
time by the rebellion's own stores, no casualties on either side.

"Eleven weeks for four hundred men and not a single death," Hask said, closing out the account. "I
don't know that anyone will ever tell this one as a story."

"Good. I'd rather it stayed exactly that boring." Bane watched the last of the garrison's men file
out, unarmed and unharmed. "The stories are for the ones I couldn't do this way."
