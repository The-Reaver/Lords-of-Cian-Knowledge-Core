# Lauris Chronicle XCVII: The Condition His Index Had No Page For

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1714`). Ninety-seventh entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of a third Strand W (Witness / present-day, quiet register) push. A
stakes-free entry pairing Lauris with Damu (Julian Dael-Koss, `CC-100`), the crew's blood-sense
medic -- their paths have crossed for years without either ever having professional cause to sit
down together, since Lauris has never once required his medical attention. Puts `CC-134`'s
combat-joy trait on the page as the pleasure of being read accurately, by choice, for the first
time. No new named characters.*

-----

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Julian asked whether he could read me,
purely for his own record. I have been read many times by many instruments, none of them asking
first. I said yes before he finished the question."*

I record this account with a certain private satisfaction, since it concerns two people who have
shared a crew, a war, and roughly a century and a half of proximity without once having occasion to
speak at length to one another, for the entirely mundane reason that Lauris has never been
sufficiently injured, ill, or poisoned to require Julian Dael-Koss's attention, and Julian has never
had cause to seek her out for anything beyond the ordinary courtesies of shared quarters. It took, in
the end, nothing more than his own idle curiosity to correct this.

He found her in the archive wing one evening, transcribing, and asked -- carefully, in the manner of
a man who has spent a hundred and fifty years learning that a body's consent to be read is not a
formality but the entire foundation of his craft -- whether she would permit him to take a reading of
her, not for any diagnostic purpose, but because his Red Index, which he has told me privately he
considers the truest thing he owns, has never once had occasion to catalogue a synthesis-evolution
Karesian, and he suspected, correctly, that it never would again.

"You may," she said, and extended her hand before he had finished asking whether she minded the
contact itself.

I want to record precisely what I watched cross his face in the moment his own ability engaged,
because I have known Julian long enough to recognize when he is genuinely startled by what he feels
rather than merely attentive to it. His sensitivity distinguishes upward of two hundred specific
physiological conditions through touch alone, reading a person's complete state as felt sensation
rather than image or word, and I am told -- by him, afterward, at some length, with the particular
excitement of a scholar who has just been handed a text in a language he did not know he could
read -- that what came through her hand was not two hundred conditions layered atop a baseline he
recognized, but a body operating at a design tolerance his own diagnostic vocabulary had no entries
for at all. Not illness. Not damage. Simply capacity, fully and permanently realized, with nothing
left unused for his instruments to flag as deficient.

"I do not know what to write down," he admitted, which I gather he does not often say aloud, given
how much of his authority within this crew rests on the presumption that he always does.

"Write down that you do not know," Lauris said. "I have found that the honest gap in a record is
worth considerably more than a guess dressed up to fill it."

He laughed at that, a short, surprised sound, and set about doing precisely as she suggested --
opening, by his own later account to me, an entirely new page in the Red Index, headed only with her
name and a description of what he could and could not classify, rather than force the reading into
a category that did not fit it. He told me afterward that in a hundred and fifty years of cataloguing
every body that crossed his path, willing or otherwise, he had never once been thanked for admitting
he did not know something, and that she had done exactly that, and meant it.

What struck me most, watching from across the room, was not the reading itself but what it plainly
cost her nothing to give. I have written elsewhere of the researchers and Directorate instruments
that spent centuries reading Lauris without once asking her leave to do so -- reading her as subject,
as sample, as a problem to be solved from the outside. Julian's reading was the precise inverse of
all of it: offered as a question, answered as a gift, closed the moment either of them wished it
closed. She told me afterward that she had enjoyed it, plainly and without qualification, the way I
have come to recognize she enjoys most things that ask something real of her and ask it honestly --
not because being read holds any particular thrill for her, but because being read *well*, by
someone who cared more about being accurate than about being finished, was a kind of attention she
has been given perhaps four or five times in six thousand years.

"You may read me again," she told him, as she was leaving, "whenever your index is curious."

He has, since, according to his own account, found himself curious rather more often than his
professional discipline strictly requires.
