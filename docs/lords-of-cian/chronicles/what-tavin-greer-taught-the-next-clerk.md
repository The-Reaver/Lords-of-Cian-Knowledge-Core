# What Tavin Greer Taught the Next Clerk

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle XXXIX, wave 13.
Rebellion era, age 18, before the Black Trench. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters beyond the already-locked Tavin Greer.*

---

Tavin Greer had never asked to become the man everyone pointed to when they wanted to know whether
the Dredge-Line chalking had actually happened the way the stories said — a role he'd carried
uneasily for months, mostly by fielding questions he hadn't chosen to be the answer to.

What he could control, he eventually decided, was what happened after him.

"You're the one who marked the officer," the new clerk said, a nervous young man barely older than
Kanja himself, assigned to the five-district cross-checking work after the depot manifest exposure
had made independent verification suddenly urgent across the whole canal. "Everyone says you're the
one who actually remembers what it was like before any of this had a name."

"I remember it. Doesn't mean I understand it any better than you do, just because I was there first."
Greer had grown used to disappointing people who expected more from the encounter than he had to
give. "What do you actually want from me?"

"I want to know how you learned to verify a number you couldn't check against anything else. The
first time, I mean. Before there were five districts and independent clerks and a printed sheet
everyone trusts. How did you know to believe the first tally, when there was nothing else to compare
it to?"

Greer considered the question longer than he'd expected to need to. "I didn't believe it because I
had a way to check it. I believed it because I watched him read it aloud to a district that had every
reason to assume it was another lie, and he read it slowly enough, and plainly enough, that anyone
could have caught him in an error if there'd been one to catch. That's not verification. That's
something else — watching whether a man's afraid of being checked, or whether he's inviting it."

"That's not really a method, though. That's just a feeling."

"It became a method. That's the part worth teaching you." Greer found, somewhat to his own surprise,
that he had more to say than he'd expected walking in. "The feeling came first, because there was
nothing else available yet. The method — the clerks, the cross-checking, the sheets — came after,
built by other people, to make sure nobody else ever had to rely only on a feeling the way I did that
first day. You've got the method now. You don't need my feeling anymore. But I'd want you to
understand it existed, once, before there was anything sturdier to stand on."

The clerk left with considerably more than he'd arrived expecting — not a story to retell, but a
sense of what the district's now-elaborate verification system had actually grown out of, and Greer,
watching him go, found the encounter had cost him less than every prior one had.
