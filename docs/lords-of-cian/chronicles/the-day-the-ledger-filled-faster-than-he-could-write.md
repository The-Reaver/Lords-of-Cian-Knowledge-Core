# The Day the Ledger Filled Faster Than He Could Write

The refinery district was larger than anywhere the campaign had worked before — six thousand workers
across three shifts, a single sprawling complex that made every prior district look like a single
yard by comparison — and by the second hour of the first morning, the line waiting to testify had
grown faster than Ezio and Kanja together could write it down.

"We're falling behind," Ezio said, not looking up, his hand already cramping from an hour of
unbroken entries. "At this pace we lose a full day just to backlog before we've even started the
cross-check."

Efa Gol was managing the queue the way she'd learned to since the milling combine's crowd, but even
her best ordering couldn't slow six thousand people down to the speed of two pens, and Pell Ostra,
watching the gap widen, put down the satchel he'd been guarding all morning and picked up a spare
sheaf of blank pages instead.

"I've watched you do this enough months running to know the shape of it," he said. "Four words, worst-
off first, write it exact, don't soften anything. I'm not as fast as either of you and I'll get things
wrong the first hour. But wrong and correctable beats a backlog nobody catches up from."

Kanja hesitated only long enough to weigh it, then handed over a stack of pages without further
argument, because the alternative — turning workers away, or rushing entries past the point of
accuracy — was worse than the risk of an untrained scribe's early mistakes. Efa took a second sheaf
minutes later, splitting the line into three parallel queues instead of one, each feeding its own
scribe, testimony passed hand to hand and cross-checked against the others' entries at the end of
each hour to catch anything recorded twice or missed between queues.

It was untidy in a way no district's ledger had been before — four different hands across the pages,
corrections layered over corrections, the whole system held together by constant cross-referencing
rather than any single scribe's careful pace. But it worked. Six thousand testimonies, taken across
two exhausting days instead of the week the district's size would otherwise have demanded, closed
with an error rate, once fully cross-checked, no worse than any smaller district's single-scribe
tally.

"We nearly drowned in our own method today," Ezio said, that evening, flexing a hand that hadn't held
a pen that long in one stretch before.

"We didn't, though." Kanja looked at the four-handed pages, imperfect and complete both. "The method
was never really about who holds the pen. It's about making sure nobody who wants to be heard goes
unheard because the two of us happened to be slower than the crowd."
