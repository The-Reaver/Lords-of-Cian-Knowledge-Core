# The Year She Didn't Come

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1763`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XXXIV — fourth entry in the Val Mirel strand's second wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not
present in this entry. Ozmund is roughly twenty-six to twenty-seven, spanning a full year after
Chronicle XVI. Extends Val Mirel's established "chosen distance" theme (Chronicle XIV, `MCD-1743`)
through her longest single absence in the strand, deliberately left unresolved rather than explained
away. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story
Ozmund told him years later. No new named characters.*

---

This is the story the General told me the flattest, in the fewest words of any of them, and I want to
say plainly that I don't think the flatness meant it cost him less. I've come to think the opposite. The
stories he gave me the most words for were the ones he'd already made peace with by the time he handed
them to me. This one, I don't believe he ever fully did.

He was twenty-six the last time she came before the gap began, and he told me he had no way of knowing,
standing in the yard watching her ride out that morning, that it would be the longest year of his life
before he saw her again — not because anything was said to warn him. Nothing was. She left the way she
always left, no ceremony to it, and it was only in hindsight, he told me, that he understood the visit
itself had carried a certain weight to it he hadn't recognized at the time, the weight of someone
closing a door quietly enough that you don't notice until you've already tried it and found it shut.

Word came, eventually, in the sparse, official way the Seventh Wing's own channels allowed rather than
anything personal — a campaign, unnamed in any dispatch that reached House Verehimu, that had pulled her
command somewhere the General never learned the shape of, not that year and not in any of the years
after. The dispatches that did arrive were not written in her hand. He told me that mattered to him more
than their content ever did — a confirmation she was alive, nothing more, filtered through whatever
officer's duty it was to see that families of the Wing's tacticians weren't left with silence alone. He
kept every one of them. He told me he could still, decades later, recite the wording of the first,
because it had taught him, reading it, the exact difference between knowing his mother was safe and
knowing anything at all about her.

He held the Stone Count every dawn that year regardless, alone in the yard where he'd once held it beside
her, and he told me he was honest enough with himself, even at twenty-six, to know the discipline had
changed its meaning somewhere in those months without his permission. It had started as a practice she'd
given him. It became, across that year, something closer to a vigil — a way of standing in the exact
place she would have stood, at the exact hour she would have stood there, as though holding the shape of
her absence precisely might somehow shorten it.

He wrote to her four times that year, he told me, though he had no confidence any of the letters ever
reached her hands rather than some clerk's filing case in a war he wasn't cleared to know the details of.
He never told me what he wrote. What he did tell me was that somewhere past the sixth month, writing the
third of those letters, he caught himself questioning, for the first time in his life, the very thing she
had told him so carefully on that terrace years before — that her distance had always been a choice, made
for him, at real cost to her. He told me he sat with the pen in his hand a long while that evening,
turning over a colder possibility he had never let himself hold before: that perhaps the choice she'd
described to him had simply, somewhere along the way, stopped being one. That the Order had not asked
her permission this time any more than it had ever asked his.

He never resolved that doubt that year. He told me that plainly, and I believed him, because I never once
heard him claim otherwise in all the years after, not even once he'd had decades to make his own kind of
peace with it. What he told me instead was that he learned something from the not-knowing itself, a
lesson the Stone Count had never quite managed to teach him on its own: that a discipline built to
outlast urgency was not the same discipline as one built to outlast doubt, and that he had spent a year
discovering he needed both, standing alone in a training yard at dawn, holding a rhythm for a woman who
might, for all he could prove that year, have simply stopped being able to keep it herself.

The year ended the way it had begun — without ceremony, without warning. A single dispatch arrived in
early autumn, no different in form from the dozen impersonal ones before it, except for four words
added at its close in a hand the clerk's careful script hadn't produced. He never told me what those four
words were. He told me only that they weren't an explanation, and weren't an apology, and that he had
never once, reading them, needed either.
