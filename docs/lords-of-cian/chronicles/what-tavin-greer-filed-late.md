# What Tavin Greer Filed Late

*Locked canon, Batch 255, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1131`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LXXIV, wave 25.
Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. Tavin Greer, still in Directorate service, finds a legal way to be
slow.*

---

Tavin Greer had never told his superiors, in the months since the chalking, that he bore the crew who
had marked him anything other than the professional disdain any officer owed the men who'd humiliated
him. It had not, strictly speaking, been a lie. What he felt about the Trench Monarch and his crew had
never settled into a single clean emotion he could have named for anyone who asked, and nobody had
asked closely enough to make him try.

He was ordered, that autumn, to compile precise timing and access details for a site the crew was
expected to correct within the week — routine intelligence work, the kind any officer in his position
was expected to produce without comment. He compiled it exactly as ordered. Every fact in the report
he filed was accurate. He simply filed it four days later than his own competence would ordinarily
have allowed, citing, with scrupulous and entirely defensible precision, a records-verification delay
in the archive office that had genuinely, if not urgently, needed doing.

Nobody above him questioned the delay. Greer had built, in the months since his release, a
reputation for exactly the kind of careful, unhurried thoroughness that made a four-day gap look like
diligence rather than anything else. By the time the strike force reached the site with his
intelligence in hand, the site correction had already concluded, cleanly and without incident, three
days prior.

He never told anyone what he'd done, and never claimed credit for it, because claiming credit would
have required admitting to himself what the delay had actually been for. He filed his after-action
report on the missed opportunity with the same scrupulous precision he brought to every report,
noting honestly that the intelligence had arrived too late to be actionable, and recommending — with
no apparent irony he would have acknowledged if asked — a faster records-verification process for
future assignments.

Kanja never learned Greer's name was attached to the delay at all. What reached him, weeks later, was
only the plain fact that a strike meant to catch the crew mid-correction had somehow arrived after
the fact, with no explanation anyone bothered chasing down. He mentioned it once to Hask, in passing,
as one more piece of good fortune in a season that had produced its share of bad.

Greer, for his part, kept doing his job competently, thoroughly, and — on the occasions when doing so
cost the crew that had marked him nothing at all — exactly as slowly as the truth could plausibly
bear.
