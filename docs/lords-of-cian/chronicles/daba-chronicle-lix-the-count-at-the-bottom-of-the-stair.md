# Daba Chronicle LIX: The Count at the Bottom of the Stair

The requisition came in Orsk Dresk's hand, copied off a quartermaster's slate in a supply yard two
districts east of the Fenwold blocks. Lamp oil, twelve casks. Pitch, four. Stair charges, four, set
for timber footings. Signed for a correction column. Dated six days out.

Daba read it twice at Kether's table. The paper smelled of nothing. He smelled oil anyway, the
oiled edge of a smoke meant to catch and meant to spread, and under it the flat counted rhythm of
boots on boards. He set the paper down square to the table's edge.

"Stairs first," he said.

Kether looked at him. "You know the method."

"I know the method."

1804 did not fight columns. Anything large enough to be seen was large enough to be burned. So the
cells did what the doctrine allowed: they emptied the Fenwold blocks quietly, a household at a time,
at night, under a rumor of fever that the Trust's own clerks would not bother to check. Orsk Dresk's
cell ran the west block. Kether's ran the alleys. Daba walked both stairwells of the east block
himself the night before, with a lamp and a stick of chalk, and marked every footing where a man who
had done this before would set a charge. Kether did not argue. She had heard the Rookery spoken of
by the two people in the network who had been inside it, and one of them was walking the stairs.

The other was Mika. She had asked to come. He had let her.

The column came a night early.

He heard it before anyone shouted: boots, counted, the rhythm of men who did not expect to run.
Then the oil. The west block was empty and caught first, lit from the yard. The east block still had
its top floor full: a night class for the laundry-guild children and the four women who taught it,
moved last because the class had been the fever rumor's best cover. Twenty-two people on the fourth
floor. One service stair down the back, past the laundry passage, out to an alley the Trust's maps
did not draw.

"Mika," he said. "Top of the stair. Count them out."

She went up without a word. He went down.

The laundry passage was dark and wet, steam-stained timber and the sour smell of lye. At the stair
foot a single lantern sat on the flags, shuttered to a slit. A man knelt beside it at the footing
Daba had chalked the night before. Officer's coat. Collar plate. A slow-match burning in his left
hand, the coal a dull red eye. The charge packed against the footing. The fuse two hand-widths from
the coal.

Above them, on the stair, small feet. Mika's voice, low and level, calling a count.

The officer heard Daba's step on the flags and came up off his knee fast, sword half drawn in the
right hand, the match still in the left. He did not drop the match. He moved it toward the fuse.

Daba did not remember deciding anything. He was already inside the sword's reach, where a blade that
long had no room to work. Left forearm across the officer's right wrist, pinning the half-drawn
sword against the scabbard throat. His own short blade in under the jaw, where the collar plate
stopped a finger's width short of the bone. One thrust, upward, full weight behind it. The officer's
legs went before the rest of him. The match fell.

Daba caught the coal in his bare hand and closed his fist on it.

Heat, white and complete. The smell of his own skin. He held it until the red was gone between his
fingers, then opened the hand and let the dead match drop beside the dead man. He did not look at
either of them.

He stood at the bottom of the stair and counted.

They came down in the dark in a line, small hands on the rail, the women spaced among them the way
the doctrine taught, Mika at the tail. He counted heads the way he counted stitches. Eleven. Twelve.
A boy who stopped on the last step to stare at the shape on the flags, and Daba turned him by the
shoulder toward the alley door without a word. Twenty. Twenty-one. Mika.

Twenty-two at the top. Twenty-two at the bottom.

He said the number aloud once, to no one, and the alley took it.

Behind them the column lit the east block from the yard and stood back to watch it. It burned
empty. Kether's cell had dropped two barrow-loads of rubble and a cart across the alley mouth, and
by the time the column found the alley it was holding nothing but smoke. No one from Fenwold died
that night. No one from 1804.

---

Kether brought him the officer's coat two days later, emptied onto her table. A purse. A seal-ring
with a Trust device. A service book, small, oilcloth-bound, kept in a careful hand.

Captain Edran Brannick. Decades of entries. Postings, commendations, corrections. Most of
the corrections were a line each.

Daba turned pages until he found the one he had known, since the stair, that he would find.

*Rookery. Correction. Stairs first. Complete.*

Mika read it over his shoulder. She did not say anything for a long time. The bandage on his left
hand had soaked through again at the palm, and she reached over and pressed it closed without
looking at it.

"The Trust lists him dead in a fire," Kether said. "Fenwold. Undetermined origin."

"Good."

"You going to write him?" Mika asked.

His own list sat in the drawer where it always sat. Every name it held belonged to 1804. Fennic's
was first.

"No," Daba said. "He kept his own book."

The service book went into 1804's record with the requisition and Orsk Dresk's copy, filed under
the operation. Inside the network it is the Fenwold stair. The Trust's ledger has a captain lost to an accident of fire. Fenwold has a block of ash
and twenty-two people who slept somewhere else that night.

That winter Daba read his list as he did every winter. It held no new names. He read every one of
them anyway, slowly, and closed the drawer.
