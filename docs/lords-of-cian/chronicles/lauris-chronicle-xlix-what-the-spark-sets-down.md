# Lauris Chronicle XLIX: What the Spark Sets Down

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1666`). Forty-ninth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, the fifth of the "Strand W" (Witness / present-day, quiet-register) thread. A

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Anansi did not want to talk tonight. I
did not make him. We played instead, and I lost twice on purpose and once honestly, and he noticed
which was which, and neither of us said so."*

I include this account with some hesitation, since Anansi is not a man who volunteers himself easily
to a record, and I have tried across the whole of this project to write nothing of him that he has
not, in some fashion, already permitted to be written. What I watched that evening he permitted
simply by not leaving the room, which for him is closer to an invitation than most people's spoken
one.

He carries rage the way I have already noted Kanja carries grief and Sephtis carries time — not as
an event he can point to, but as a working condition, the fuel a man spends lighting other people's
resolve without, I suspect, ever quite banking enough of it for his own rest. I do not think Lauris
set out to address that directly. I have never seen her address anything of his directly. She simply
arrived where he was, some hours after whatever work of his own had worn him down that particular
day, and set a worn cloth pouch of marked stones on the table between them without a word of
preamble.

"I am not in company tonight," he said.

"I am not asking you to be in company. I am asking you to play, which is a different request
entirely, and one you may decline without offending me."

He did not decline it. I want to note, for the sake of the record, that he did not accept it warmly
either — he drew the stones toward himself with the flat economy of a man conserving effort he
suspects he will need again before the night is through, and said nothing further for the better
part of twenty minutes beyond what the game itself required of him.

I have watched Lauris across enough of these evenings now to recognize her particular way of losing
on purpose, which she does not do out of pity — I do not believe pity is a register she has much use
for — but out of something closer to patience for a mood she does not need explained to her. She
lost the first round cleanly, gave the second away with a deliberateness I do not think she troubled
to disguise, and won the third honestly, without apology, the moment his own attention had settled
enough to make honest play the more respectful choice.

He noticed. I watched him notice, in the small adjustment of his shoulders that is, for Anansi, the
nearest thing to a raised eyebrow he permits himself in company he has not fully decided to trust.
He did not remark on it. Neither did she. I have come to think this is closer to the actual shape of
their friendship than anything either of them would state outright — an accounting kept silently,
on both sides, of exactly how much the other was willing to give a particular evening, with neither
one demanding the ledger be read aloud.

"You did not need to let me win the second," he said, eventually, in the low register I have learned
means he is closer to speaking plainly than his usual public register ever permits.

"I did not let you win it for your sake. I let you win it because I wanted to watch what you did
with a small mercy you had not asked for and did not expect. You did nothing dramatic with it. I
found that instructive."

"What did you expect me to do."

"Nothing in particular. I simply enjoy finding out."

He considered this for a long moment, turning one of the stones over in his fingers without setting
it down, and I want to record — since I believe the record would be incomplete without it — that
something in his face eased that had not eased since he had entered the room. Not resolved. I do not
believe a game of stones resolves what he carries, and I would distrust any account that claimed it
had. But set down, for the span of an evening, the way a man sets down a weight he has carried so
long he has stopped noticing the carrying, and only notices, briefly, the absence.

They played three more rounds after that, all of them honest on both sides, and he won two of the
three. I do not think the score mattered to either of them by then. I record it anyway, because I
have found, across enough of these accounts, that the smallest true details are the ones a reader
trusts most — and because I believe Lauris herself would want it recorded exactly that plainly, with
nothing added to make the evening sound larger than the quiet thing it actually was.
