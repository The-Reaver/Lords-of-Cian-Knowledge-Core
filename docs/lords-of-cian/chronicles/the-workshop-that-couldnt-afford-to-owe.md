# The Workshop That Couldn't Afford to Owe

*Locked canon, Batch 232, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1034`). The Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LX, wave 20, closing the wave. A small workshop owner genuinely owes his three workers back wages, but full payment would close the workshop and end all three jobs at once, forcing the method to weigh justice for the workers against the survival of the smallest, least culpable employer it has ever documented. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The workshop had three workers, one owner, and a debt that added up honestly to more than the owner had ever had in hand at once.

It wasn't a fortune. That was the problem. Every other case the crew had built against an administrator or a mill owner involved someone who could absorb the finding and keep operating, someone above them who could be traced to and made to pay, or at minimum a business large enough that the debt was a fraction of its worth. This was a one-man cabinetmaker's shop that had underpaid its three journeymen for two years not out of contempt but because the owner had been paying himself less than any of them the whole time and still falling behind on his own debts to his timber supplier. Ezio's arithmetic was clean and undisputed. So was the second arithmetic, the one nobody wanted to say out loud first: collecting the full amount, today, closed the shop and cost all three journeymen their jobs along with the owner his.

The three workers didn't want that either. They said so themselves, separately, before anyone asked them to.

There was no chain above this owner to trace the debt to, the way the crew had once followed a shortfall up through four honest intermediary layers to an absentee financier who could actually afford to pay it. There was no fabrication to catch, no staged compliance to test, no overseer to remove. There was just a small, honest failure with nowhere left to put it that didn't break something else.

Kanja didn't waive the debt. He recorded it in full, exactly as owed, because the ledger had never once adjusted a figure to make anyone comfortable and wasn't going to start now. But the settlement he built alongside the finding was the first of its kind: a repayment schedule stretched across three years, tied to the shop's own survival rather than a fixed timetable, with the three journeymen — not Kanja, not Ezio — setting the pace themselves, since they were the ones with the most to lose either way it went.

"This isn't mercy," he told the owner, who'd expected either forgiveness or ruin and gotten neither. "The debt is real and it's written down as real. What we're deciding is only how fast collecting it kills something none of us actually want dead."

The owner paid on schedule for three years, slower some months than others, and the workshop was still standing at the end of it. The ledger entry closed clean, the same as any other — full amount, fully satisfied — with a note beside it that this was the first settlement the method had ever built around a debtor's own survival rather than around the size of the debt owed.
