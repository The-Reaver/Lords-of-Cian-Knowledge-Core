# The Last Thing the Old Hand Said

*Locked canon, Batch 127, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-430`). Captain Alias Chronicle VI, closing the second
wave. Rebellion era, a retirement. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The old dockhand had been with the crew since the earliest days on Pier Nine, before either alias or
title had settled on anyone, and his hands had finally given out past the point any field medic
could argue him back into service — a quiet, undramatic ending to a war most of the newer recruits
assumed only ended in death or glory, never in a man simply deciding he'd given what he had left to
give.

"I'm not asking your permission," he told Kanja, the morning he formally stepped back from active
duty. "I'm telling you as a courtesy, because I've served under you since before you had a name
anybody bothered giving you, and I figured you'd want to hear it from me instead of a report."

"You don't owe me a courtesy. You've more than earned the right to walk away without explaining
yourself to anyone." Kanja set aside whatever he'd been working on, giving the moment its full
attention. "What are you going to do?"

"Teach, I think. There's a whole generation of dockhands who've grown up in this war without ever
knowing what the piers looked like before it. Somebody should remember that for them, the way
somebody once remembered it for me." The old man's hands, permanently stiffened from decades of
labor and years of war on top of it, trembled slightly as he spoke, though his voice stayed
perfectly steady. "I called you a lot of things over the years, before the crew settled on the one
that stuck. Boy, at the start. Then Trench Monarch, same as everyone. It's Captain now, and has been
for a long while. I wanted to tell you, before I stepped back, which one actually meant the most to
me."

"Which one?"

"None of them, and all of them, honestly. What mattered wasn't the word. It was that every single one
of them, you earned honestly, one decision at a time, in front of people who were watching close
enough to know the difference between an earned name and a given one." He extended a hand,
still trembling, still offered without hesitation. "I've served a lot of men who wanted to be called
something. You're the first one I've served who seemed to actually understand that the calling was
never the point. Take care of the crew I'm handing back to you. They're good people. They deserve the
same honesty you've always given the rest of us."

He left the following week, quietly, without ceremony, and taught dockworking apprentices for the
remainder of a long life — telling them, when they asked about the war stories everyone assumed he
had, less about battles than about a young man who had once earned every name he was ever given, one
honest decision at a time.
