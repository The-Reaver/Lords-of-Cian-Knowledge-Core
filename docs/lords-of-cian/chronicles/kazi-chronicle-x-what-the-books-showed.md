# Kazi Chronicle X: What the Books Showed

*Locked canon, Batch 289, 2026-09-12 (`MCD-1529`). Fifth entry in Kazi's own Chronicles, Femi
(`PH2-066`) as protagonist, not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, intervention, or resolution credit. Narration is close-third on Femi throughout.
Set after Femi's election (Kazi Chronicle IX, `MCD-1528`), dramatizing his trustee access forcing a
vote no one expected.*

---

A trustee's real power, Femi discovered in his first month holding the seat, was not the vote he cast
at meetings — one vote among five, easily outnumbered — but the plain right, written into the fund's
own bylaws in language no one had bothered enforcing in years, to inspect the books whenever he asked.
He asked in his second week. It took the fund's administrator eleven days to produce them, and when he
finally read them straight through, twice, he found what he had gone looking for without quite
admitting to himself that he'd expected to find it: three years of disability claims from the plant's
oldest section, denied at a rate nearly double every other department's, with no medical review on
file for most of them at all — only a stamp, and a date, and a signature belonging to a man who had
left the fund's employ two years before half the stamps were dated.

He did not bring it to the floor first. He brought it to the other four trustees, in a closed meeting,
laid the eleven days' worth of pages out in the order the dates actually ran rather than the order the
file kept them in, and asked a single question: whether any of them could explain the signature. None
of them could. One of them, visibly, did not want to try.

"You're proposing a full audit," the fund's chairman said, in the flat tone of a man measuring how
much trouble a request would cause against how much trouble refusing it would cause instead. "That's
not a small thing to put to a vote. It implicates people."

"It implicates a signature on a stamp that predates the man who's supposed to have made it. I'm not
proposing to decide what that means. I'm proposing the five of us vote on whether anyone gets to look
closely enough to find out." Femi kept the pages in the order he'd laid them, not touching them again,
letting the dates make the argument he wasn't going to make twice. "You can vote no. I'd want it on
record that you did, and why, same as I'd want it on record if I voted no on something this plain."

The vote passed three to two, closer than the eleven-vote margin that had put him in the seat at all,
and closer than he'd have liked on a matter that plain — but it passed, and it was the first vote of
its kind the fund had held in longer than anyone currently sitting on it could remember. Irin heard
about it the same evening, from Kunle, and said little beyond what Femi had half expected him to say.

"One vote. One fund. It's not the floor moving as one body — it's five men in a room deciding whether
to let daylight in." Irin didn't say it unkindly. "I won't pretend I understand yet what it's worth
against calling a halt that four thousand men feel at once. But I've learned not to mistake a
different kind of weapon for no weapon at all."

"It's not a weapon that moves fast," Femi said. "It moved eleven days to get me the books, another six
to get the vote called, and it'll likely move another two months before the audit itself says anything
plain enough to act on. But when it says it, it'll have said it in the fund's own language, in the
fund's own record, in a way the fund itself can't later claim it never knew."

A stranger who had spent that evening moving boxes of the fund's older archive from one storage room
to another, at Femi's own quiet request and no one else's notice, carried the last of them out after
the trustees had gone, said nothing about what he'd read on the way past, and was not there the
following morning when the audit's clerk arrived to begin.
