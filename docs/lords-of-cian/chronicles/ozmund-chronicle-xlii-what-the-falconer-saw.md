I never sought Annis Fairweather out. She found me, the way the truest sources always seemed to,
having heard secondhand that someone was collecting what could still be collected of a man most of
Cian only knew as a title. She kept a mews of her own by then, well past Verehimu service, training
hunting birds for lesser houses who could afford a falconer but not a mystery like the one she'd once
had daily access to. She told me she'd never once told anyone the story she was about to tell me,
mostly because no one had ever thought to ask a falconer what she knew about a lord's son, and partly
because she'd suspected, all these years, that it was the kind of thing that belonged to him rather
than to her.

He would have been seven, perhaps eight — young enough that the mews weren't yet forbidden to him,
old enough to have worked out that they were more interesting than anywhere else in the House he was
allowed to wander unsupervised. Annis said he came most mornings that one particular season, always
alone, always quiet in the specific way of a child who has learned that being quiet is how you're
allowed to stay somewhere. She let him, mostly because he never touched anything without asking and
never asked for anything twice if the answer was no.

The bird in question was a young goshawk, difficult even by goshawk standards, that had refused to
take the glove from anyone but Annis herself for the better part of two months — a bird with a temper,
she said, that made most of the House's own hunting staff nervous just standing near the block. Ozmund
had watched her work the bird for weeks without once asking to try, which she said was itself
unusual for a boy his age, before he finally did ask, on a morning she remembered specifically because
it had been raining and the mews had smelled the way it only smelled when the birds were restless with
weather.

She told him no, the first time, on the reasonable grounds that the bird had put a hunting master's
own forearm through a leather sleeve not a month before. He accepted it without argument, which she
said surprised her more than the asking had, and went back to sitting quietly on the overturned crate
he'd claimed as his own. It was three mornings later that he asked again, and this time she said yes
— not because anything about the bird had changed, but because she'd decided, watching him, that she'd
rather see exactly how careful he actually was than keep guessing at it.

What she remembered, sixty years on, wasn't caution exactly. It was concentration — a child entirely,
completely absorbed in the specific weight and balance of holding his arm just so, gauging the bird's
mood through its stance the way Annis had spent a lifetime teaching grown men to do and rarely
succeeding. The goshawk took the glove without incident. It also, a moment later, misjudged its own
landing in the particular way young birds do, overbalanced, and beat its wings hard enough to knock
the boy backward off his feet into a stack of empty creels — an undignified, thoroughly ordinary fall,
the kind any child takes a dozen times a season and forgets about by supper.

He laughed. That was the whole of what she'd come to tell me, in the end, stripped of everything else
— that a boy sat on his back in a pile of overturned creels with a startled goshawk beating the air
above him and laughed until he had to wipe his eyes, an open, unguarded, completely undignified sound
she said she'd never once heard from him before or after, in a House where she suspected — though she
was careful to say only suspected — that he was rarely permitted to be anything but exactly who he was
expected to be. She helped him up. He thanked her, straightened his own clothes without being told to,
and asked, very seriously, whether he'd frightened the bird. She told him no. He came back the next
morning as if nothing at all had happened.

Annis said she'd thought about that fall more than almost anything else from those years, long after
she'd left Verehimu service and long after the boy had become someone the whole of Cian had opinions
about. Not because it meant anything by itself, she was careful to tell me — she didn't claim to know
what it meant, if it meant anything at all. Only that she'd been given, for the length of one wet
morning, an hour of a child being nothing but a child, and that she'd come to suspect, the older she
got, that hours like that one had been rarer for him than for most boys born to far less.
