# Lauris Chronicle LI: The Bout With No Score Kept

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Valen and I did not speak for most of
three hours today. I do not think either of us noticed the silence as silence. I noticed it only
afterward, trying to write this down, and found I had very little to report except that it had been
a good three hours."*

I have written one account already of an evening spent watching Lauris and Valen spar, and I
considered, briefly, whether a second was worth the paper. I have decided it is, precisely because
the two evenings looked almost nothing alike, and I think a reader deserves to see both faces of
what their companionship actually is, rather than assume the first account was the whole of it.

The prior evening had a rhythm to it — a count kept, a question asked and answered, something
recognizable as conversation threaded through the bouts themselves. This one had none of that. I
arrived at the training deck perhaps twenty minutes after they had already begun, judging by the
state of both of them, and settled into my usual place at the edge of the room expecting, from
long habit, to have something to transcribe before the hour was out.

I transcribed almost nothing. They worked through what I estimated, afterward, to be somewhere
near forty exchanges across three hours, and I counted eleven words spoken between them across the
whole of it, four of which were his correcting her stance and seven of which were her acknowledging
the correction without argument. I do not say this to suggest the time passed emptily. I say it
because I think the emptiness of speech was the entire point, and I do not believe either of them
would have called it emptiness at all.

Valen does not fill silence the way some people do, out of discomfort with it. I have come to
understand, across enough hours in his company, that silence is simply his native register, and
that speech is, for him, closer to an expenditure he makes only when the exchange specifically
requires it. What struck me watching him work through three wordless hours with Lauris is how
completely she matched that register without, so far as I could see, any effort to match it. She did
not perform patience for him. She simply had it in the same quantity he did, and the two of them
spent the afternoon in a kind of shared stillness that I do not think either could have sustained,
in quite the same way, with anyone else in this crew.

"Again," he said, once, some two hours in, and it was the whole of the sentence — no correction
attached, no assessment offered, simply an invitation to continue that she answered by resetting her
stance without a word of her own.

I watched her, across those three hours, more closely than I usually allow myself to watch a
training bout that carries no stakes worth recording, because I wanted to know whether the joy I
have written of elsewhere required an audience, or a victory, or even a defeat sharp enough to
register as loss. I do not believe it required any of those things. She lost most of the forty
exchanges, by my own rough count, the same as she had the evening before, and she met each loss with
the same unhurried reset, the same evident satisfaction in a body doing exactly what she had asked
of it, whether or not the asking produced a win.

Near the close of the third hour, with the light beginning to change through the high windows in a
way that suggested the hour had genuinely slipped past both of them without either noticing, Valen
finally broke from the pattern he had held all afternoon.

"You did not tire," he said. Not quite a question, though I had learned by then to treat most of his
sentences that way regardless.

"No."

"Neither did I."

"I know," she said, and there was, in the two words, something I want to call satisfaction rather
than surprise — as though the fact that neither of them had tired across three nearly wordless hours
was itself the entire content of the afternoon, and required no further remark to be complete.

They did not speak again before parting, so far as I recorded. I have come to think that is the
truest thing I can say about the whole of this account: that a woman who has spent six thousand
years being handed unanswerable ledgers to close, and a man who measures his every motion against a
standard he has never once let himself meet, spent three hours together in near-total silence and
both came away from it, by every visible measure, rested. I do not think either of them would call
that an unusual afternoon. I think, for the two of them specifically, it may be closer to an
ordinary one — and I have come to believe the ordinary ones are the ones most worth setting down.
