*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Orlok asked me what I do with what I
have seen. I told him I keep it. He said he does the same, and that he has kept a great deal longer
than I have. I did not find that a competition. I found it a comfort."*

Orlok visits this crew rarely enough that I have come to treat each occasion as worth its own
account, whatever else may or may not have happened during it. Nothing happened during this one, in
the sense the word usually carries in a chronicle. He arrived, sat with Lauris for the better part of
an evening in a room with no strategic value, and left again the following morning for Celestial
Zenith. I record it anyway, because I do not believe a chronicle of this crew is complete without
some account of what passes between its two oldest present-day members when neither has any
particular reason to guard anything from the other.

She had prepared nothing for the visit, which I mention only because I have watched her prepare a
great deal for other occasions and I think the absence is itself informative. She simply sat with
him, the way she sits with Sephtis on the evenings I have already recorded, at a conversational
volume so low I transcribed perhaps a tenth of what actually passed between them, most of the rest
given over to silence that neither seemed inclined to fill.

"You keep what you witness," Orlok said, some way into the evening, not quite a question. "I have
heard it described to me that way. A discipline, rather than a memory."

"It is both," she said. "The memory does not require effort. Keeping it well does."

He considered this with the particular stillness I understand to be characteristic of a man who has
had seventy-six thousand years to develop a relationship with stillness that most beings do not live
long enough to begin. "I have kept things a long while myself. Longer, I suspect, than is fully
comfortable to admit in company that has not asked to know the length of it."

"I am not asking you to name the length. I am asking whether it has grown heavier with the keeping,
or lighter."

He did not answer that quickly, and I want to note, for the sake of an honest account, that I do not
believe the delay was evasion. I believe it was simply the true shape of the question meeting a man
who takes the trouble to answer questions correctly rather than promptly. "Neither, most days," he
said eventually. "It has grown quieter. I do not know that quiet and light are the same thing. I
have had a great while to consider the difference and have not settled it."

"I would not expect you to have settled it in seventy-six thousand years," she said, "if I have not
settled the same question in six. I do not think it is the kind of question that settles. I have
made a kind of peace with carrying it unsettled."

I want to be careful here not to overstate what I watched, since I believe the evening's whole value
lay in how little either of them overstated to each other. There was no confession exchanged, no
secret surfaced that either had been keeping from the other or from this account. Orlok's own
restraint — the timing he weighs so carefully with matters I have written of elsewhere and will not
repeat here — went entirely untouched across the whole of the evening, and I do not believe Lauris
pressed for it, or would have accepted it if offered without his own readiness behind it. What passed
between them instead was smaller and, I have come to think, rarer: two people who have each spent
an almost unthinkable span of years watching things happen that they did not, in the moment, have the
power to prevent, sitting together without either one needing to explain to the other why that
particular weight does not announce itself the way grief or doubt or rage might announce themselves
to someone who has not carried it as long.

"You carry it lightly," he said, near the evening's end, in a tone I understood as genuine
observation rather than either envy or reproach. "I have watched a great many people attempt what
you carry. Few of them arrive at anything resembling joy on the other side of it."

"I did not arrive at it quickly," she said. "I would not call it luck, either, though I understand
why it might look that way from outside. I simply decided, a long while ago now, that grief and joy
were not required to take turns in me. I do not think that decision was easy. I think it has simply
had a great deal of time to stop feeling like a decision."

He left the following morning without ceremony, the way he arrives at most things, and I do not
believe either of them expected the visit to be remembered as anything more than what it was. I
record it here regardless, because I have come to believe that the rarest thing this account can
offer a reader is the sight of two very old people finding, briefly and without effort, that neither
one has to explain the weight of their own years to the other — and because I think Lauris, reading
this someday, would tell me plainly that the evening needed nothing more said about it than that.
