# The Watch That Forgot to Rest

*Locked canon, Batch 257, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1189`). Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LXXVIII, wave 26, closing the wave. A fatigue/exhaustion safety-culture entry -- the enemy is complacency and overwork, not the Directorate.*

---

The near-miss cost nobody their life, which was the only reason anyone found out how close it had actually come. A night crew, six weeks into rotations that had crept from ten hours to fourteen without anyone formally approving the change, missed a groaning support beam that should have been obvious to eyes that weren't half-shut with exhaustion. A second crew, arriving fresh, caught it twenty minutes before the section would have failed with people still under it.

Kanja heard about it the next morning and didn't wait for a formal report.

"Fourteen hours," he said, reading the rotation logs Garren Hask kept updated more out of habit than instruction. "Nobody ordered that. It just happened, a little at a time, because everyone wanted to prove they could keep up."

It wasn't sabotage. It wasn't even, strictly, anyone's individual failure -- every worker on that crew had genuinely believed they were still sharp enough, the same way every crew before them had believed it right up until the moment they weren't. The threat wasn't the Directorate or the earth or a collapsing gallery. It was a culture that had quietly started rewarding endurance over judgment, and had done it so gradually that nobody working inside it had noticed the line moving.

He didn't discipline the crew. He rewrote the rotation schedule himself, hard caps enforced by the council rather than trusted to individual discretion, and made himself the first visible example of following it -- walking off a shift at the mandated hour even mid-task, handing an unfinished assessment to a rested relief crew rather than pushing through on will alone the way legend would have expected of him.

"People are going to say the Blue-Collar Titan got tired and quit early," one of the council representatives warned him, half-joking.

"Let them say it." Kanja handed off his tools without hesitation. "I'd rather be the story about a man who knew when to stop than the story about the crew that finally didn't wake up one morning."

The near-miss became the association's own cautionary case, cited in every training cycle after -- not as a failure anyone was punished for, but as the moment the crews collectively understood that the most dangerous shift was never the one under obvious attack. It was the one that felt, gradually and wrongly, like it was still fine.
