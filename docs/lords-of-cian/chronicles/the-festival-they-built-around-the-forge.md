# The Festival They Built Around the Forge

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LVII, wave 19, closing the wave. Rolling Foundry Campaign, age 27. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. Reuses the campaign's recurring senior smith; no new named characters.*

---

Nobody on the crew had planned the festival. It grew the way local custom sometimes grows around a
long-standing presence without anyone deciding to build it — a settlement's own autumn harvest
observance, already decades old, simply widening one season to include the forge crew that had by
then worked the district for the better part of a month, until the two occasions were no longer
easily told apart.

"We didn't ask for a holiday named after us," Kanja said, watching apprentices and settlement
children string dried gourds and hammered-tin bells between the terrace posts together, no clear
line anywhere between who belonged to which group.

"Nobody's naming it after you," the senior smith said, amused. "They're just folding you into the
one they already had. That's a different kind of honor than you're used to getting, and I'd argue
the better kind. Nobody builds a festival around a burning. People only ever build them around
things they mean to keep."

There was no threat anywhere in the day. No raid drill, no watch rotation beyond the ordinary, no
lesson dressed up as a story the way so much of the tour's teaching had been. The forge ran through
the afternoon not to finish a season's tool-stock or rebuild anything at all, but because a working
forge was, it turned out, most of what the festival actually wanted from it — sparks against the
evening light, apprentices showing off six weeks of hard-won technique to settlement families who'd
never seen iron worked up close, small trinkets struck to order and handed out for nothing more than
the asking.

Kanja spent the evening at the anvil taking requests — a child's toy hinge that opened and closed
for the sake of opening and closing, a good-luck nail bent into a ring for a coming wedding, a
useless, beautiful little iron bird that served no purpose the floor's usual judgment would have
called worth the heat it cost — and found himself, for the length of one evening, doing work that
answered to nothing but whoever was standing in front of him wanting it made.

"You could be rebuilding something right now," a settlement elder said, watching him finish the
bird and hand it to a delighted child who'd asked for exactly that and nothing more practical.

"I could be. Eighteen months of this tour, and it's mostly been fire meeting fire, one way or
another." He turned the last of the evening's heat down, banking it low rather than let it run
through the night. "Turns out the floor can also just make something because somebody asked for it
and it'll make them happy. Hadn't done that in a while. Forgot it counted as real work too."
