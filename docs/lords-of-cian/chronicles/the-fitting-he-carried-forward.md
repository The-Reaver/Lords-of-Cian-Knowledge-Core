# The Fitting He Carried Forward

*Locked canon, Batch 233, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1036`). The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LIX, wave
20. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The journeyman who arrived with a supply caravan eleven years after the Sewer War of Killane was
recognizable only once he spoke, and even then it took Kanja a moment — the fourteen-year-old who'd
demanded to be taught killing had grown into a broad-shouldered man of twenty-five with a level's worth
of calm in his hands, and a boy of his own age trailing a half-step behind him, carrying a satchel of
tools like it was the only thing he owned worth protecting.

"You won't remember the fitting," the journeyman said, by way of greeting, and held up a pipe joint
worn smooth from years of handling. "I kept it. Figured you'd want to know it worked."

"I remember the fitting." Kanja studied the boy behind him, maybe thirteen, with the particular
stillness of someone recently orphaned and not yet sure what to do with the anger underneath it — a
stillness he recognized from a much longer time ago than eleven years. "Who's this?"

"Found him three weeks after a raid on his district. Wanted me to teach him to fight." The journeyman
said it flatly, without irony, though the irony sat between the three of them regardless. "I didn't
have a speech for it the way you did. I just handed him a fitting and told him it was what I had to
offer, and that I'd learned from someone who meant it when he said the same thing to me."

"Did it work?"

"Ask him."

The boy hadn't said a word yet, turning the fitting he'd clearly been handed weeks earlier over in his
own hands, the same motion Kanja remembered from a stranger's tunnel years before either of them had
been in it — the same motion, he realized, that the journeyman must have practiced in front of him
without either of them noticing it become a habit. "He said the boring work matters," the boy said
finally, quiet, testing the phrase like it wasn't fully his yet. "I don't understand it. Not really.
But I don't want the blade anymore. I want to understand the tunnel."

"That's not smaller than wanting the blade," Kanja said, and watched the journeyman's mouth move with
the words half a beat ahead of him, already knowing them. "It just doesn't get you killed learning it,
and it actually adds up to something when the war's over and you're still here to see it."

The journeyman didn't correct him, didn't need to — he'd said the same sentence himself, three weeks
earlier, to a boy who hadn't yet decided whether to believe it. What struck Kanja, watching the two of
them stand there with the same fitting between three sets of hands across eleven years, wasn't that the
lesson had held. It was that he'd never taught it to be repeated. He'd only ever meant it for one boy,
once, in a tunnel with no one watching — and it had traveled anyway, carried forward by someone who'd
needed it enough to hand it to the next person who did.

"Teach him the joints," Kanja said. "You already know how. You've been doing it for weeks."
