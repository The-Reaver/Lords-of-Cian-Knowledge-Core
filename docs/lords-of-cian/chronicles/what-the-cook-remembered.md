# What the Cook Remembered

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Bane Alias Chronicle XXI, closing the seventh wave.
Rebellion era, age 19, months after the Black Trench. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The crew's cook had never fought at the Black Trench, never carried a charge for Pell Ostra or run a
diversion for Efa Gol, and had no story that belonged in the same accounts as any of that. What she
had, instead, after a year of feeding whatever column Bane happened to be moving with, was the
smaller, unremarked knowledge of what he actually ate, how much of it, and what he did with his
hands while he waited for it.

He mended his own clothes. Badly, at first — she'd watched him ruin a sleeve trying to close a tear
the wrong direction — and then less badly, the way anyone got better at a task nobody was going to
do for them. He ate whatever was put in front of him without asking for more or better, even on the
nights the column's supply ran thin enough that "whatever was put in front of him" was noticeably
less than what everyone else got, because he'd quietly instructed her weeks earlier to serve him
last and serve him least whenever supply ran short, and to never mention it to anyone who might turn
it into a story.

"You could eat better," she told him once, plainly, the way she told anyone anything. "You'd still
have enough left for the column even if you didn't take the smallest share every single time."

"I know. I'd rather not find out how easy it gets to stop noticing the difference." He finished the
plate she'd given him, thin as it was, without comment. "The day taking the smallest share stops
feeling like a choice is the day I've started believing the reputation instead of just using it."

She never told that story to anyone outside the cook fire's own small circle — it wasn't dramatic
enough to travel the way the gatepost rumors and the ravine standoffs traveled, and she suspected,
correctly, that he preferred it that way. What she kept, instead, in the plain private way a person
keeps the details nobody else thought worth asking about, was the specific, unglamorous shape of a
man who mended his own sleeves badly and ate last on purpose and never once let either become
something the legend could use.

"They'll write books about the ravine," she said to him once, years past when either of them
expected to still be saying anything to each other at all. "Nobody'll write one about the mending."

"Good." He'd meant it. "Let the mending stay mine."
