# The Twelve Locks of the Vault Door

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Iron Bastard Alias Chronicle XXXIV, first entry
in the twelfth wave. Rebellion era, age 25. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material — a
precision-extraction entry, the doctrine's first non-destructive use. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The records office held twelve rebel identities the Trust had compiled from informant testimony, each
name still unconfirmed, still salvageable if the list never reached the interrogators waiting to act
on it — and the vault door protecting the records was rigged with a twelve-point locking mechanism
that would jam permanently, sealing the documents inside for months, if any lock failed under force
rather than releasing in the correct sequence.

Kanja had spent three years learning to bring structures down. Tonight required something he'd never
deliberately practiced: opening one without damaging anything inside it.

He read the mechanism the way he'd read any tension-bearing structure — twelve separate resonance
signatures, each lock's own internal spring tension distinct from its neighbors — except that instead
of a single discharge collapsing everything at once, he needed each lock answered individually, in
the sequence its own tension implied, precise enough that release rather than failure was the result.

It took forty minutes, each lock diagnosed and coaxed open in turn, the discharge tuned so finely to
each one's specific signature that Mafesto's usual role — absorbing overflow force — had nothing to
absorb at all. No impact. No collapse. Just twelve points of tension, released one at a time, until
the door swung open on documents no interrogator would ever read.

"I've never seen you work this slowly on purpose," the rebel courier said, watching him seal the
vault shut again behind them to buy time before the theft was discovered.

"Most of what I do with this doctrine is about understanding a structure well enough to know exactly
where to break it. Tonight was about understanding one well enough to know exactly how not to. Turns
out that's a harder discipline than the other one, not an easier one — every lock I got slightly wrong
would have jammed the whole mechanism instead of just failing to open it."

"Would you have collapsed the door if you'd run out of time?"

"I'd have had to. I'm glad I didn't need to find out what that would have cost the documents inside."
