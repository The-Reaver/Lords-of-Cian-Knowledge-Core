# Lauris Chronicle LIII: The Archive He Never Let Anyone Touch

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1670`). Fifty-third entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of a second Strand W (Witness / present-day, quiet register) push. A
stakes-free entry pairing Lauris with Fermand Aurelias himself, stepping the narrator partly into
his own frame for the first time in the series -- he continues narrating in the established
third-person Baroque/Zafón-Noir register per `CC-034`/`VB-024` even while describing his own
actions, rather than switching to first person. Puts `CC-134`'s combat-joy trait on the page in

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Fermand has kept every page himself for
thirty years and has never once asked anyone to help carry them. I counted the crates today.
Forty-one. He counted them differently. He said 'enough.' I did not argue with the number he
chose."*

I will note a peculiarity of this account before I begin it properly, so that a reader is not left
wondering at it partway through. Fermand Aurelias appears in the pages that follow as a participant
rather than only as the hand that sets them down, and he intends to hold to exactly the same
discipline in describing himself that he has held to for thirty years in describing everyone else:
the third person, the clinical remove, no confession dressed up as narration. Fermand was there. He
did not care for being there. He is writing it down regardless, because leaving a true thing out of
the record on the grounds that it happened to him would be a worse failure of the account than any
small discomfort the telling costs him now.

The archive room beneath his own quarters had not been properly reorganized in perhaps eleven
years — not through neglect, precisely, but through the ordinary attrition of a man who adds to a
thing daily and reshelves nothing, on the theory that there will always be a slower month in which
to do it properly. There was not, as it turned out, ever going to be a slower month. There were
forty-one crates of loose transcription, cross-referenced shorthand, and two decades of Ezio's
correspondence copied in Fermand's own hand, stacked against three walls in an order that made
sense to no one, including, by his own admission when pressed, Fermand himself.

Lauris had come down only to return a borrowed volume. She stood in the doorway for perhaps four
seconds, looking at the crates the way a woman looks at a debt she has just noticed is overdue, and
said, "This will take you weeks."

"It will take me weeks," Fermand agreed, "which is why I have been declining to begin it for
eleven years."

"It will take me an afternoon."

He informed her, with what he believed at the time was entirely reasonable firmness, that the
archive was his own domain, ordered by a logic legible only to him, and that a well-intentioned
afternoon of enthusiastic lifting was considerably more likely to produce a second, worse disorder
than to produce any order at all. He recognizes now, setting this down, that what he was actually
defending was not the archive's logic — the archive had no logic; he had simply memorized where
things badly were — but the narrower, more embarrassing fact that no one had ever offered to carry
something for him before, and he did not immediately know what to do with the offer beyond decline
it.

She did not accept the decline. She also did not argue with it, which he has since decided was the
more effective approach. She simply asked him to name, crate by crate, what belonged with what, and
began carrying while he named, and within perhaps twenty minutes it became apparent to him that
narrating the contents of forty-one crates from a chair was considerably less exhausting than
narrating them while also lifting them, and he sat down, and stayed sat, and continued.

I want to record plainly what it was to watch this, since I have spent thirty years watching her do
harder things and have never quite found the vocabulary for this particular register of her. She
carried crates that required both arms and her full attention, three and four at a stack, up a
narrow stair, for the better part of two hours, and she did it the way I have seen her cross a
threshold under fire — unhurried, entirely certain of her own capacity, visibly, unmistakably
enjoying the use of a body built without compromise for exactly this kind of total, uncomplicated
exertion. She was not performing patience for his benefit. She simply liked doing it. I have written
before, of an evening with Sephtis, that I watched her carry that same unqualified pleasure inward,
spent on nothing more dangerous than an unresolved question. Here it was spent on nothing more
dangerous than his own neglected shelving, and it looked, from the chair, exactly the same.

By the end of it the crates stood in four ordered rows against one wall instead of three disordered
ones against all three, labeled in her own economical hand rather than his own increasingly illegible
one, and Fermand found himself in the unfamiliar position of having nothing left to do with his
afternoon except sit in a room that finally made sense and watch someone else be pleased about it.

"You did not need to do this," he said, which he recognizes, writing it now, was not gratitude so
much as the closest approximation of gratitude his own habitual reserve permits him to produce
without rehearsal.

"I know," she said. "I wanted to."

He has thought, since, about how rarely anyone extends him a kindness with no ledger attached — he
has spent thirty years as the one who keeps the account of other people's debts and doesn't often
find himself owed anything in return, nor does he particularly expect to be. He does not know, fully,
what to do with an afternoon that cost him nothing and asked nothing back. He is aware, setting this
down, that the honest thing to do with it is simply to record that it happened, exactly as he would
record it for anyone else, and let the account stand on its own without further embellishment. So he
has. The crates remain in order. He has not moved them since.
