# What Stays After the First One

*Locked canon, Batch 215, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-937`). Bane Alias Chronicle LIII, wave 18. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The skirmish at the Drennic crossroads was small enough that Hask's ledger recorded it in two
lines, and it would have stayed a footnote if not for the boy who'd killed his first man in it —
seventeen, three weeks in the column, a farmer's son who'd walked off his own family's land rather
than be conscripted into the Directorate line he'd have faced otherwise. He'd fought well. Everyone
said so, and meant it, and none of it reached him at all.

Bane found him after, sitting apart from the fire the rest of the column had built without him,
turning a whetstone over in his hands without using it on anything.

"You did what the moment needed," Bane said, sitting down at a distance that didn't crowd him.

"I know that." The boy didn't look up. "I keep telling myself that and it doesn't move anything.
I thought it would feel like something. Like fear, or like winning. It doesn't feel like either
one. It just feels like a fact now, sitting where a person used to be able to sit."

Bane didn't offer him the easy version — that it would stop mattering, that time made it smaller.
He'd learned better than that a long time before this column existed. "It doesn't get lighter," he
said instead. "I used to think mine would. Mine didn't. What changes isn't the weight. It's that
you learn to carry it without it being the only thing you're carrying — you get room in your hands
for other things again, eventually. The weight doesn't go anywhere. You just stop needing both
hands for it."

"How long did that take you?"

"Longer than three weeks. I'm not going to lie to you about that either." He looked at the whetstone
still idle in the boy's hands. "Sharpen the blade. Not because it needs it tonight. Because your
hands need something to do that isn't this, and that's an honest use of them."

The boy sharpened the blade until well past when it needed it, and slept that night closer to the
fire than he had the night before, and Bane didn't tell anyone else what had passed between them,
because it hadn't been column business. It had been one man who remembered his own first time
telling a boy the truth about what didn't get easier, so the boy wouldn't spend years thinking
something was wrong with him for feeling the way he did.
