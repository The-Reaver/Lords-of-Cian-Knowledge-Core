# The Man They Punished for Standing Near Him

*Locked canon, Batch 255, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1128`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LXXI, wave 24.
Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. An entirely uninvolved bystander pays the reputation's price.*

---

Nobody could say afterward that the dockhand had done anything at all. He hadn't carried a message,
hadn't cooperated with the tally table, hadn't so much as raised his voice on any grievance the
district had ever brought forward. His entire offense, as far as anyone could reconstruct it, was
standing at a public well one afternoon when Kanja stopped to ask him where the nearest working pump
was, exchanged perhaps a dozen words with him, and moved on.

An owner's men found him that night and broke two fingers on his left hand, not as punishment for
anything he'd done, but as a message aimed past him entirely — a warning to anyone who might be seen
talking to the Trench Monarch, however briefly, however innocently, that proximity itself now carried
a cost.

Kanja heard about it the next morning from Efa Gol, who'd found the man being tended by his own
family and had said nothing to him about who'd sent her before confirming what had happened.

"He didn't do anything," she said. "That's the part that's going to be hard to sit with."

He sat with it anyway, at length, before he did anything about it. Every prior threat the reputation
had drawn down on the district had landed on someone with a reason attached — a cooperating tally
worker beaten for helping, a clerk targeted for testifying, people who had chosen, at some real risk,
to be involved. This was different in a way that made the usual response — protect the cooperator,
document the retaliation, let the method absorb the cost — not quite fit. There was no cooperation
here to protect. There was only a man who had answered a stranger's question about a water pump and
paid for it in two broken fingers.

What Kanja did, in the end, was walk the same stretch of dock publicly, alone, unarmed but for Onyx
at his hip, and stop to talk to a dozen more ordinary people who had nothing to do with the tally
table at all — a fishmonger, a child minding a stall, an old man mending nets — making no effort to
hide any of it, until the pattern of who he spoke to stopped meaning anything to anyone watching for
one. He couldn't undo the two broken fingers. What he could do was make the warning behind them
useless, by refusing to let ordinary proximity to him carry information worth punishing.

The dockhand's fingers healed crooked. He never asked for anything from the tally table, and never
received a payment he hadn't earned; the method had no figure to offer him, because no wage had ever
been stolen from him. Kanja visited him twice more over the following weeks anyway, for no reason he
gave and none that was asked for.

"You can't fix everything the name costs people," Hask said once, watching him go.

"No," Kanja agreed. "But I can make sure it stops being useful information."
