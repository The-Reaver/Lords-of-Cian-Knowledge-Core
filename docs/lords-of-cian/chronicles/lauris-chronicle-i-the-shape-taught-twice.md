# Lauris Chronicle I: The Shape Taught Twice

*Locked canon, Batch 292, 2026-09-16 (`MCD-1561`). First entry in Lauris Letitia's own Chronicle
series under the Character Chronicle Gameplan (`docs/lords-of-cian/character-chronicle-gameplan.md`),
her Tier 1 starting point. Resolves that document's open narrator sub-question: narrated by Fermand
Aurelias, per the already-locked `CC-034` ("Fermand narrates all Ezio and Lauris POV chapters in a
Baroque/Zafón-Noir voice") and `VB-024`'s voice spec (clinical/methodical, no slang, no contractions,
no panic ever, warmth reserved only for "My dear Ezio") -- distinct from both Onyx's Kanja narration
and the close-third register used for the homage-era territory Chronicles, since Lauris is core Lords
of Cian crew rather than a figure Kanja meets as a stranger. Dramatizes two of her least-shown traits
directly for the first time: the Density Saturation Inversion (`ARS-357`-`374` -- fuller saturation
makes her progressively *less* detectable, the inverse of every other density combatant) and her
defining combat-joy (`CC-134`, "She Who is Crowned with Joy"). Picks up the freshest live hook in her
own canon: Operation 12's Settlement K-447 (`MCD-1536`, locked Batch 291) left explicitly unresolved
who arranged the bodies or taught the resonance-keying technique. This entry deliberately deepens
that mystery rather than resolving it -- a second, unrelated circle was taught a degraded fragment of
the same method by an unnamed itinerant instructor decades ago, confirming the original actor is
still active and still teaching, without identifying them. No new named characters. Abad's approval:
"lock it."*

---

I have kept records for men who lied to themselves and men who lied to me, and in thirty years of
transcribing Ezio Valcari's correspondence I had never once seen him hand a report to Lauris Letitia
without first reading it himself. He handed her this one unread. That, more than anything in the
report, told me what it was.

The report came from a frontier holding two days inland of the Korren Highlands -- forty-one souls, a
tin-ore concession, nothing the Sovereign Trust's ledgers would miss for a season. A courier had found
the perimeter fence marked in white chalk: concentric rings, precise to the inch, the kind of
precision that comes from measurement rather than fear. No bodies yet. The courier had not gone past
the fence to look for any.

"You know this shape," Ezio said. Not a question.

"I buried it," Lauris said. "I did not destroy it."

She left within the hour, and I went with her, because someone has to write down what she will not
say to Sephtis until she has already decided what to do about it, and because I have found that a
report improves considerably when the man carrying it has watched the thing happen rather than heard
it secondhand.

*

I will not pretend I understood, on the ride out, what she was afraid of. Lauris is not, in my
experience, a woman who fears outcomes. She fears *repetition* -- the possibility that a thing she
thought singular was only the first instance she happened to witness. Seventy-three subjects had come
out of a buried resonance node at Settlement K-447, decades before I knew her, fed by a geometry
someone had arranged from the bodies of three hundred and forty murdered civilians. She had cleared
it. She had reburied the node rather than report it. She had never learned who possessed the keying
knowledge to build that shape in the first place, and in every year since, that absence has sat in her
personal archive as an unclosed account -- the one debt in a life otherwise ruthlessly accounted for.

We reached the holding at dusk on the second day. The chalk rings were still incomplete -- perhaps a
third of the pattern laid, fresh enough that the powder had not yet settled into the frost. Whoever
was building it had not finished, which meant they were still there, which meant, for the first time
in this particular account, Lauris would arrive before the ledger closed rather than after.

She did not wait for me to suggest caution. She rarely does. She crossed the open ground toward the
settlement's grain store at a walk, unhurried, the way she crosses any ground she intends to own by
the time she reaches the other side of it.

*

Six of them. Not soldiers -- I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only gift I
have to offer a reader who was not present. Six ordinary people, ore-weighers and a smith and a woman
who I later learned kept the settlement's grain accounts, standing over the unfinished rings with the
particular concentration of amateurs following instructions they do not fully understand. They had
rope. They had a single crude ward -- a strung lattice of bent nails and thread, the kind a
hedge-charlatan sells to farmers worried about blight -- hung at the store's threshold, tuned, badly,
to detect exactly the kind of density signature Lauris carries.

It worked, for the first four seconds. I watched the strung nails shiver as she crossed the
threshold, and I watched every one of the six turn toward the doorway with the flat certainty of
people who have been told precisely what to expect.

Then she kept walking, and the shiver stopped.

I have since had this explained to me twice, once by Sephtis and once -- more patiently -- by Lauris
herself, and I record it here as faithfully as I am able: her density does not simply increase under
pressure. It saturates. And past a certain threshold of saturation, the very density that should
announce her instead closes around her signature the way deep water closes around a stone dropped
into it -- the disturbance is greatest exactly where the detection is worst. Every combatant on Cian
who works in density becomes easier to find the harder they push. Lauris becomes, past that same
threshold, nearly impossible to hold in a ward built to catch the ordinary case. The six had built
their charm to catch a woman approaching at rest. By the time she reached them she was not at rest.
The nails had stopped shivering because there was, as far as their crude lattice could report,
nothing there to shiver at.

I will not dwell long on what followed, because it was brief, and because Lauris does not permit me
to write violence as though it were the interesting part of her. She disarmed the smith before his
hand closed on the rope-hook. She took the grain-keeper by the wrist rather than the throat. Within
perhaps ninety seconds the six were seated in a row against the store's own wall, uninjured, and the
seventh person in the room -- a man who had been standing apart from the others, directing rather
than laying chalk himself -- was on his knees with Lauris's hand resting, not gripping, on his
shoulder.

She was, I should note for the record, smiling. Not the smile of a woman enjoying cruelty. The smile
of a woman doing, competently and at full capacity, the one thing her body was built without
compromise to do. I have watched her smile like that at a boarding action, at a training bout with
Valen, once at a burst water main she repaired with her bare hands rather than wait for the
engineers. Joy, unqualified, in the use of a body that has never once betrayed her. It is, I have come
to think, the strangest thing about her, and the thing I understand least, and the thing I am most
careful to record accurately, because everyone who meets her expects grief and gets this instead.

*

The kneeling man gave up little, and what he gave was worse than nothing. He had not devised the
shape himself. He had been taught it -- the geometry, the keying sequence, the precise phrase to
arrange the bodies in -- by an itinerant instructor who had passed through the holding some thirty
years prior, calling the technique "insurance" against a debt collector no one in the settlement had
ever met. He did not know the instructor's name. He did not know whether the instructor was still
alive, or where, or how many other circles like this six had also been taught the same insurance in
the same itinerant passing.

Lauris did not press him further. She has told me, since, that pressing a frightened man past the
edge of what he actually knows produces confident lies rather than honest gaps, and she would rather
carry the gap.

She let all six of them live, on the condition -- self-enforced, not negotiated -- that the frontier
holding's chalk was washed from the ground before the frost set again, and that word of the shape's
real cost, seventy-three dead at Settlement K-447, was told openly rather than kept as a secret worth
insuring against.

*

We rode back in near silence. I asked her, once, whether this closed the account.

"No," she said. "It confirms there is more than one page in the ledger."

I wrote that down exactly as she said it, because I have learned, across thirty years of
transcription, that the sentences Lauris chooses not to embellish are the ones that matter most.
Someone taught that shape to a circle of frightened tin-weighers three decades ago, and if this
account exists, others surely do. The person who arranged three hundred and forty bodies at
Settlement K-447 is not dead, not caught, and not, as far as either of us could determine that
night, finished teaching.

She has not closed the account. She has, for the first time in longer than I have known her,
admitted that it may never close on her own timetable. I record this because I believe it is the
truest thing I have ever written about her: Lauris Letitia does not fear an unfinished war. She fears
an unfinished ledger. And this one, I think, she now understands she may carry for the rest of a very
long life.
