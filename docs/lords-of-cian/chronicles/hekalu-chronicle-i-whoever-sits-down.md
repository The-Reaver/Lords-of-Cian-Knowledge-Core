# Hekalu Chronicle I: Whoever Sits Down

*Locked canon, Batch 90, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-353`). First entry in Hekalu's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Adom (`PH2-055`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`) through Taifa Chronicle I (`MCD-352`). Narration is
close-third on Adom throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Continues Detroit's own run of territory
Chronicles. Slots into no existing mainline battle — original homage-era material set in Hekalu
itself.*

---

Adom set a table the way other men set traps, except that a trap closed on something whether it
wanted to be caught or not, and Adom's table only ever closed on someone who'd chosen, freely, to sit
down at it. He had learned this distinction the hard way, years before, preaching a gospel of
liberation to men who nodded along and changed nothing about how they lived once the sermon ended.
Bread did what sermons never had.

The man who came that evening had no reason to trust the invitation. He ran a rival cooperative two
blocks over, undercutting Hekalu's own bakery on price by paying his workers less and calling it
efficiency, and he had spent a year assuming Adom's table was a trick he simply hadn't spotted the
mechanism of yet.

"I know what people say happens once you eat at this table," the man said, standing in the doorway,
not yet inside it. "That a man walks in a stranger and walks out owing something he never agreed to
owe."

"Nobody owes anything who doesn't sit down and eat first." Adom gestured to the empty chair, not
pressing it. "I can't make you sit. I've never been able to make anyone sit. Whatever you've heard
about what happens after — that part's true. The part before it, the part where you choose to come
to this table at all, that part was always going to be entirely yours."

The stranger was already at the table when the man finally sat, having arrived an hour earlier the
way he arrived at most things, unannounced, and taken a seat without being asked to leave it. He ate
the way he did most things too — present, unhurried, offering nothing that could be mistaken for an
opinion on the negotiation unfolding two seats down.

The rival ate slowly, testing every word Adom offered across the meal the way a man tests a floor he
doesn't trust before putting his full weight on it. By the meal's end, something had shifted that
neither man could have named cleanly if asked — not agreement exactly, something underneath
agreement, a thread now running between the table and the man that hadn't been there when he'd
walked in suspicious of the whole enterprise.

He came back three days later, unprompted, with a proposal neither of them had discussed at the
table itself: a joint wage floor across both cooperatives, undercutting no one, a thing his own
ledger said he couldn't afford and did anyway.

"You didn't ask me for that," he said, sliding the proposal across the table. "I don't fully
understand why I'm offering it."

"You ate at this table. That's the whole of the mechanism, as much as I understand it myself." Adom
read the proposal without surprise, the way a man reads something he half-expected without ever once
counting on it. "I don't put anything into the bread. I've had it tested, by people who trust me
less than you did a week ago. It's only ever been bread. Whatever it does happens somewhere past the
bread itself, in whatever a person actually brings to the table when they finally sit down at it
honestly."

The stranger, still at the table, said nothing, and Adom had long since stopped expecting him to.

There was a man once, early in the table's history, who'd sat down meaning to mock the whole
practice — ate the meal with a smirk he never fully dropped, left the same evening exactly as
suspicious as he'd arrived, and nothing in him had shifted at all. Adom kept that memory close,
precisely because it proved the limit as plainly as the rival's proposal proved the gift: a man who
sat down without ever actually meaning to be there walked out unchanged, every time, no matter how
long the meal ran.

"It only ever works on someone who actually chose to be here," Adom said, once the rival had gone.
"Not someone forced to the table. Not someone humoring me for an hour. Someone who sat down and
actually let themselves eat." He looked at the empty chairs, already being reset for the next
evening's table. "That's not a weakness in it. It's the only reason anyone's ever trusted it enough
to sit down twice."

The stranger was gone before the next evening's table was set, the way he always seemed to be. He
left nothing carved, no phrase repeated hand to hand — only, the following week, one more empty
chair added to the table without anyone remembering who'd asked for it.
