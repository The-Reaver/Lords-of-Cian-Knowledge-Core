# The Funeral Efa Gol Asked Him to Speak At

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Captain Alias Chronicle LI, wave 17. Rebellion-adjacent era. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters — reuses already-locked Efa Gol; one unnamed deceased founding crew member.*

---

The old rigger had been with the crew since before there was a crew worth the name — one of the first
hands taken on at Pier Nine, back when the whole operation was a handful of dockworkers and a rumor.
He died the ordinary way, in his sleep, in a bunk he'd earned the right to keep even after his knees
gave out and his climbing days ended, and the crew treated the news the way it treated every other
loss it had learned to carry: quietly, and together.

Efa Gol found Kanja the morning after. "He asked for you, before. Not to save him — he knew there
wasn't anything to save him from. He asked if you'd speak at the service. Said you were the only one
left who remembered him from before he was anybody worth remembering."

"I'm not good at this kind of speaking."

"None of us are. That's not the same as not doing it."

The service was held on the deck at dawn, the way the crew had come to bury its own — no Directorate
ceremony, no formal rites borrowed from any Trust or Kingdom, just whoever could stand a watch off to
gather and say what needed saying. Kanja stood last, after Corren Halst had spoken of the rigger's
knots, after two hands who'd apprenticed under him had spoken of his patience, after Efa Gol had said
simply that he never once complained about a task beneath him.

"I met him the week I had nothing," Kanja said, when it was his turn. "No name worth using, no crew,
no plan past the next day. He didn't ask what I was going to become. He handed me a coil of line and
showed me how to splice it properly, because the deck he was building didn't have room for anyone who
didn't know how, and he wasn't going to make an exception for anybody, including whatever I might
someday turn into. I've been given a great many names since then. He never once used any of them. He
called me by the name I actually had, the whole time I knew him, because he'd decided that was the
one that mattered."

He paused, and for once didn't try to smooth the silence over with anything practiced.

"I don't know how to promise a man like that his name won't be forgotten. I can only promise it won't
be forgotten by me, and that as long as I'm the one still standing here, that's going to have to be
enough."

The crew committed him to the sea the old way, without ceremony beyond the doing of it, and the deck
went back to its watches within the hour — not because the loss was small, but because the rigger
himself had never once, in decades of service, wanted a day's work stopped on his account, and the
crew honored that the only way it knew how: by getting back to it.
