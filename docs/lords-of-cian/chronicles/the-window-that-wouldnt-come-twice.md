# The Window That Wouldn't Come Twice

*Locked canon, Batch 270, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1406`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle XCI, wave 31, first
entry. Age 313, V4 gear. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

Efa Gol's successor had scouted the route herself, twice, the year before -- a stretch of coastal
road running between two garrisoned checkpoints, passable only on the eleven nights a year the tide
cut it in half, timed to a patrol schedule no commander had bothered adjusting in decades. She'd
handed Kanja the details the same night he'd sat with the Forge-Coat folded across his knees,
deciding against deciding. Three weeks later, the window opened on schedule, and this time he went
himself.

He hadn't led a field operation personally in years, content to let the successor's own
three-diversion doctrine run without him the way it had been built to run. This one he wanted his own
hands on -- not because she needed the help, but because the choice to put the coat back on had been
specific to this: eighty-one people behind a compound wall who had no way of knowing a year-long
window existed at all, let alone that it was three weeks from closing.

Pell Ostra timed the breach to the minute the tide finished turning, three charges set to open the
seaward wall without touching the load-bearing arch the captives were held beneath -- work she'd done
a hundred times and still measured twice, murmuring numbers to the charges the way she always had.
The successor ran her diversion up the patrol road, drawing both checkpoints' attention north exactly
long enough. Kanja went in through the breach alone, moved eighty-one people down a stretch of
exposed beach in under forty minutes, and was back aboard before either checkpoint had finished
investigating a diversion that, by the time they understood it as one, no longer mattered.

Efa Gol met him at the rail when it was done, the way she had a hundred times before, and said nothing
about the folded coat or the three weeks of silence that had come before it. Garren Hask logged
eighty-one names into a ledger that, by his own count, now ran past two hundred and seventy-nine years
without missing a single one. Neither of them asked whether it had been worth choosing to stay for.
The number, this time, was its own answer.
