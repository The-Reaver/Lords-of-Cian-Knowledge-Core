# Daba Chronicle XXXI: The One He Picked

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1601`). Entry 31 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes the

Daba had been turning the Rexmar patterning over in his own hands for the better part of a year before he let anyone else near it, and even then he did not choose the way he suspected most men would have chosen. He did not go looking for the strongest arm in the network, or the fastest, or even the one who wanted it most — wanting it most, he had learned long before he had a name for the lesson, was usually its own kind of danger. He went looking, instead, for the ones who could hold a secret the way a covered well holds water: completely, without a ripple showing on the surface, for as long as it took.

Coen was a boy of the Fenmark salt-flats who had apprenticed three years under a tinker before the tinker's cart was seized at a Trust checkpoint for want of a stamp nobody had told him he needed. He had not gone home angry. He had gone home quiet, and stayed quiet for two years, mending what needed mending for people who could pay him in bread and never once mentioning the tinker's name to anyone who asked. Daba had watched him for four months before he ever spoke to him about anything beyond the weather and the state of a hinge.

Sabeth he found a hundred miles east, in a river town whose name did not appear on any Trust map worth the ink, working a forge that belonged, officially, to her uncle, and in practice to her alone since the uncle's hands had stopped answering him three winters back. She spoke less than Coen did. She had buried two brothers in the same season the Rookery burned and had never once, in Daba's hearing, said the word aloud.

He taught them the same technique and told each of them nothing about the other. That part mattered more than the forging itself, though it took Coen a long while to understand why. "You could save us both a season," he said once, turning a half-finished vambrace in the light of Fenmark's low winter sun, "if you let me talk to whoever else you're teaching this to. Compare notes. Go faster."

"I could," Daba agreed, and did not do it.

"Why not?"

"Because the day someone puts a knife to your throat and asks who else knows what you know, I want the honest answer to be one name. Mine. Not two." He took the vambrace from him and turned it over, checking the temper line along its inner curve with a thumbnail, the way Kanja had once checked his own work in front of him without a word of explanation, trusting Daba to understand the gesture would teach more than the sentence would have. "You're not being taught a trade, Coen. You're being taught a branch. Branches don't need to know about each other to hold the same tree up."

He said something close to the same thing to Sabeth three weeks later, standing in her uncle's forge with the shutters closed against a town that did not need to see who visited her after dark. She took it better than Coen had, or worse — it was hard to tell with her, and he had stopped trying. She only nodded, set the tongs down, and asked when he'd be back to check her angle on the socket-weld, which he understood to mean she had already decided the reasoning wasn't hers to argue with, only his to be right about.

He was not, if he let himself think honestly about it in the hours he allowed himself for honest thinking, entirely certain yet that either of them would hold the line the way he needed them to, years from now, under conditions worse than a closed shutter and a quiet town. That was not a thing a year of watching could answer. It was a thing only the years after would answer, one at a time, the way every real test of a person's silence eventually did. What he was certain of was narrower and, he thought, sufficient: that he had picked two people who already knew, in their bones and before he ever arrived, what it cost to keep a thing to yourself. He had not taught them that. He had only found it, and built on top of it, the way Kanja had once told him you built a forge — not by choosing the strongest ground, but by finding ground that had already proven it would hold.
