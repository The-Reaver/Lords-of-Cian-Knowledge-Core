# Xaragua Chronicle III: Where the Table Began

*Locked canon, Batch 98, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-361`). Third Xaragua Chronicle written, but
chronologically the earliest of all three by
decades -- it precedes both Xaragua Chronicle II (`MCD-337`, itself already chronologically first
among the previously-locked pair) and Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`), matching the same
write-order-versus-in-universe-order pattern `MCD-337` already established. Protagonist Arturo
Salvatierra Duho (`PH2-061`), decades before "de la Muerte" -- this is the flagged prequel material
Batch 67 held open: "the intended place to show the vulnerable, breaking version of him before this
stillness was earned." Dramatizes the founding of "No Blood at My Table" and the origin of the loss
`PH2-061` already locks (the dock-boy cohort lost to war and Xaragua's own street war, leaving only
Arturo and Yaisa). An unnamed Kanja appears only at the margins, one indistinct face among many
helping in the aftermath, with no dialogue and no interaction with Arturo -- consistent with why
Arturo does not recognize or remember him by name decades later in Xaragua Chronicle II. Yaisa
(`PH2-062`) appears as a survivor, not yet as Arturo's second-in-command.*

---

He was gone eleven days, running the western supply line the way he'd run it a dozen times before,
and came back to a district that no longer had a single one of the faces in it he'd left behind.

He knew before he reached the block. He knew from the particular quality of the quiet, the kind of
quiet that only ever settles over a street after something has finished happening in it rather than
before something is about to. He walked the last stretch anyway, because there was no version of
turning around that changed what he would find, only a version of finding it slower.

Nzila was in the doorway of the room they'd all slept in, at one time or another, across six years of
being the closest thing any of them had to a family. Tunde was two streets over. Bendu had made it as
far as the water before whatever had caught him caught up. Arturo would not, for the rest of a very
long life, ever fully lose the specific order in which he found them, block by block, though he
would spend decades learning not to say their names aloud in the wrong company.

He found Yaisa last, and alive, in the cellar under the dry-goods store, where she had dragged
herself after the fighting moved past her and left her for finished. She was not finished. He knelt
beside her in the dark and pressed both hands flat against a wound in her side that should have
killed her an hour before he ever reached her, and felt something move through his own hands into
her that he had no name for and had never once tried to summon on purpose — a warmth, a pressure, a
sense of something in her body answering something in his that neither of them had asked to exist.

The bleeding stopped.

He would spend years afterward trying to understand what he'd actually done in that cellar, and never
fully manage it, only ever manage to do it again, more deliberately, when it mattered enough. What he
understood immediately, kneeling there with his hands still pressed against a wound that was healing
under them in a way no wound should, was smaller and worse than any of that: it had worked on her,
finally, when there had been someone left alive to use it on. It had done nothing at all for six other
people he'd known his entire adult life, because he had not been there, and because whatever this was
in his hands had apparently been waiting, unclaimed and unknown to him, for exactly the one night it
would have mattered most and hadn't been anywhere near enough of them.

There were people in the street by the time he carried her up out of the cellar — neighbors, dock
hands, a handful of strangers who had simply come because a street this quiet the morning after a
street war this loud draws people the way a wound draws hands, wanting to help with whatever's left
to be helped. One of them, a man Arturo would not remember clearly enough to describe a week later,
carried water down the block without being asked and helped an old woman move a fallen gate off her
own doorway, and was gone again by the time Arturo thought to ask anyone his name.

The man who had started it — who had let a dispute over docking fees become a street war rather than
swallow the smaller loss, and had done the arithmetic that six dead boys was an acceptable price for
winning it — held court three days later at his own table two blocks over, entirely unconcerned,
because men like him rarely paid for arithmetic like that themselves.

Arturo went to that table with Yaisa still healing behind him and nothing in his hands but the same
thing that had saved her life in the cellar, and found, kneeling across from a man who had never once
raised a hand against him directly, that the same gift ran two directions if a man let it.

It was quick. It was, by every account Arturo ever gave of it afterward, in the very few rooms he
ever spoke of it in at all, the only time in a very long life he ever let that particular door open
all the way, and the last thing he remembered clearly before the gray came into his own hair that
had not been there the week before was understanding, with a clarity that never entirely left him
again, exactly what it had cost him to use it, and exactly how easily a man could spend the whole of
himself paying debts that never actually stopped accruing.

"You look older," Yaisa said, when he came back to her, three days into a healing that was not yet
finished. She had not asked where he'd gone. She had not needed to.

"I am older. That part isn't a trick of the light." He sat beside her, the two of them the entire
surviving population of something that had, eleven days before, been six people deep. "I don't
intend to spend myself that way again if I can help it. Once was already more than I understood the
price of when I agreed to pay it."

"Then what do you intend."

He was quiet a long while, the particular quiet of a man building something in the space where
grief had been living, because grief on its own does not hold a shape and a man cannot carry it
indefinitely without something to pour it into. "A table," he said, finally. "Not this one. Mine.
Whoever sits at it is safe there, completely, the way none of us were safe in that room the night it
mattered. I can't undo what happened to six people I loved because I wasn't standing close enough to
them. I can build the one thing that makes sure it never happens again to anyone who sits close
enough to me to matter."

"That's not grief. That's a debt."

"It's both. I don't see the use in choosing between them." He looked at her, the only other person
left alive who would ever fully understand what that street had cost either of them, and found, in
her, the first true test of the thing he'd just decided to build. "You're the first to sit at it.
There isn't a table yet. There's just you, and me, and whatever this becomes."

She took his hand, the same hand that had saved her life in that cellar and ended another man's three
days later, and held it the way a person holds the one piece of a thing that survived, and neither of
them said anything else that night that either would ever repeat to anyone.
