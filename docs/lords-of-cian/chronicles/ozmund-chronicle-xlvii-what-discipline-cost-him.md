# What Discipline Cost Him

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1776`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XLVII, second entry of the closing coming-of-age strand, set strictly pre-
Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. Dramatizes a real personal cost of the
"no threshold to cross" discipline (`CC-015`/`017`/`MCD-024`) for the first time in the series — a
near-friendship Ozmund deliberately keeps at a controlled distance across several years of his
father's twice-yearly road circuits (extending the custom established in Chronicle I,
`MCD-1730`), and loses to ordinary circumstance before he ever lets it become what it could have been.
Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. Introduces Joren, a wheelwright's
apprentice at the grain markets — a new minor named character, collision-checked clean against the
full live ledger. No other new named characters.*

---

Not every story the General gave me had an enemy in it. I want to say that plainly, because it would
be easy to hear thirty years of his history and assume every cost he ever paid was paid to someone
trying to hurt him. This one he paid to no one. He told me that was, in its way, the part that stayed
with him longest — that the thing he lost, he lost to nothing crueler than his own good sense, applied
exactly as it was meant to be applied, and that this made it very hard to be angry at anyone about it,
including himself.

He met the boy Joren the way he met most of the world outside the House walls in those years — on one
of his father's circuits, at the grain markets Aethelgard still insisted on walking twice a year so
that neither of them would forget what the walls were built to protect. Joren was apprenticed to a
wheelwright whose stall sat along the same row the Verehimu party passed through each visit, a year or
two older than Ozmund by the reckoning that mattered least between them, loud in the particular way of
boys who have never once had to consider whether the wrong word might cost them something. He did not
know who Ozmund was, not truly — knew only that a well-dressed boy from somewhere upriver kept turning
up twice a year with time on his hands and a willingness to be put to work on a wheel-rim, which was
more than most well-dressed boys ever offered. That was enough. It became, over four or five such
visits, something the General was careful never to call a friendship out loud, though he told me it
was the nearest thing to one he'd had up to that point in his life that owed nothing to his name.

He told me what discipline looked like from the inside of it, in that particular friendship, and it
was smaller and more constant than I had ever pictured. He never let Joren beat him at anything that
mattered, not because he needed to win but because he could not be certain, in the heat of a wrestling
match or a footrace down the market row, exactly how much of himself he was actually holding back, and
the uncertainty itself was the danger — so he held back further than the danger required, every time,
without exception, until "holding back" was simply the only way he knew how to be near another boy his
own age. He never stayed past dusk, even when invited, even when the wheelwright's household would
have fed him and put him up without a second thought. He never explained why. He told me he'd practiced
a dozen different excuses across those years and never used the true one even once, because the true
one required explaining what he was, and what he was, was the one thing in his life he had already
decided no one his own age was going to be allowed to know unless they had to.

Joren noticed. Of course he noticed — the General was clear with me that he had never believed
otherwise, even at the time. A boy who is watched that closely for four or five visits running, who is
always the one leaving first, always the one who won't quite let a wrestling match run its full course,
always polite in a way that starts, eventually, to feel like distance rather than manners — that boy
knows something is being withheld from him, even if he has no way to guess what. The General told me
Joren asked him about it exactly once, near the end, half joking and half not: whether he thought
himself too good for a wheelwright's row, whether it would have killed him to just say so if that was
the truth of it, rather than keep showing up and keep leaving.

He didn't answer. He told me that was the part he regretted most cleanly of anything in the whole
account — not the years of distance, which he still believed, even telling it to me decades later, had
been the only sound choice available to him at the time, but that single silence, in that single
moment, when a true answer might have cost him nothing at all and he gave a false one instead out of
sheer habit, because the habit had gotten that deep by then.

The wheelwright's household moved on before the next circuit came around — an apprenticeship contract
taken up in Khorvane, better terms than the grain markets could offer, nothing more sinister than
ordinary opportunity — and by the time Aethelgard's party passed that row again, the stall belonged to
someone else, and no one there could say with any certainty where the family had gone. The General told
me he stood in that row for longer than the visit required, long enough that his father noticed and
said nothing, and understood, standing there, something about the shape of what he'd built for himself:
that the discipline had done exactly what it was designed to do, kept him safe, kept his secret, kept
no one in danger who hadn't chosen the danger — and that it had also, with the same quiet efficiency,
cost him the one person who had ever wanted to know him for nothing more complicated than a boy who was
good with a wheel-rim and terrible at losing gracefully. He told me he never tried to find Joren again.
He told me he thought about him more than he ever let anyone know, for longer than seemed reasonable
for so small a thing, and that it taught him something about the exact price of the life he was going
to have to keep choosing, over and over, for reasons that were sound every single time and never once
made the choosing painless.
