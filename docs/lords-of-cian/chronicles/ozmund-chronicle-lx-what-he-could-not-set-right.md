# What He Could Not Set Right

series, Chronicle LX — the Aethelgard strand's third-wave opening entry, set strictly pre-Book-1,

He told me about Sennick only once, and unlike most of what he gave me only once, he didn't ask me to
keep it quiet. I think, looking back, that was itself the point of the telling — that some things his
father carried, he wanted repeated exactly as they'd been given to him, precisely because keeping them
quiet had been the original mistake.

Sennick sat three days upriver from the nearest road, reachable in low water only by a narrow channel
that closed itself entirely for six weeks most winters — a settlement of perhaps two hundred souls that
the House governed mostly by trusting the district steward's twice-yearly report to say what needed
saying. That was, the General told me, simply how a country that size worked. No lord governs every
acre in person, and Aethelgard, for all his riding out to check what didn't sit right with him, could
not ride out to check everything. He had learned to trust the mechanism. That year, the mechanism
failed him.

A wasting fever moved through Sennick in early winter, the kind that starts slow enough to look like an
ordinary bad season and only shows its true shape once it's already too late to call it anything else.
The steward's report, when it finally came — delayed once by the closing channel, delayed a second time
by the steward's own judgment that it could wait for the spring circuit rather than warrant a special
rider — reached Aethelgard nearly two months after the first deaths. By the time he read it, seventeen
people were already gone, more than a tenth of the settlement, most of them its youngest children.

He went himself. Not with the column he usually brought on circuit, the General said, but alone, on a
boat he rowed the last stretch of himself once the channel narrowed too far for anything larger,
because he would not make the people of Sennick wait one more day for him to arrange an escort. What he
found when he arrived wasn't a problem waiting to be solved. The fever had already burned through and
mostly passed. There was no levee to walk, no formula to correct on the spot, no single order that
would put back what was already gone. For the first time in anything the General ever told me about his
father, Aethelgard arrived somewhere with nothing left to fix.

He stood in front of what remained of Sennick — fewer people than he usually addressed on a good
circuit, the General said, and every one of them looking at him the way people look at a lord who came
too late — and he didn't do what the boy, watching, half expected. He didn't blame the closed channel.
He didn't blame the steward's judgment. He didn't offer the kind of grief that costs a man nothing to
perform. He told them plainly that the House's own arrangement for hearing what happened at the edges
of its own land had failed them — that a report allowed to wait for spring was a report built for a
country where nothing urgent ever happened at its farthest points, and Sennick had just proven that
assumption wrong at a cost he could never repay. He said he was building something faster before the
winter was out — a standing relay, riders and boats kept ready specifically for the settlements the
channel could cut off, paid from his own purse rather than the district's, so the next Sennick, wherever
it turned out to be, would never again wait two months to be heard. And then, the General said, his
father did the thing that stayed with him longest: he said, in front of all of them, that the relay
wouldn't help the seventeen who were already gone, that nothing he was about to order changed that, and
that he wasn't going to stand there and let anyone mistake fixing the system for having fixed what had
actually happened.

The General told me it was the first time he understood that some of what his father governed couldn't
be set right, only answered for. Every other story he'd carried about Aethelgard's mistakes — Greyfen's
levee, a treasurer's formula — ended with something repaired, some number corrected, the wrong made
whole again by the time the story was over. Sennick didn't end that way. The relay got built. It
worked, as far as the General ever knew, for the rest of his father's life. But he said his father
visited Sennick every single year after that, without fail, long after the settlement had any practical
need of the visit — not because it needed him anymore, but because he had decided, once, standing in
front of what was left of it, that a debt you cannot pay off doesn't stop being a debt just because
no one is asking you for it anymore.
