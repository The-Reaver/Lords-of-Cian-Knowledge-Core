# What the Senior Smith Set Down

*Locked canon, Batch 262, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1312`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LXVI, wave
22, closing the wave. Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241). An unplanned
institutional-discontinuity entry, distinct from the successor's own established training bookend
(MCD-887). Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The senior smith's hands had been shaking for three weeks before she said anything about it, and
she only said something at all because a settlement apprentice noticed a hammer come down a finger's
width off true and asked, with no unkindness in it, if she was well. She was not well. It had a
name the campaign's own healers gave her plainly, once she finally let them look — a wasting in the
joints that no forge-side remedy touched, that would only get worse, and that made the difference
between a hammer landing true and a hammer landing on someone's hand a matter of when, not if.

She told Kanja herself, at the end of a working day, before she told anyone else. "I've trained her
for six years," she said, meaning the successor everyone on the terrace already deferred to without
being told to. "I told myself that was so she'd be ready when I was old. I don't think I meant
this soon."

"Nobody means any of it soon," Kanja said. "That doesn't make her less ready."

There was no ceremony to it, because the senior smith didn't want one — no speech, no formal
handing-over of a title that had never been official in the first place, just an ordinary morning
where she stood at the terrace's edge with her arms folded instead of at the anvil with a hammer in
them, watching her successor run the floor exactly the way she'd been running half of it unofficially
for a year already. The apprentices adjusted inside a single shift, the way people adjust to a
change they'd half-expected without being told to expect it.

"It doesn't feel like losing anything," the senior smith admitted later, surprising herself with it.
"I thought it would."

"That's because you didn't build something that needed you standing in front of it forever," Kanja
said. "You built something that only needed you standing in front of it long enough."

She stayed on the terrace after that — teaching what could still be taught with hands that could no
longer be trusted with a hammer, correcting angles by eye instead of by demonstration, present every
day in a role nobody had a name for yet and nobody seemed to need one for.
