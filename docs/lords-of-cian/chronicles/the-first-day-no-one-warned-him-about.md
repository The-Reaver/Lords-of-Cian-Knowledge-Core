# The First Day No One Warned Him About

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle XXXIX, closing
the thirteenth wave. Rebellion era, age 20, the Sewer War of Killane. Not a territory Chronicle.
Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The newest recruit was sixteen, eager in the specific way of someone who'd heard every story about
the crew's fighting and none of the stories about the actual work, and his first morning underground
he arrived expecting to be handed a weapon.

Kanja handed him a bucket and a measuring cord instead.

"I thought I was joining a fight," the boy said, turning the bucket over with obvious
disappointment.

"You are. This is most of it." Kanja led him toward the section that needed surveying before any
digging could safely proceed. "The stories that reach the surface are all about the parts that look
like a fight. Nobody tells the version where most days down here are measuring, hauling, checking a
brace twice before trusting it once. That's not a lesser version of the war. It's the actual shape
of it, most hours of most days."

"When do I get to fight?"

"When there's something worth fighting and nothing else left to do about it. Not before. Fighting
first, thinking second, gets people killed down here faster than almost anything else I've seen." He
showed the boy how to hold the measuring cord taut against the tunnel wall, patient with the
fumbling first attempt. "I know that's not the story you came in expecting. I'd rather you leave this
crew disappointed in the story than leave it in a box because I let you believe the war was mostly
the exciting part."

By midday the boy had measured four sections, badly at first and then with genuine improving care,
and asked, unprompted, whether the next day's work would look the same.

"Some of it. Some days it looks like today. Some days it looks like the fight you came in wanting."
Kanja checked the boy's last measurement against his own, close enough to trust with correction.
"The trick is not needing today to be exciting for it to matter. Every measurement you took right
today is one less thing that goes wrong later, for someone who's trusting a tunnel they'll never
know you helped survey."

The boy stayed the full six weeks, fought exactly twice in that span, and spent the other days
doing precisely the unglamorous work he'd arrived hoping to skip — carrying, by the siege's end, a
quieter pride in the measuring cord than he'd ever expected to feel about a bucket.
