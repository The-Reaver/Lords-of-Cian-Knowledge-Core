# Sankofa Chronicle IV: What the Clinic Wasn't Told

*Locked canon, Batch 225, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1023`). Fourth Sankofa territory Chronicle, second to
touch the forged-letter conspiracy from Chronicle II (`MCD-360`). Protagonist Baálé (`PH2-021`), not
a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as an unnamed guest, present and observant but granted no
command, credit, or resolution authorship. Deliberately deepens the conspiracy rather than resolving
it, consistent with `PH2-021`'s own framing of a threat that "never shows its face." No new named
characters.*

---

The clinic had been open four months when the pamphlets started.

They did not arrive the way the letter had — folded, addressed, delivered to one man's hand in the
dark. They arrived nailed to walls, tucked under doors, left in stacks at the market three streets
from the clinic's entrance, where anyone waiting for water or bread might pick one up without
meaning to. They were well-printed — too well-printed for a rival crew or a disgruntled patient —
and they said, plainly, that the clinic Baálé had built to heal the wounded regardless of what
they'd ever raised a hand against him was in truth a net: that every patient who walked through its
doors and accepted its care left owing a debt only "The Turn" itself could explain, that healing was
the bait and binding was the catch.

It was not true. It was, Baálé understood within an hour of reading the first one, worse than a
lie, because it took the one thing he'd built specifically to sit outside his own reputation and
folded it back inside the same shape as everything else people already feared about him.

Kra found him on the clinic steps that evening, three pamphlets in his hand, saying nothing until
Baálé looked up.

"They're smarter this time," Kra said. "The letter went after one man's trust in you. This goes
after everyone who was ever going to need this place and hadn't decided yet whether they were
afraid of you."

"That's exactly what it's built to do." Baálé turned a pamphlet over in his hands, looking for
anything — a print mark, a particular hand, a seam in the paper — that might tell him who'd made it,
and found nothing he hadn't already failed to find in the letter three months before. "The letter
wanted one man's fear. This wants a city's."

He did not send anyone after the pamphlets, and he did not deny them from a stage, which a smaller
fear would have reached for first. Instead he did what the clinic had never needed to do before that
week: he opened its books. Every patient's name who consented to it, every treatment given, every
ledger of who had been asked for what in return — which was nothing, and had never once been
anything — set out on tables in the clinic's own front room for three days, for anyone who wanted to
walk in and read it themselves rather than take his word for it.

A courier was caught on the second night, nailing a fourth wave of pamphlets to the clinic's own
outer wall, close enough to the door that it read less like propaganda now and more like a taunt.
Kojo held him until morning. The man knew nothing worth knowing — paid in advance by a stranger he'd
never seen twice, through a second stranger who'd found him at a dice game and never given a name,
three layers between the coin in his pocket and whoever had actually decided the clinic needed
burning down from the inside instead of the outside.

The stranger, who had read every page laid out on those tables without ever once being asked to,
was the one who found the courier's own trail had already gone cold two streets back before Kojo had
finished questioning him.

"Same hand," Baálé said, once the courier had been let go with nothing more than a warning neither
of them believed would hold. "I don't need to see the ink to know it. It's patient. It's willing to
wait months between moves. And it's decided, this time, that the thing worth attacking isn't me at
all — it's the one place I built that was never supposed to be about me in the first place."

"The books worked, though," the stranger said. "People are still coming."

"The books worked because the truth was already there to be shown, and because enough people
wanted to believe it more than they wanted to believe a piece of paper nailed to a wall." Baálé
looked out at the clinic, lamplight still burning in its front room past the hour it should have
closed. "That won't work every time. Whoever this is already knows that too. They're not finished.
They were never trying to win tonight — they're teaching themselves what doesn't work, the same
patient way I once had to learn what does."

He gathered the pamphlets from the table before the last patients left, folding them the way he'd
folded the letter months before, careful not to lose whatever they might someday prove, once he had
a hand to finally hold them against.
