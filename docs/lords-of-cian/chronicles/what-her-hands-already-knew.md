# What Her Hands Already Knew

*Locked canon, Batch 244, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1069`). Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LXII, wave 21. A cross-class, cross-barrier mentorship entry -- Kanja sponsoring another tradesperson's guild recognition rather than being tested himself. Not a territory Chronicle.*

---

She'd been reading load-bearing stone for six years under her late father's license, and the stonemasons' guild — a separate charter from the smiths' guild Kanja had already earned standing with — had never once questioned the work, only ever the name on the contract, which was still his, because the charter didn't recognize a woman as a principal mason regardless of whose hands had actually done the reading.

Kanja found this out the way he found most things in Killane: by asking a question about a support wall and getting an answer sharper and more precise than the guild-certified assessment stapled beside it. The certified version had missed a hairline stress fracture running three courses down from where anyone had thought to look. Her version hadn't.

"You wrote this?" he asked, meaning the uncredited notation in the margin, not the name on the header.

"I diagnosed it. He signed it. That's how it's worked since I was fourteen." She said it without heat, the flat delivery of something argued out of her a long time ago. "Guild won't license a woman as principal. Doesn't matter how many walls I've read right that their own men read wrong."

He'd earned his own guild standing the hard way, tested by a guild master who trusted nothing he hadn't proven with his own hands — the memory of it was recent enough that the unfairness of hers landed with real weight rather than abstract sympathy. But he didn't offer to vouch for her the easy way, a word from a man the guild already respected smoothing over a judgment they hadn't actually made. That would have solved the paperwork and insulted the work.

Instead he asked the guild elders to test her exactly the way they'd tested him — put her alone in front of a structure with a real, undisclosed flaw, no second name attached to soften a wrong answer, and let the stone itself argue her case. They chose a supply depot with a subsidence problem three journeymen had already misread. She found the actual fault line in under an hour, a lateral shift eight feet from where every prior assessment had concentrated, and explained the physics of why it had fooled three trained readers before her — the visible crack was a symptom two courses removed from the cause.

The stonemasons' elder running the test read her report twice before he spoke.

"Your father teach you to read like that?"

"My father taught me to read. I taught myself to read like that." No performance in it, just the same flat honesty she'd used on Kanja. "He never got tested for a license either. Just inherited his father's name and never had to."

They licensed her within the week — not on Kanja's word, which he'd deliberately never given, but on a structural diagnosis three of their own certified men couldn't match. He was there when the guild seal went on her own name for the first time, and he told her plainly it was overdue by at least six years and every wall she'd already read correctly under someone else's signature.

"I know," she said. "I didn't need you to fix it. I needed them to finally look."

"That's the only part I actually did," he said. "Made them look."
