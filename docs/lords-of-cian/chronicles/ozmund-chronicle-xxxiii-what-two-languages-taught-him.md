# What Two Languages Taught Him

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1762`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XXXIII — third entry in the Val Mirel strand's second wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not
present in this entry. Ozmund is roughly twenty-three, between Chronicles XV and XVI. Dramatizes the
first time Ozmund consciously fuses his two inheritances — Val Mirel's Kareth War-Order patience
(`MCD-101`/`CC-004`) and House Verehimu's political register — into a single unified method rather than
holding them as separate tools, extending Chronicle XIII's "two currencies against the same debt"
observation into an actual synthesis. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years later. No new named characters.*

---

The General told me this one with a kind of quiet satisfaction I didn't hear from him often, the
satisfaction of a man describing not something that had been done to him or given to him, but something
he had, for the first time in his own accounting, built entirely himself. I've come to think it was one
of the few stories about his youth he was genuinely proud of rather than merely grateful for, and the
difference mattered enough to him that he made a point of it, telling it to me.

Two of his father's stewards had come to a disagreement that had gone on long enough to start costing the
House more than either man's stubbornness was worth — an old, tangled argument over water drawn from a
shared millrace, each man convinced, and not entirely without cause, that the other had been quietly
tilting the flow in his own favor for years. Aethelgard was away, as he so often was by then, and the
matter had settled, by default rather than design, onto Ozmund's own desk. He was twenty-three. It was
not the first dispute the House had trusted him to settle alone, but it was, he told me, the first one
where he caught himself, partway through it, doing something he had never consciously chosen to do
before.

His first instinct — the House Verehimu instinct, the one his father's whole world had trained into
him since before he could properly walk — was to gather both stewards, hear each man's case in the
other's presence, and find the words that let both of them walk away with something to protect. It was,
he told me, the correct instinct as far as it went. It had worked for his father a hundred times over. But
he told me something in him balked at using it immediately, the way his mother had once balked at
answering a seneschal's plan before she'd earned the right to disagree with it, and he found himself,
almost without deciding to, doing what she would have done instead: he said nothing to either man yet.
He went to the millrace itself.

He spent four days there. Not openly — he told me he made a point of not being seen doing it, walking
the banks at odd hours, marking what he found the way he'd once watched his mother mark a ridge above a
grain road: which gate opened first each morning, which man's tenants drew earliest and heaviest, which
stretch of the race ran lower than either steward's own account of it explained. By the fourth day he
understood the truth of it plainly enough — neither man had been stealing from the other at all. The
race itself had silted low at a bend neither steward had ever thought to walk that far to check, starving
both their fields equally, and each man, watching his own yield fall, had simply assumed the fault must
be a rival rather than the water itself.

Here, he told me, was the part he hadn't planned and couldn't fully explain even years later, describing
it to me. He didn't present the finding the way his mother would have — flatly, as an observed fact,
letting the truth do its own convincing. He didn't present it the way his father would have either — as
a negotiated settlement, each man given something to save face with. He found himself doing both at
once, in the same breath, without any sense of switching between them: he called the two stewards
together and let the silt itself, dredged and carried into the hall in a covered basin, make the
argument no words of his needed to — the patient, watched, undeniable fact his mother had trained him to
find and trust before anything else — and then, only once the fact had done its own work and neither man
had anywhere left to stand on his old grievance, he gave them the thing his father's world had always
given people in that position: a way to be wrong together rather than each man wrong alone, a joint
petition to the House for the dredging costs that let both of them walk out of that hall having lost
nothing but a mistaken suspicion neither would ever have to admit to holding first.

He told me it wasn't until the two stewards had gone, and he was alone again, that he understood what
he'd actually just done. He had not used his mother's patience to prepare the ground for his father's
persuasion, the way he'd caught himself doing once or twice before without quite noticing it. He had not
alternated between them. He had reached for both at the exact same instant, in the exact same motion,
the way a man reaches for a tool without first deciding which hand to use, and it had not once occurred
to him, doing it, that they were two different things at all.

"I understood, standing in that empty hall," he told me, the words close enough to his own that I've
never let myself round them off, "that I had stopped being my mother's son in one room and my father's
son in another. I had simply become a man who spoke both, in the same sentence, without translating
between them anymore."

I never once, in all my years beside him, saw him go back to using them separately.
