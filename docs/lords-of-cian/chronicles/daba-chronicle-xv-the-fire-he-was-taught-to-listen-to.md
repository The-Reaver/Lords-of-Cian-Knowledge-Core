The forge Daba found for him was not much of a forge -- a disused smith's shed at the edge of the
Corrow cut, abandoned when the Trust concession decided it was cheaper to ship finished tool-heads in
than to maintain a local smith, its bellows patched twice over and its chimney drawing badly on a wet
wind. It was the best Daba could risk without drawing attention onto a cell that survived precisely
because nothing about it drew attention, and the boy had looked at it for a long moment when Daba
first showed him, and said only, "It'll do," in a voice that told Daba he meant it.

"Before you touch the fire," the boy said, on the first real morning, "you have to stop thinking about
what you want the metal to become. That's the mistake in every blade that failed on you. Somebody
decided what shape they wanted before they ever listened to what the steel could actually hold."

Daba had expected, going in, to find the lesson difficult the way any unfamiliar craft is difficult --
a matter of hands not yet knowing a task, corrected with repetition. He had not expected to find it
difficult the way the boy's own tactical instinct was difficult to watch: as evidence of something
Daba could imitate but never quite own the way its source owned it.

"Put your hand here," the boy said, indicating a spot on the anvil face a finger's width from where
Daba would have placed his own hand by guesswork, "and don't move it while I work. You're not
learning to hit the metal yet. You're learning to feel what a good strike does to it, so you know what
a bad one feels like before you're the one making it."

Daba did as he was told, which was not, he reflected, a posture he assumed often or comfortably. He
had spent twenty years being the one who decided where hands went and what they struck. He found, to
his own mild surprise, that he did not resent the reversal here. The boy did not teach the way Daba
taught -- did not build a plan and drill it until it became reflex. He taught the way he fought,
by attention: watch this, listen to this, notice what changes when the color shifts here rather than
there, feel how the ring of the hammer against the stock tells you something the color alone won't.

"You're not just hammering it into shape," the boy said, working a length of scavenged stock that
would become, by the end of the week, a blade meaningfully better than anything the cell currently
carried. "You're finding out what shape it was already trying to be, and helping it get there before
it has to fight you for it."

Daba, listening, understood the sentence before the boy finished it, because it was nearly the same
sentence the boy had used, weeks before, at the lip of a dry wash, describing what he did to a runner's
stride and a chasing crowd. Weight goes where it wants to go. I just help it get there early. It was
not, Daba realized, two separate gifts wearing two separate names. It was one instinct, turned twice
-- once toward breaking things cleanly, once toward making them.

He asked questions the boy had not expected, and answered plainly when he found he could, which Daba
noted and filed alongside everything else he was learning about him. Why does the fold matter more
than the heat. What does the ring tell you that the color doesn't. How do you know when it's ready to
quench rather than merely hot enough. He did not ask because a student is supposed to ask questions.
He asked because he intended to be good at this the same way he intended to be good at everything he
had ever let himself commit to, and because he understood, with a clarity the boy seemed to find
almost unremarkable in him, exactly what a blade that didn't fail at the seam would mean for people
whose lives already ran on margins this thin.

"You're not like the others I've tried to teach this to," the boy said, near the end of that first
week, turning the finished blade in the shed's poor light, checking the fold along its spine with the
same attention he'd given the failed blade the night this whole trade began.

"What others?"

"Nobody real. I mean I've imagined teaching it, before. In my head. Nobody ever asked the right
questions in my head either." He handed the blade to Daba, hilt first. "You listen like you already
know this is going to matter to somebody who isn't in this room yet."

Daba turned the blade over once, testing its balance, and did not answer right away, because the boy
was not wrong, and because Daba had learned, across twenty years of building something meant to
outlast him, that the debts worth carrying were always the ones that mattered to people not yet in
the room.

"It will," Daba said finally. "That's the whole of why I'm here."
