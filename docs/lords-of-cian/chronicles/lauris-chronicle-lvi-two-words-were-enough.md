# Lauris Chronicle LVI: Two Words Were Enough

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1673`). Fifty-sixth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, Strand W (Witness / present-day, quiet register). A stakes-free entry pairing
Lauris with Callum Breck, whose voice was rebuilt gradually through purely functional speech after
the Black Trench (`CC-119`) -- consistent with that established economy of words rather than
restaging his silence arc itself. No new named characters. Written under Abad's blanket
authorization: "lock it, continue uninterrupted, test and push to main."*

---

*Archive fragment, present-day: "Callum Breck and I spent an afternoon mending a net and said,
between the two of us, perhaps forty words. I did not need more than that to know exactly how the
afternoon had gone. I am told most people find this uncomfortable. I found it restful."*

I have written elsewhere, in accounts closer to the war than this one, of the four months Callum
Breck spent unable to speak to anyone but his infant daughter after Nev Torr died under his watch at
the Black Trench, and of the twenty-six words that, years later, guided the fleet's escape through
Ghost Harbor's reef gap. I do not intend to retell that story here. I mention it only because it is
the reason I found the afternoon I am about to describe worth setting down at all: a man who once
went four months in silence, and who rebuilt his own voice one purely functional sentence at a
time, does not spend words carelessly even now that speaking again costs him nothing. Every sentence
he offers still means, roughly, exactly what it says and nothing more.

He was mending a net on the dock when Lauris found him — one of the older, patched kind, more repair
than original weave by this point, the sort of task a man takes on less because the net is worth
saving and more because his hands need something to do while his mind is elsewhere. She sat beside
him without asking whether the company was wanted, which I have come to understand is, between the
two of them, its own established form of asking.

"Net's beyond saving," she said, after a while, examining a section that had clearly failed and been
reknotted so many times the knots had begun to outnumber the original mesh.

"I know."

"You're mending it anyway."

"I know that too."

She picked up the second shuttle without further comment and began working the opposite end toward
his, and for the better part of two hours neither of them said anything further that was not
strictly about the net itself — a snag pointed out, a knot corrected, once a request, wordless, for
the second spool passed hand to hand. I was present for perhaps half of it, and what struck me,
watching, was how little either of them seemed to require of the silence. Lauris has never, in my
experience, needed a conversation filled to feel at ease in someone's company. Callum Breck, for
reasons I understand rather better after writing his own story, has learned the same lesson from a
harder direction. Between the two of them, the quiet was not an absence of anything. It was simply
the shape their company took.

"Sera came by the workshop last week," he said eventually, apropos of nothing in the net itself.
"Asked after you."

"Did she."

"Wanted to know if it's true you carried a burst water main on your own back rather than wait for
the engineers."

"It is true."

He considered this with the particular stillness I have come to recognize in him — not hesitation,
exactly, but the deliberate weighing of a man who still, all these years on, treats every sentence
as worth the cost of choosing carefully. "She thinks that's the finest thing she's heard all year."

"Tell her I thought so too."

He nodded, and that, as far as either of them seemed to require, settled the matter entirely. They
returned to the net. I did not hear either of them speak again for the better part of twenty
minutes, and I want to record plainly that nothing about that stretch of silence read, from where I
sat, as anything less than entirely comfortable — no strain in it, no waiting for the other to fill
it, simply two people working with their hands beside someone whose company they had no need to
justify with conversation.

When the net was finally, imperfectly mended — more patch than weave by the time they finished, as
he had said from the start — Lauris held it up, turned it once against the light to check the
tension across its length, and set it down with the same satisfied economy I have watched her bring
to considerably larger repairs.

"It'll hold," she said.

"I know," Callum said, and that, between the two of them, was the whole of the goodbye. I have
written a great deal, across this series, about what Lauris carries so that other people do not have
to. I set this account down instead for the plainer, quieter fact it demonstrates: that some
afternoons, in this crew's company, she is not carrying anything at all. She is simply mending a net
beside a man who does not need her to say more than she means, and finding, in that, exactly the
kind of rest the rest of her life so rarely affords her.
