# Daba Chronicle XIX: The Water He Wanted to Move Himself

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1589`). Entry 19 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes genuine
two-way exchange within Kanja's mentorship under Daba (`MCD-1568`): Kanja pushes to move beyond reading
existing terrain toward deliberately manufacturing it, the specific proposal Daba initially resists and
then narrows into an acceptable form -- the direct conceptual seed of Kanja's own later Dredge-Line
Ambush (`MCD-231`), which floods rather than merely reads terrain. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles
in batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

---

It began, as most of Kanja's disagreements began, with a question asked in a tone that did not sound
like a question. "Why do we only ever use the ground we find? Why not the ground we make?"

They were sitting at the edge of the camp's cook fire, three weeks past the ford, and Daba had learned
to recognize the particular restlessness that meant the boy had been turning an idea over for days
before finally letting it out. He set down the strap he was mending.

"Say what you mean."

"The staked ford. It worked because the water already narrowed the crossing to where you wanted it.
But what if the water isn't already there? What if the ground you need doesn't exist yet, and you build
it -- dig a channel, break a levee, flood a road that was dry the day before. You wouldn't need to find
the right terrain. You'd make whatever terrain the fight required."

Daba felt the old, familiar unease that came whenever a student's reach exceeded his caution, and he
did not hide it from his face. "You're describing something a hundred people would see built. A dug
channel doesn't dig itself quietly. A broken levee announces itself to everyone downstream of it the
moment the water starts moving somewhere it never moved before."

"Not if you do it small. Not if you do it once, in one place, and you're already gone before anyone
connects the flood to a cause."

"And if you're wrong about how it moves? Water doesn't negotiate with your intentions once it's
loose. You could drown people you never meant to touch, on ground you don't control the way you think
you do." Daba shook his head. "Everything I've built, every doctrine 1804 runs on, exists to keep us
small enough that nothing we do is worth anyone's attention. You're proposing something with a scale to
it. Something visible. Anything large enough to be seen is large enough to be burned -- that's not a
saying I made up for effect. I watched it be true."

He expected the boy to argue the point directly, the way he usually did, pressing until either the
argument gave or Daba's patience did. Instead Kanja was quiet for a long moment, and when he spoke
again his voice had lost some of its certainty, replaced with something closer to genuine working-out.

"Then don't scale it to the war. Scale it to the crossing." He picked up a stick and drew a rough line
in the dirt between them -- a channel, a road crossing it. "Not a levee that drowns a province. A cut
narrow enough that one man could dig it in a night, timed to a single column on a single road, gone
before the water's even finished rising. The same ford, in effect. You're just not waiting for the
ground to happen to be right. You're making it right, once, for the one crossing that matters, and then
you're gone and there's nothing left standing for anyone to trace back to you."

Daba looked at the crude drawing for a long moment. The unease had not left him entirely -- he did not
think it ever fully would, not about a tool with water's own indifference to intention -- but the shape
of what Kanja was describing had narrowed considerably from where it started, and narrowed in exactly
the direction 1804's own doctrine demanded. Small. Timed. Deniable. Gone before it could be traced.

"That," he said slowly, "is not the same idea you opened with."

"It's the same idea. I just let you tell me what was wrong with it first."

Daba almost smiled at that, and did not quite let himself. It was, he thought, the clearest evidence
yet that the mentorship had stopped running in one direction -- that the boy was no longer simply
receiving doctrine and testing it against imagined fights, but building on it, pressing it past its own
edges and then, when the edge pushed back, refining rather than abandoning what he'd proposed. Daba had
taught men before who mistook correction for defeat. This one treated it as raw material.

"Small," Daba said again, mostly to himself, turning the drawing over in his mind the way he turned
over any tool before deciding whether 1804 could afford to carry it. "One crossing. One column. Gone
before the water settles." He looked up. "If you ever build that for real, you make certain it drowns a
problem and not a village. The ground doesn't care which one it gets."

"I know," Kanja said, and something in how simply he said it told Daba the boy had already been turning
over exactly that risk on his own, unprompted, before he had ever brought the idea to the fire at all.
