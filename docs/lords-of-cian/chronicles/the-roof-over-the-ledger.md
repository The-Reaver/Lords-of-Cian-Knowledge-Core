# The Roof Over the Ledger

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1163`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXXIX, wave 27. The method's first tenant-rights case -- rent-gouging in company housing, a domain distinct from every prior wage or contract dispute.*

---

No wages were in dispute at all when the crew arrived, which was itself unusual enough that Ezio double-checked the summons before they set up the tent. The workers at the ironworks were paid correctly, on time, at a fair posted rate — the complaint was entirely about what happened to that pay the moment it left their hands and crossed back to the same employer through a different door: company housing, mandatory for anyone working the furnace floor, priced at a rent that climbed every few months without explanation, deducted straight from wages before the worker ever saw the difference.

"We've never worked a housing case," Ezio admitted, laying out blank pages for a kind of ledger he'd never built before. "I don't know yet what the honest number even looks like here."

He built it the way he built everything else — patiently, from the ground up. He priced comparable non-company housing in the nearest free district, adjusted for genuine differences in quality and proximity that the company housing did offer, and cross-referenced the rent increases against the company's own maintenance and construction costs to see whether the climbing price reflected anything real. It didn't. The housing hadn't been improved in six years. The rent had risen four times in that span, each increase timed, Ezio noticed once he laid the dates against the wage-rate calendar, to land within a month of any raise the furnace floor received — absorbing every gain in pay before it could ever accumulate into anything a worker could save or spend elsewhere.

"They're not stealing wages," Ezio said, once the pattern was fully mapped. "They're renting the wages back."

The finding Kanja and Ezio produced treated it exactly that plainly: a mandatory-housing rent structure engineered to track and consume wage increases rather than reflect any real cost of the housing itself, a form of theft dressed as ordinary landlording because the employer and the landlord happened to be the same man with the same incentive either way. The remedy fixed the rent at a rate tied honestly to comparable free-district housing, indexed independently of the ironworks' own wage schedule going forward, and returned the difference on the four engineered increases as back-owed wages in every sense that mattered, even though not a single hour of labor had been underpaid.

The owner argued, weakly, that housing pricing wasn't wage theft and fell outside anything the method had ever claimed authority over. Kanja's answer was simple enough that Ezio wrote it into the finding itself, nearly verbatim: a wage a man never gets to keep isn't meaningfully different from a wage he was never paid, whichever door the company takes it back through.
