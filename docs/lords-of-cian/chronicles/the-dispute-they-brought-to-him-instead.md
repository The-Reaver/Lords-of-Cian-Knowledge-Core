# The Dispute They Brought to Him Instead

*Locked canon, Batch 196, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-704`). Bane Alias Chronicle XXXIX, wave 13 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The freed captives at Dessin Hold had no fight left to give anyone by the time the liberation was
three weeks old — the garrison broken, the work of rescue finished — and what remained instead was
the smaller, harder problem of sixty people who had survived captivity together and now disagreed,
bitterly, about who among them had informed on whom during the years it took the rebellion to reach
them.

Two names circulated as the likely informant, both denied, both plausible, no evidence solid enough
to settle it, and the dispute had begun to split the freed community into factions that trusted each
other less than they'd trusted anyone under the garrison's watch. They brought it to Bane not because
he'd asked to judge it, but because no one among them could be trusted to judge it without being
accused of favoring one side.

"I don't think this is mine to decide," he told them plainly, the first time the two factions
gathered expecting him to simply name a verdict. "I wasn't here. I didn't live what you lived, and I
don't know either of these two people the way you all do, for better or worse. Whatever I said would
just be a guess dressed up as an answer because I'm the one people are afraid to argue with."

"Then who decides?" one of them asked, frustration plain. "We can't. We've tried for three weeks."

"You can. You've just been trying to decide who's guilty instead of deciding what you actually need,
which isn't the same question." Bane didn't offer a verdict. He offered a process instead — a
gathering of everyone, informant accusations set aside entirely, focused only on what each of the
sixty had actually witnessed during the captivity, cross-checked account against account the way
Onyx's own Black Ledger might have read a battlefield, except done here by the community itself,
plainly, out loud, with no one outside it deciding for them.

It took four more days. The truth that emerged was smaller and sadder than either faction's
accusation — no deliberate informant at all, only a guard who'd overheard careless talk and reported
it without anyone meaning to betray anyone. The factions didn't fully reconcile. They stopped
accusing each other, which was most of what the community actually needed.

"You could have just told us who did it," the same freed captive said afterward, some of the
bitterness eased if not gone. "It would have been faster."

"It would have. It also would have made it my truth instead of yours, and you'd have had to decide
whether to believe me the same way you couldn't decide whether to believe each other." Bane watched
the community, imperfectly mended, begin the slower work of actually living together again. "I'd
rather it took four days and belonged to you than take an hour and belong to me."
