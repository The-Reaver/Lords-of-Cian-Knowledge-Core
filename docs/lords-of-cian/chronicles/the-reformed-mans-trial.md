# The Reformed Man's Trial

*Locked canon, Batch 258, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1209`). Sovereign Ghost of the Great Sea Alias Chronicle
LXXI, wave 24. Testing the long-term credibility of a parole granted years earlier. Not a territory
Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The name meant something to Garren Hask before it meant anything to the rest of the crew — a captain
paroled four years back after his ship was disabled raiding a Trust supply line, released on the
fleet's standard terms once it became clear his crew had been driven to raiding by an unpaid garrison
rather than genuine cruelty. Hask had logged the parole himself. Now the same name surfaced again,
this time attached to an accusation of a massacred trading post, six dead, cargo stripped, black
sails reported by two separate witnesses.

Kanja went to find him before assuming anything either way, the same standard he'd have applied to a
stranger. He found the man running a legitimate trade route out of a small port, three years married,
no black sails anywhere near his current ship, and utterly unaware anyone had accused him of anything
until Kanja said so directly.

"I haven't raided since the day you let me go," the man said, and there was no fear in it, only a
kind of tired certainty that came from having built something real out of a second chance and now
watching it threatened by a crime he hadn't committed. "Ask anyone in this port. I've had four years
to prove it or not, and I've spent every one of them proving it."

The trail, once Danne Sok and Efa Gol ran it down properly, led to an unrelated crew that had adopted
the reformed captain's old black-sail markings specifically because the name still carried weight — a
borrowed reputation used to cover an atrocity, the same trick the fleet's own legend had absorbed
before, now turned against a man trying to live it down rather than trade on it.

Kanja cleared the reformed captain publicly, in the same port, in front of the same witnesses who'd
first pointed a finger, and made a point of doing it without ceremony, the way he'd want any
accusation resolved. "The doctrine only means something," he told Garren Hask afterward, "if it holds
for the man four years out as well as it did the day we let him go."

Hask logged the parole's fourth year the same way he'd logged its first — clean, unbroken, the reform
genuinely real.
