The bosun came aft in the grey before sunup with the Captain's words in his mouth and nothing in his hands.

"Mole's yours."

She was walking the rail. Three strides to the taffrail, three back. The sea had told her an hour ago, and she had been waiting for someone to say it aloud.

Windbreak sat under its hill with its roofs hunched to the wind. Grey slate. Thorn trees combed flat. A stone mole ran out from the town and bent at the elbow, an arm held up against weather. The harbor-watch had lit the fire on the elbow early. Smoke lay along the water.

Past the point, keels. Many. Laden low. Oars dipping in time, a beat no fisherman kept.

Half the fleet was away at Ashcoral. The rest lay at anchor in the roads, and the Captain had said mole.

"All of it?"

"All of it." The bosun scratched his jaw. "And Ren. If Ren's willing."

A skiff from the point had come alongside as well, with the harbor-watch's count of it: seven boats, two barges, a cart on the second barge with a log slung inside.

Her three were already at the head of the ladder. Edda with the old scar from Tideglass showing white through her hair. Hamund with a coil of line on his shoulder. Odile with her back to the mast. Nobody had called them.

She went forward.

The faces on the deck turned as she passed and stayed turned. She went through them to the bow.

Ren sat on the anchor housing with his bowl on his knee. The ring lay around him as it had for three years, but the ring had drawn in. A man could stand at the edge of it now without his knees bending. He wore the boots the Captain had built him, and the planks under the housing groaned low.

She stepped inside. The weight came down on her shoulders, less than it had been. The chains hung heavy at her knees.

"Boats," she said. "Seven, and two barges. A cart with a ram in it."

He set the bowl down.

"There is a mole. Will you stand on it?"

"To do what?"

"Stand."

"That is all?"

"And when I say, the other thing."

He looked at his hands. He had worked the field up and down on a bare shelf of rock for three years, and never once with a line of men on the other side of it.

"Where?"

• • •

The mole ran a hundred and thirty paces from the town to its head, ten across, cap-stones laid in courses. At the elbow the stone widened into a pad, twenty paces square, where a crane had stood in some earlier generation. Its footing remained, a square of cut blocks sunk in the paving. Behind the pad the mole drew in to a neck six paces wide, and at the root of the neck stood the harbor gate, iron-banded oak, shut.

She walked it twice at a stride. Round the pad. Down the neck. Up again.

Windbreak's harbor-watch had rolled forty water-filled casks out of the net-lofts and stacked them across the neck, two courses high. A lane the width of one body ran through the middle where a cask had been left out. Twenty-five paces from the pad's nearest edge.

She scuffed a line across the cap-stones with her boot-heel.

"The casks. Edda, left shoulder."

Edda went.

"Hamund. Right."

Hamund went.

"Odile. The lane."

Odile set her back to the first cask beside it and said nothing.

Ren stood at the pad's middle, where she had put him. The stone under the boots the Captain had built him lay quiet.

"They will come at me," he said.

"At the pad."

"That is the same."

"Yes."

"And they will stop."

"Yes."

He considered it. He walked out from the middle of the pad and counted strides to the first of her scuffs. He stopped, went back, and counted again.

"Fifteen. A little more." He pointed past the line. "There. I will feel the edge."

She scuffed it farther out with her heel.

"I'll be past that."

"You will be past the casks."

"Past the casks."

"I have not done it with anyone on the other side."

"I know."

She went out beyond the pad, along the straight run toward the head, and stood at a line of her own, ten paces beyond the pad's seaward edge. The smoke had thinned. The keels were past the point.

• • •

They came on the flood in the first light. Seven long boats with oars, two barges behind them under sweeps, low and broad. They grounded on the head's stair and the barges nosed in alongside, and the soldiers came up the stair in iron caps with shields on their arms and pikes in their fists. A hundred and sixty. More behind, rowing the barges clear.

The cart came off the second barge on planks. A timber frame on four wheels, a hide stretched over its face, a squared oak log slung inside on chains and banded with iron at the head. Forty men took the push-poles at its back.

Two paces behind the front rank, a man in a better cap walked the line with his arm out, saying one thing over and over, low and level.

"Eyes down. Her boots. Watch her boots."

She had not heard that before.

She stood where she was and let them form. Pikes level. Shields up. Ten abreast across the head, closing to the width of the mole. The column moved up the stone together, a long dark animal with a hide on its face, and every foot of it set down on the same beat.

She read them as they came.

The captain: careful. He would stay behind and send the rest.

The pikes: drilled. They would hold as long as the beat held.

The cart: the hard part. If it reached the casks, the casks would go and the gate after them.

Break the beat.

The Fangs came down off her forearms and swung at her knees.

The front rank came within a long pike of her, and every eye in it was on her boots.

She went in under the first points.

A Fang left her hand and hooked the haft below the lead pikehead. She pulled. The man came with his own pike, dragged across his own front, and into the Star as it came off her back with its talons open. The Star took the haft out of his hands and threw it behind her. The jar of it came up her arm heavier than she had sent it. She put it into the next throw.

The links began to warm.

She ran the Fangs in a figure-eight. Left, right, crossing before her. The chains keened as they crossed. A cap spun away. Pikes left hands. A shield-rim opened a forearm to the bone, and the man stood holding the arm and staring at it. Each blow came back up her shoulders heavier than she had sent it, and the next went out faster.

The soldiers held. A hundred and sixty boots set down together.

She opened her throat.

Under hearing. The gorget's crystal took the hum and threw it ahead of her in a wedge.

The front rank felt it in their chests. Men who had kept their eyes on her boots found the boots swimming. Pikes swayed. A man stepped back to where he judged himself clear, one pace out of the Fangs' reach, and had judged wrong. The next pass caught him square. His shield went spinning across the stone.

The beat broke.

It broke in the middle first, where the voice lay thickest. Then at the shoulders. A third rank man lifted his eyes from the boots to her face, and his face held. His pike went slack. The captain's hand came down on his collar from behind and spun him round and sent him to the rear.

Too late for the rest. Eyes were coming up all along the front. More of them stayed up every breath.

A big sergeant braced at the center, legs apart, shield locked. A Fang went out and wrapped the shield's rim, and she hauled it taut and held it, four paces of chain bowed between them. Her other hand worked three pikes out of three grips. The sergeant leaned on his shield against the chain. The chain held him where he stood.

Behind him the cart's pushers shoved.

• • •

It came through the opening the front rank had left.

The hide face swung ahead of the poles, the ram log swaying on its chains behind it. Forty men leaning. It crossed the first line she had scuffed and the stone rang under its wheels.

Past her. Nobody could stop it from the front. If it reached the casks, the casks would go.

The ring showed itself before she had done anything about it. The soldiers behind the cart bowed away from the pad as they came, men at the edges shouldering inward, pikes slanting off to either side. Nobody wanted to be the nearest to the man at the center. The pad lay open in front of them, twenty paces of bare stone and one figure standing in it, quiet, with his hands at his sides.

The cart did not bow. It had too much weight behind it. It rolled straight at him.

She dropped the sergeant's shield. He stumbled and went to one knee. Both Fangs went out together.

One hooked the cart's top rail. The other took the near wheel-pin. She set her feet and pulled, and the pull came back through her heavier than she had sent it, because forty men were pushing on the other end. She fed that into the next throw. The links ran warm across her fingers and the chain sang. She whipped a wrap round the axle, hauled, and the whole frame yawed and leaned. The lashings on the hide parted one after another with sounds like snapped bowstrings. The wheel-pin sheared. The cart lurched off its line and rolled slantwise onto the pad's seaward half, twelve paces from Ren.

Ten.

She cut the last lashing and let the Fang go.

Ren had not moved. He was looking at her.

"Clear?"

She ran.

Back the way she had come, across the pad, past him, down the neck at a dead run with the chains swinging. Nobody followed. Nobody wanted the middle of the pad.

The lane. Odile's arm lay across the gap at waist height. It lifted. Anirak went through. The arm came down behind her.

Past the casks. Past the scuff marks. She turned.

"Now."

• • •

Ren set his feet and breathed out.

A hull groans like that under a heavy sea. This was the same sound, slowed and laid across everything in sight, a long low note that came up through the stone and the air together. The smoke from the harbor-fire flattened against the water. The hide on the cart's face bellied inward like a sail in a head wind.

The ram log swung once on its chains and stopped.

The frame bowed, slowly, the way a ship's back bows when she hangs between two swells. The top beams split along the grain with a sound like cloth tearing. The iron bands screamed on the oak. Then the axles went, both in a breath, and the cart came down on the log and the log came down on the stone.

The paving heaved. A crack ran out from the log's point in a star, ten paces long, twelve, the cap-stones riding up on their edges like broken ice. The old crane footing split in half.

The forty pushers went to their knees.

All forty, at once, with the poles still in their hands, as though something had set a flat palm on the back of every neck. Shields rang on the stone. A cap rolled across the pad and rolled and fetched up against Ren's boot. The soldiers who had crowded onto the pad behind the cart were down on hands and knees with their pikes under them, and the pike-men still on their feet beyond the reach of it stood and stared, their faces gone grey.

Ren held it. She counted. Eleven breaths. He let go on the twelfth.

The sound lifted off the stone like a lid.

• • •

She went back out through the lane.

The captain had not gone down. He stood beyond the pad's seaward rim, outside the fifteen strides, with a dozen of the rear rank around him, pikes up. His better cap sat straight on his head.

"Once," she said. "Sit, or go to the boats."

The rear rank had watched the pad go. Their pikes shook. A few came down. Four men sat where they stood, with their weapons across their knees. Six turned and ran for the stair, and nobody struck them.

The captain came on.

The Fang went out and wrapped his shield's rim. She held it. The other chain took his sword arm at the wrist, and the Star came off her back in her free hand and closed its talons on the blade and wrenched. The sword left him. He stood on the pad's seaward edge, clear of Ren's ring, holding a shield he could not lift, with a hand he could not close.

She walked the line he was standing on. Round him. Round again.

"Sit."

He looked at her. His face turned to her and held, and she let him look. The chains cooled along their links.

He sat down on the broken cart's axle-beam.

The barges were already backing off the stair with the six who had run and the ones who could walk. The harbor-watch of Windbreak stood on the neck behind the casks with boat-hooks and gaff-poles, and nobody had needed them.

Hamund came up the lane wiping his hands on his shirt. Odile came behind him.

"Two came round the harbor side," Hamund said. "On the wall-top."

"And?"

"Odile."

Odile shrugged. "They're in a net-loft. Tied."

• • •

The harbor-master of Windbreak was a woman in oilskins with a boat-hook for a staff. She walked the length of the main crack with the hook dragging behind her, and stopped at the ram log lying in the broken frame, and put her thumb in a seam of the paving and looked at how far it went in.

"Whose word was it?"

"Mine." Anirak stood at the star's edge, shifting from foot to foot, the chains swaying at her knees.

"A year of stone, that."

"The pad is mine to mend."

The harbor-master looked at her face. Her eyes held, as all of them did. She looked down again at the stone.

"Cutters. Masons. By autumn."

"By autumn."

She went off along the mole with the hook scraping.

Ren stood on the split paving, in the middle of what he had done. The stone beneath him did not groan. It lay broken and still, the worst of it finished.

"I did that," he said.

"I said now."

"I waited."

"Yes."

"You came out past the casks. I felt you cross the edge. You were on the far side of it before I let go."

She had no answer. She walked the rim of the crack.

Edda came as far as the crack's far rim, outside Ren's ring, and held out a heel of bread. She went to Edda for it and ate it on her feet.

"Tomorrow," Ren said. "If they send another."

"There."

He looked at the broken stone he was standing on.

"The pad is already broken."

"Cheaper."

He looked at the star of cracks, and at the cart in pieces. His mouth moved.

"Cheaper," he said.
