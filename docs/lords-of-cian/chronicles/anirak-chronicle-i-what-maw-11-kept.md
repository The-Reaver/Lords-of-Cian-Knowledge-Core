The table in the Captain's cabin was too small for the map. She drew it anyway.

Charcoal on the planks. A ring for the Slab. A square for the yard. A long bar for the pen block,
four tiers deep, under the hill. One hard stroke for the mast that stood over the pens.

She did not sit. She walked the table's edge while she drew, round and round, the charcoal never
lifting long.

Ghostwind leaned in the doorway. Sylas, twenty-nine, the wind-harness slack on his shoulders and the
disc on his breastbone ticking now and then as the weather shifted outside. He watched the lines go
down. The Captain stood at the stern window with his back to them both.

"Front gate." Her finger on it. "Yard. Slab. Three doors off the yard. Two go to the armories. One
goes down."

"Down where?"

"The tunnels. Staging, then the pens." She drew the tunnels. The old ways under the main run. The
drains under the staging floor. The old feed chute, bricked at the top and open at the bottom. The
gap behind the triage station where the wall had settled and nobody had cared. "Ninety-one steps from
the chute to the first grate. Dark the whole way."

"You walked it."

"I counted it."

He looked at the drawing a long while. Then at her.

"Every eye at Maw-11 knows your face."

"Every eye anywhere."

That was true. She had seen it in every harbor since Chain Harbor. Faces turned when she passed.
Stayed turned. She never knew which of them had chosen to.

The Captain spoke without turning from the window. The collar was off, so the voice came out plain,
low and rough.

"So you go in the front."

"Yes."

"And they all look at you."

"Yes."

"And nobody looks at him."

Ghostwind smiled at that, a little. He had a roll of oilcloth under his arm. He set it on the table
and opened it. Bronze reeds, thirty of them, each the length of a hand. A pen-worker could hide one
in a fist.

"Tuned to their mast," the Captain said. "Off the Trust's own books."

She picked one up. Blew across it once. No sound she could hear. The table hummed under her palms.
She knew that hum. It was the low one the crew sang at the oars. It was close to her own.

She set it down.

"They sing it in the pens," she said.

"They sing it. The weight comes off them. They walk."

She knew the weight. Every Branded under that mast knew it. Heavy in the limbs. Slow in the blood. Arms
too heavy to lift against a grate.

"How long."

"Ghostwind needs an hour below. They need a quarter of one to learn the note." He turned at last.
"You hold the yard that long."

"Cost?"

"Don't stop moving."

• • •

Maw-11 sat in a fold of the southern coast hills. Grey stone. Low
walls. A regional house, small beside the great Maws of the capital. Three thousand eight hundred
Branded in its pens. A handful of bouts a month. A steady trade.

She came up the road at dusk with her three behind her. Edda. Hamund. Odile. No hoods. No stealth.

The smell reached her first. Before the walls. Before the gate. Lamp oil and old sweat. Wet straw
gone sour. Iron, and the copper underneath iron that is blood dried a long time. The smell had not
changed. It went into her nose and down into her chest and lay there.

The mast stood over the hill behind the walls, black against the last light. Taller than she
remembered. A thin pole with a box at the top. Nothing came off it that a person could see or hear.
She felt it all the same. A pressure behind the eyes. A drag in the shoulders. Faint, out here.
Stronger every step.

Her hands wanted to slow. She did not let them.

Two gate guards. Spears grounded. Lanterns on hooks.

The first one looked up. His face turned to her and stayed turned. His mouth opened. The spear-butt
slid on the stone and he caught it late.

The second one looked. Same.

She walked between them. Neither moved until she was past.

Behind her, Edda and Hamund took the gate. Odile put her back to the hinge post. The gate would not
close tonight.

• • •

The yard. Packed sand over stone. Raked smooth in rows. Lamps on poles at the corners. The Slab in
the middle, a raised floor of fitted blocks, stained dark in the seams. The smell of it stronger here.
Old blood in the sand. New blood on the Slab's edge from the afternoon's work.

Forty men in the yard. Slab crews at the reset carts, sweeping. Guards at the armory doors. A knot of
handlers at the water trough, cups in hand.

Every face found her.

Cups stopped halfway. Brooms stopped. The noise of the yard went out like a lamp in wind. They stood
with their faces on her, and the pull went out of her eyes and into them, and some of it came back.
She felt it come back. It always did. Forty men looking at her as if she were the only thing in the
world. She could not tell how much of that was hers.

Someone said it. Then several.

"Blades Fury."

She kept walking. Along the Slab's edge. Around it.

The guards at the armories came off the doors. Eight of them. Spears and hook-poles. Then the
handlers from the trough, with clubs off the rack. Then the Slab crews with their iron reset bars.
Thirty, thirty-two. The rest stayed where they were and watched.

She read them as she walked.

The guards: drilled. They spread to flank. They would come together.

The trough handlers: bold with numbers, slow with clubs. They would come behind the guards.

The crews: afraid. They would come only if the others went first.

Hold them here. All of them alive.

She let the Fangs down off her forearms. Chain ran through her fingers. The hook-swords hung at her
knees and swung there, cold.

The first guard came in fast with his spear low. She did not wait for him. She went to him.

A Fang went out on its chain and hooked his forearm behind the wrist. She pulled. The arm came across
his body, the spear with it, the point turned into the sand. Her free hand had already gone over her
shoulder. The Morning Star came off her back in its talon shape and caught the spear-shaft where the
arm had brought it. She twisted. The shaft left his hands. He went past her, empty, and sat down hard.

The hook came back to her on its chain, and its weight came back with it, and she fed the weight into
the next throw.

The chains warmed. She felt it in the links where they crossed her fingers. Dull heat. Her heart
went up a step.

Two guards at once. She ran the Fangs in a figure-eight, left and right in turn, crossing in front of
her. The air moaned through the links. One hook took a spear out of a man's grip and threw it end
over end into the dark. The other opened a forearm to the bone. He dropped his pole and held the arm.

Each strike came back up her arms heavier than she sent it. Her shoulders loosened. The next throw
went out faster than the last.

A hook-pole came at her from behind. She felt the air move. She stepped into the swing, inside the
hook, and took the shaft across her shoulder. The blow went into her and stayed in her. She turned
with it and gave it back through the Star into the man's thigh. He went down sideways.

The rest bunched. Six handlers with clubs, a guard on either end. They meant to rush her together.

She let her voice go.

It came from low in her throat, under hearing. The crystal nodes in the gorget took it up and laid
it out ahead of her like a blade of water. The handlers felt it in their chests first. Then in their
feet. The sand tilted. The lamps swung in their eyes though the lamps were still. The man in front
judged her six paces off and stepped back to be safe. She was four paces off. He stepped back into
the hooks.

She opened the Star's head wide and swept it through them. Clubs went out of hands, spinning. Men
went to their knees in the sand and stayed there with their heads down, sick, breathing hard. A
guard tried to rise. He looked at her, and his face held there. He sat back down.

No one came again.

She kept moving. Round the Slab. Round again. The men on the ground lay still. The ones still standing
at the edges stood still. Every face in the yard was turned to her.

The chains glowed now along their links. Dull amber. Her heart sat high and steady in her throat.

Warm. She knew the line. She had lived on its near side every day since Chain Harbor.

• • •

She felt the chant. It made no sound.

It came up through the stone under the sand. A hum below the floor of sound. It sat in her ribs the
way her own voice sat in other people's. Faint at first. Then more voices. Then many.

The pens were singing.

She knew what it was doing because her body told her. The drag behind her eyes was thinning. The weight
in her shoulders was going out of them. Down there, under the mast, three thousand eight hundred bodies
were lifting their arms for the first time in years. Down there, men who could break iron were
remembering it.

Ghostwind had found them. Ghostwind had given them the reeds.

She kept walking. Round the Slab.

Then a hole tore in the low note, near the first grate. Voices stopping. Then
starting. Then stopping again.

Someone was at the grate. Someone the singers were afraid of.

She looked at the third door off the yard. The door that went down. It stood open. It had been shut
when she came in.

One man had not stayed to watch her. One man had gone below.

She knew who held the keys to that door.

She broke off. Her chains came up off the sand. She went through the door at a run, and the heat in
the links did not climb any higher.

• • •

Gethin Brule ran the tunnels at Maw-11. He had run them in her years there. The staging. The armory
keys. The pens.

A free man. A careful one. He knew every Branded in his tunnels by number, and every number by its
price. Nothing left his tunnels uncounted.

He would have been below when the singing started. He would have felt it in the floor, the way she
had. He would know what it was. Every tunnel master in the south had heard of the Blackout.

The staging rack by the third door kept the hook-poles. She passed it at a run. One hook was gone.

• • •

The tunnel. Stone close on both sides. The ceiling low enough that tall men stooped. Lamps far apart,
most of them out. Water running down the walls and pooling in the worn places of the floor. The
smell here was the pens' smell. She had slept in it.

Ninety-one steps from the old chute. She did not need the chute tonight. She counted from the staging
floor. Forty more.

The singing filled the dark. Low. Uneven. Holes in it, opening and closing.

The first grate. Iron bars set floor to ceiling, crossbars at waist and head height, a lamp on a
bracket beside it.

Brule stood at the bars with his back to her.

Beyond the grate, the first pen. Faces at the bars. Thin faces. Brands on the inner left forearms of
the hands gripping iron. A bronze reed in a fist here and there. Some of them still singing. Some
backed away from the bars, mouths shut.

One woman stood at the front. Grey-haired, her arms scarred to the elbow. She held a reed to her lips
and sang into it, and she did not step back.

Brule had the hook-pole set through the bars. The hook was at her throat.

"Stop," he said to her. "Stop that, or it goes in."

She sang.

Anirak read it in a breath.

The tunnel: narrow, wet, one man in it.

The man: one pole, hooked, set at a throat, his weight already leaning into it.

He is killing her now.

She spoke from the dark, one time.

"Leave it. Walk."

He turned his head. The lamp lit her eyes, and his face stayed turned. For a heartbeat he only looked.
She saw him know her. She saw him measure the dark, the four paces, the chain, and come up short.

"Anirak," he said.

Behind the bars, faces turned to her too. The singing faltered all along the grate. The low note
thinned toward nothing.

The grey woman's voice held. She sang louder. Others found the note again around her.

Brule's eyes went back to the woman.

His shoulders set to drive the pole.

The Fang left her hand. Four paces of chain. The hook took his forearm above the wrist. She pulled.
The arm came back out of the bars across his body, the pole with it, the hook scraping the iron. The
Star was already in her other hand. Its talons closed on the shaft. She twisted. The pole went
clattering down the tunnel.

He had a knife at his belt. He drew it.

He did not turn to her. He went back to the grate, low and fast, the knife up, his arm going through
the bars for the woman's belly.

The Star swung once. Everything she had gathered in the yard, every blow taken and given, came
through her arm into the iron head. It met the side of his skull above the ear.
The bone gave like a plank under a dropped anvil. His head went into the bars. His body went down the
bars into the water on the floor.

The knife fell into the pen. The grey woman put her foot on it.

She sang through all of it.

• • •

She did not let herself stand. She walked. Back and forth in front of the grate, two steps each way,
so the weight would not drain out of her while the hum built.

The faces behind the bars stayed on her. She kept her own face turned down the tunnel, away from them.
The singing found its depth again.

It filled the tunnel. It filled the stone. She felt it in her teeth. The drag behind her eyes was
gone entirely now. The weight was gone from her shoulders.

In the pen, a man put his hands on two bars. Took breath. Pulled.

The iron groaned. It bent outward. Rust burst off it in flakes. Then the crossbar sheared at its
seat with a crack like a mast going in a gale.

Others came to the bars beside him.

Ghostwind came out of the dark at the far end of the tunnel, the harness humming faintly on his back.
He looked at the body at the grate. He looked at her.

"Pens two through four are singing," he said. "Pen four's grates are already down."

"The yard's clear."

"Then they walk."

He was gone again, fast, the air sucking after him.

• • •

They walked.

Up through the staging tunnels and out the door to the yard. Grey faces, scarred arms, bare feet on
packed sand. They walked past the men still sitting in the sand by the Slab, and none of those men rose.
They walked past the reset carts and the trough and the dark armory doors. They walked out through
the gate where Edda and Hamund stood aside to let them pass, and Odile kept her back to the hinge
post, and the gate stayed open.

Three thousand eight hundred.

Some of them looked at her as they passed. Their faces turned and stayed turned until the gate took
them. A few of them she knew. A scar. A way of walking. A number she had heard called across the sand.
They knew her too. They did not say her name. Out on the road, someone was already saying the other
one.

The grey woman passed last of her pen. She stopped in front of Anirak. She had the reed still in her
fist.

She held it up a moment. Then she put it in her shirt and walked on.

• • •

The Captain waited on the hill road beyond the gate, with the wagons and the crew. Coat on now. The
collar closed. The amber eyes burning dull in the dark of the hood.

The people went past him in a long grey river. He watched every one.

She came out last, her three behind her. Her chains hung cooling at her knees.

"Count," he said. The collar ground the word down to gravel.

"Three thousand eight hundred."

"And."

"One."

He looked at her a long moment. The pull went into him. She watched him feel it go, and put it aside,
and look at her hands.

"Who."

"The man with the keys. He had a hook at a singer's throat."

"Terms?"

"Once."

He nodded once. He turned back to the road and the river of people on it.

She waited beside him, shifting foot to foot. The chains swayed at her knees.

The mast stood over the empty pens behind them. Nothing came off it that any of them could feel.
