# Anirak Chronicle I: What Maw-11 Kept

The table in the Captain's cabin was too small for the map. She drew it anyway.

Charcoal on the planks. A ring for the Slab. A square for the yard. A long bar for the pen block, four
tiers deep, under the hill. One hard stroke for the mast above the pens, and a small square at its top
for the box.

She walked the table's edge while she drew. Round and round. The charcoal lifted only to move.

Ghostwind leaned in the doorway. Sylas, twenty-nine, the harness slack on his shoulders, the disc on
his breastbone ticking as the weather shifted outside. The Captain stood at the stern window, his back
to them both.

"Front gate." Her finger on it. "Yard. Slab. Three doors off the yard. Two to the armories. One down."

She drew the tunnels. The old ways under the main run. Drains beneath the staging floor. A feed chute,
bricked at the top, open at the bottom. A gap behind the triage station where the wall had settled.

"Ninety-one steps, chute to first grate. Dark all the way."

Ghostwind studied the lines a long while. Then her face.

"They'll know you at the gate."

"They know me everywhere."

He set a roll of oilcloth on the table and opened it. Bronze reeds, thirty, each as long as a forearm,
weighted at the foot. Ghostwind lifted one and hummed down it. The table
shook under her palms, low and slow.

Ghostwind set it down.

The Captain turned from the window. Collar open. The voice rough and low.

"Hold the yard an hour."

"Cost?"

"Don't stop moving."

• • •

Maw-11 sat in a fold of the southern coast hills. Grey stone. Low walls. A regional Maw, small beside
the Grand Maws. Three thousand eight hundred Branded in its pens.

She came up the road at dusk, her three behind her. Edda. Hamund. Odile. No hoods. The Captain's
wagons waited on the hill road below the gate.

The smell reached her before the walls did. Lamp oil and old sweat. Wet straw gone sour. Iron, and
the copper under iron that is blood dried a long time. It went into her nose and down into her chest
and lay there.

The mast stood over the hill behind the walls, black against the last light. Taller than she
remembered. A thin pole. A box at the top, its face turned down toward the pen block.

She knew what came off it. She had carried it in her arms for years, in that place. Slow blood. Heavy
limbs. Arms that could not lift high enough to try a grate. Tonight it touched nothing of her.

Two gate guards. Spears grounded. Lanterns on hooks.

The first looked up. His face turned to her and stayed. The spear-butt slid on the stone. He caught it
late.

The second looked. The same.

She walked between them. Neither moved until she was past.

Edda and Hamund took the gate behind her. Odile set her back to the hinge post.

• • •

The yard. Packed sand over stone, raked smooth in rows. Lamps on poles at the corners. The Slab in the
middle, a raised floor of fitted blocks, the seams stained dark. Blood in the sand. Blood on the Slab's edge,
the afternoon's, not yet swept.

Forty men. Slab crews at the reset carts with brooms and iron bars. Guards at the armory doors. Handlers
at the water trough, cups in hand.

Every face found her.

Cups stopped halfway to mouths. Brooms stopped. The noise of the yard went out. Forty faces held on her,
and she could not tell how much of that was hers.

Someone said it. Then several.

"Blades Fury."

She kept walking. Along the Slab's edge. Around it.

The guards came off the armory doors. Eight. Spears and hook-poles. The handlers came behind them with
clubs off the rack, twelve of them, bunched, clubs held too tight. The Slab crews stayed at the
carts, bars in their fists, watching.

She read them as she walked.

Guards: drilled. Spreading to flank. They would come together.

Handlers: they would come behind the guards.

Crews: they would come only if the rest went first.

Nobody in this yard has to die.

Hold them here. All of them alive.

She let the Fangs down off her forearms. Chain ran through her fingers. The hook-swords hung at her
knees and swung there, cold.

The lead guard came fast, spear low.

A Fang went out on its chain and hooked the haft below his hands. She pulled. The haft turned across
his hips and dragged him a step off his line. Her other hand had already gone over her shoulder. The
Morning Star came off her back with its talons open and caught the shaft at the hook. She wrenched. The
spear left him. He went past her empty-handed and fell on his side in the sand.

The pull came back up her arm heavier than she had sent it. She fed it into the next throw.

The links warmed where they crossed her fingers. Dull heat. Her heart went up a step.

Two guards at once. She ran the Fangs in a figure-eight, left and right in turn, crossing before her.
The air moaned through the links. One hook took a spear from a man's grip and threw it end over end
into the dark. The other opened a forearm to the bone. He dropped his pole and clutched the arm.

Each strike came back up her arms heavier than she sent it. Her shoulders loosened. The next throw went
out faster than the last.

A hook-pole came at her back. She felt the air move and stepped into the swing, inside the hook, and
took the shaft across her shoulder. It went into her and stayed in her. She turned with it and gave it
back through the Star into the man's thigh. He went down sideways.

The handlers bunched. Twelve, the last guards at the ends. They meant to rush her together.

She let her voice go.

Low in her throat. Under hearing. The crystal nodes in the gorget took it up and laid it out before
her, a long blade of it, forward.

The front rank's hands went to their chests. A man swayed. Another planted his club in the sand and
leaned on it. The man at the center stepped back to where his eye said he was clear. He stood braced a pace inside
the reach. The figure-eight came round and found him.

She opened the Star's head wide and swept it through them. Clubs spun out of hands. Men went to their
knees, heads down, retching into the sand, and stayed there. A guard tried to rise. His face found her
and held. He sat back down.

No one came again.

She kept moving. Round the Slab. Round again. The men on the ground lay still. Every face in the yard
was on her.

The chains glowed along their links. Dull amber. Her heart sat raised and steady.

• • •

She felt it in the stone. A grinding, slower than a heartbeat. Up through the sand into her boots, into her knees, into her
ribs. A few voices. Then more. Then many.

The pens were singing.

Ghostwind had found them. Ghostwind had put the reeds in their hands.

Up on the mast, the box began to tick. A sound like a cooling stove. Then a whine, high and thin, that
set the yard's dogs barking somewhere behind the armories.

Down there the weight would be coming off. Down there three thousand eight hundred would be lifting
their arms.

She kept walking. Round the Slab.

Then a hole tore in the low note, near the first grate. Voices stopping. Starting. Stopping again.

Someone was at the grate.

The third door off the yard stood a hand's width open. It had been shut when she came in. Lamp-light
below it, moving away.

She knew who held the key to that door.

She broke off. The chains came up off the sand. She went through the door at a run, the heat in the
links steady, climbing no higher.

• • •

Gethin Tamber ran the tunnels at Maw-11. He had run them in her years there. The staging. The armory
keys. The pens.

A free man. A careful one. Nothing left his tunnels uncounted.

He would have felt the singing in the floor wherever he stood. Every tunnel
master in the south had heard of the Blackout.

The staging rack inside the door held the hook-poles. She passed it at a run. One hook was gone.

• • •

The tunnel. Stone close on both sides. The ceiling low enough that tall men stooped. Lamps far apart,
most of them out. Water down the walls, pooling in the worn places of the floor. The pens' smell. She
had slept in it.

The chute was ninety-one steps from the first grate. She came from the staging floor instead. Forty
from there.

The singing filled the dark. Low. Uneven. Holes opening in it and closing.

The first grate. Iron bars floor to ceiling, crossbars at waist and head height, a lamp on a bracket.

Tamber stood at the bars with his back to her.

Beyond the grate, the first pen. Faces at the bars. Thin faces. Brands on the inner left forearms of the
hands that gripped iron. A bronze reed in a fist here and there. Some still singing. Some backed from the
bars, mouths shut.

One woman at the front. Grey-haired, arms scarred to the elbow. She held a reed to her lips and sang
into it and kept her place.

Tamber had the hook-pole through the bars. The hook was at her throat.

"Stop," he said. "Stop that, or it goes in."

She sang.

Anirak came down the last ten paces still moving, the Fangs swinging at her knees.

One man. One pole, hooked, set at a throat. His weight already leaning into it.

He is killing her now.

She spoke from the dark, once.

"Leave it. Walk."

He turned his head. The lamp caught her eyes. His face held on her. For a heartbeat he only looked. She
saw him know her. She saw him measure the four paces, the chain, the dark between, and come up short.

"Anirak," he said.

Beyond the bars, faces turned toward her as well. The singing faltered all along the grate. The low note
thinned.

The grey woman's voice held. Louder. Others found the note again around her.

She shifted her weight, step to step, the chains swaying. Tamber's eyes went back to the woman.

His shoulders set to drive the hook.

The Fang left her hand. Four paces of chain. The hook took his forearm above the wrist. She pulled. The
arm came back out of the bars and across his body, the pole with it, iron scraping iron. The Star was in
her other hand. Its talons closed on the shaft. She twisted. The pole went clattering down the tunnel.

He had a knife at his belt. He drew it.

His face stayed on the grate. He went back to it, low and fast, knife up, his arm going through the bars
for the woman's belly.

The Star swung once. Everything she had gathered in the yard came through her arm into the Star's head.
It met the side of his skull above the ear. The bone gave like a plank under a dropped anvil. His head
struck the bars. His body slid down them into the water on the floor.

The knife fell into the pen. The grey woman put her foot on it.

She sang through all of it.

• • •

Anirak walked. Two steps each way before the grate. She turned
her face down the tunnel, from the bars. The singing found its depth again.

It filled the tunnel. It filled the stone. She felt it in her teeth.

Far above, through rock, a crack. Like a mast parting in a gale. Then the thin whine stopped.

In the pen, a man put his hands on two bars and pulled.

The iron groaned. It bent outward. Flakes of rust burst off it. The crossbar sheared at its seat.

Others came to the bars beside him.

Ghostwind came out of the dark at the tunnel's far end, the harness humming faint on his back. He looked
at the body at the grate. He looked at her.

"Two through four are singing. Four's grates are down."

"Yard's clear."

"Then they walk."

He was gone, the air sucking after him.

• • •

They walked.

Up through the staging tunnels and out the door into the yard. Grey faces, scarred arms, bare feet on
packed sand. Past the men still sitting in the sand by the Slab. None of those men rose. Past the carts
and the trough and the dark armory doors. Out through the gate. Edda and Hamund stood aside. Odile kept
her back to the hinge post. The gate stood open.

Three thousand eight hundred.

Some of them looked at her as they passed. Faces turned and held until the gate took them. A few she
knew. A scar. A way of walking. A face she had seen across the sand. They knew her too. None of them
said a name. Out on the road someone was already shouting "Blades Fury."

The grey woman came last of her pen. She stopped before Anirak, the reed still in her fist.

She held it up a moment. Then she put it inside her shirt and walked on.

• • •

The Captain stood on the hill road beyond the gate, with the wagons and the crew. Coat on now. Collar
closed. The amber eyes burning dull in the dark of the hood. He was watching every face that passed
when she came up beside him, her three behind her, the chains cooling at her knees.

"Count." The collar ground it to gravel.

"Three thousand eight hundred."

"And."

"One."

His eyes held on her a beat. Then went to her hands.

"Who."

"The man with the keys. Hook at a singer's throat."

"Terms?"

"Once."

He nodded once and looked back to the road.

She waited beside him, shifting foot to foot. The chains swayed at her knees.

Behind them the mast stood over the empty pens. The box at its top hung split down the face, and cold.
