# The Council That Told Him No

*Locked canon, Batch 222, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1000`). Captain Alias Chronicle LIII, wave 18. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The dispute council had existed for two years by then, five rotating seats built to settle
disagreements between crew members without needing Kanja's word to end them — his own idea,
insisted on over the objections of hands who thought it was strange for a captain to hand away
authority he'd never been forced to share. This was the first time the council's ruling landed
against something he actually wanted.

The proposal was his: divert two days' sailing and a third of the crew's spare demolition stock to
reach a reported Directorate holding cell before a scheduled transfer moved the prisoners somewhere
harder to reach. The council — Corren Halst presiding that season, Maret Vos one of the four other
seats — heard him out in full, then voted three to two against it, on the grounds that the
intelligence was six days old, unconfirmed by a second source, and the diversion would leave two
already-committed crew elements without the demolition reserve they'd been promised for their own
operation.

"I could override this," Kanja said, once the vote was read. It wasn't a threat. It was closer to a
confession.

"You could," Halst agreed. "Nothing physical stops you. You built the thing that could stop you, and
it's five people and a vote, and you could walk through both without much effort at all."

"I'm not going to."

"I know. That's why we built it the way we did. A council that only wins when the captain agrees with
it isn't a council. It's a formality with extra steps." Halst set the tally sheet down. "You want to
argue the intelligence is better than six days old and unconfirmed, argue it. We'll hear it again.
But if the answer's still no, it stays no."

Kanja didn't argue it. He'd already run the same math the council had, before he ever brought the
proposal to them, and he knew the honest weight of "unconfirmed" as well as anyone in the room. What
he hadn't accounted for, and what sat with him for the rest of the day, was how much it cost to lose
an argument he still believed in, to people he'd trusted enough to make sure they could beat him.

Maret Vos found him that evening, checking the demolition stock that hadn't been diverted. "You're
allowed to think we're wrong," he said. "The council isn't built to make you agree with it. Just to
make sure you can't act like you don't need to."

"I know that. I built it that way on purpose."

"Then stop looking like a man who forgot why."

He didn't have a good answer for that either, and didn't try to manufacture one. The prisoners in the
holding cell were transferred four days later, before any better intelligence ever arrived to prove
the council right or wrong, and Kanja never brought it up again — not because the loss stopped
mattering, but because a council that only had to be respected when it happened to agree with him was
never the thing he'd meant to build.
