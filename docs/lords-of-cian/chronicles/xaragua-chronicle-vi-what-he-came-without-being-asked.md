# Xaragua Chronicle VI: What He Came Without Being Asked

*Locked canon, Batch 253, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1093`). Sixth Xaragua Chronicle, set after Chronicle V
("The Night He Was Let Into the Room," `MCD-1024`). Direct closing payoff to the long-arc flagged
since Batch 66 (`PH2-061`): Kanja "becomes one" of Arturo Salvatierra Duho's loved ones.*

---

Word reached him the way it always reached him now — sideways, through people who didn't know they
were telling him anything. A courier passing through on other business mentioned, in the middle of
an unrelated sentence, that the man at the head of Xaragua's table hadn't been seen outside his own
rooms in eleven days, and that when Yaisa moved through the territory now she moved like someone
guarding a door rather than running a city.

Nobody had sent for him. That was the part he noticed most, turning it over on the walk in.

Yaisa met him at the outer stair before he'd said a word, which told him she'd been told to expect
exactly no one and had decided on her own that he was the exception. She didn't ask how he'd heard.
She just looked at him for a long moment — the particular look of someone re-checking a thing she'd
already decided — and stepped aside.

Arturo was sitting up when he came in, which was clearly costing him something. The gray at his
temples had spread since the remembrance; his hands, folded on the table in front of him, had the
loose, careful stillness of a man conserving movement rather than resting.

"You weren't summoned," Arturo said. Not hostile. Testing, the way he tested everything.

"I know."

"Then why are you standing in my house."

"Because I heard you weren't well, and I didn't like it." He said it plainly, without the weight of
an explanation behind it, because there wasn't one. No territory needed him here. No dispute needed
settling. He had nothing to offer this room except that he'd come.

Arturo studied him the way he studied everyone — the look that had made governors and rival captains
alike feel weighed and filed before they'd finished a sentence. Whatever he was searching for, he
didn't find it, and for the first time since the two of them had met over that long, silent testing
week years before, that seemed to be the point rather than a failure of the method.

"I used the reverse face," Arturo said finally, answering a question that hadn't been asked aloud.
"On a man selling children out of a freight hold two streets from here. It costs what it costs." He
turned one hand over, an old gesture aimed at nothing. "Yaisa's seen me do it before. She doesn't ask
why anymore, she just makes sure I eat afterward. You don't have that history with me. You don't know
what it costs, and you came anyway."

"I didn't need to know what it costs to know you were paying it alone."

Something in Arturo's face — not quite a smile, not quite the flicker that preceded one — moved and
settled.

"Yaisa remembers who I was before any of this had a name," he said. "Before 'de la Muerte' meant
anything to anyone but me. That's hers. Nobody else gets that, because nobody else was there, and
I'm not interested in pretending otherwise for the sake of being generous." He let that sit a moment,
made sure it landed as fact rather than insult. "But you weren't there before, and you came anyway,
for nothing, with nothing to gain from it — and I've had forty years to learn the difference between
a man who's useful to me and a man who's just fond of me. You're the second kind. Yaisa's the first
kind too, now, but she started as the second and I only noticed later. You skipped the line."

From the doorway, not quite hidden, Yaisa made a small, unimpressed sound that was almost a laugh.
"He means yes."

"I know what he means." Arturo didn't look away from him. "I'm not calling you what she calls me.
That's hers to keep. But I'm not leaving you standing there nameless either, not after this." He
considered a moment, the first real amusement Kanja had seen cross his face since walking in. "Back
home, before any of this, there was a fish the old fishermen talked about — rides alongside the
shark, close enough to be eaten a hundred times over, and never is, because it never once asked to be
fed. Just stays. Guaikán, they called it. The one that travels with the thing that could kill it and
doesn't, because it chose to be there and the shark, eventually, stops minding."

"That's not my name."

"I know it isn't. I still don't know your name, and today I've decided I've stopped needing it."
Arturo reached for the cup at his elbow, and this time the effort didn't look quite so costly. "Sit
down, Guaikán. Yaisa, feed him too, he looks like he walked the whole way."

"I did."

"Of course you did." And for the first time — not measured, not weighed, not the careful diplomacy of
a man keeping five territories in balance — Arturo Salvatierra Duho laughed, short and real, and
Yaisa, setting a second cup down harder than necessary, told him exactly how long he'd kept a man
waiting on the stair before finally deciding to be glad to see him.
