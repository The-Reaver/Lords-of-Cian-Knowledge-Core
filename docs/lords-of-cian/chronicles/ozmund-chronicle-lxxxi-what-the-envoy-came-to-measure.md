# What the Envoy Came to Measure

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1810`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXI, House politics strand wave 3, second entry.*

---

The General was careful, telling me this one, to distinguish between two kinds of visitors a House
like Verehimu received, and he wanted me to understand the difference before I wrote a word of it
down: there were guests, who came to be received, and there were envoys, who came to look. Lord
Ansel Varnhelt of House Varnhelt was unmistakably the second kind, and the General said he'd known it
within minutes of the man's arrival, though it took him rather longer to work out exactly what he
was being looked at for.

House Varnhelt held minor but respected standing among the eastern Houses, its wealth built on a
chain of coastal warehouses rather than land, and it had, for two generations, sent its daughters
into marriages with Houses it judged solid rather than merely prosperous — a strategy the General
said had served it well enough that Varnhelt now held more genuine influence, quietly, than several
Houses that outranked it on paper. Ozmund was twenty-four when the envoy arrived, ostensibly to
discuss a modest trade arrangement in preserved goods, and Aethelgard had told him plainly, the
evening before, exactly what the visit actually was: House Varnhelt was assessing House Verehimu as
a potential match for its youngest daughter, and Lord Varnhelt himself had come to do the assessing,
because he trusted no one else's report of a House's true character over three days of his own
observation.

The General said the trade discussions themselves were almost incidental — Varnhelt raised them,
conducted them competently, and clearly cared about their outcome not at all. What he actually did,
across those three days, was talk. He was an easy conversationalist, warm in the particular
practiced way of a man who has learned that warmth loosens tongues faster than pressure does, and on
the second evening, over wine that flowed rather more freely than the first night's had, he steered
the conversation toward a neighboring House with which Varnhelt itself had an old and, by his own
account, thoroughly justified grievance — a boundary matter, decades old, that Varnhelt still
considered an outright theft dressed up in legal language. He laid the grievance out at length,
invited Ozmund's opinion on it directly, and waited, with the particular attentive stillness of a man
who already believes he knows what answer flattery will produce.

The General told me he understood, almost as soon as the question landed, exactly what was being
tested. Varnhelt wasn't actually interested in his opinion of a boundary dispute two Houses over. He
was interested in whether the young man across the table would take the easy path — agree readily
with his host's grievance, disparage the absent House to win an evening's goodwill, and thereby
reveal himself as someone whose good opinion of anyone could be bought cheaply, with wine and
flattery and an audience of one. It was, the General said, precisely the kind of test that costs
nothing to fail and nothing to pass, which was exactly why it told a careful observer so much about
a man's character.

He didn't take the easy path, and he was equally careful, he told me, not to overcorrect into its
opposite — a pointed refusal to engage would have told Varnhelt something just as clear, and just as
unflattering, about a young man too rigid to hold an ordinary conversation. What he said instead was
that he knew only House Verehimu's own side of that dispute, had never heard the other House's
account of it directly, and didn't think it fair to either party to render a verdict on a
disagreement he'd have to invent half the evidence for. He said it lightly, without any weight of
rebuke in it, and turned the conversation, a moment later, to a question of his own — about
Varnhelt's warehouses, and how the man had built a House's standing on trade rather than land in a
region that still measured most things in acreage. Varnhelt answered at length, and warmly, and the
General said he watched something shift in the older man's posture as he did — not disappointment,
exactly, but the particular satisfaction of a man who has just confirmed something he'd hoped, rather
than merely suspected, would prove true.

Lord Varnhelt left three days later having concluded a trade arrangement of genuinely minor
consequence and, the General later learned through a letter Aethelgard received the following spring,
a rather more favorable private report on House Verehimu's heir than either of them had been led to
expect from a visit ostensibly about preserved goods. The marriage itself never came to anything — the
General was vague, telling me this, about exactly why, and I never pressed him on it — but he said he
carried the shape of that evening with him afterward regardless, as one of the earlier times he
understood that a man testing your character rarely announces that he's doing it, and that the surest
way to fail such a test was very often to try too visibly to pass it.
