# The Delegation That Never Saw a Soldier

The delegation had come to assess, not to fight — a small, cautious party from a foreign power
weighing whether backing the Rebellion was worth the risk, or whether staying neutral until a clearer
winner emerged was the safer wager. They would see what they were shown, report what they saw, and a
decision worth years of material support would follow from an impression formed over three days.

"They're not here to be deceived onto a battlefield," the apprentice said, going over the plan with
Kanja the night before the delegation's arrival. "There's no ambush to spring, no patrol to redirect.
They just need to leave believing we're stronger and better organized than we actually are right now,
and they need to leave believing it without ever catching us performing it."

It was, in its own way, the gentlest use the craft had ever been put to — no false signal aimed at
drawing violence anywhere, no evacuation riding on its success, nothing that would cost anyone their
life if it failed outright. What it cost instead, if it worked, was years of a foreign power's
material support riding on an impression built almost entirely out of sound: overlapping drill
chants from three separate camps staged to sound like six, a marching cadence relayed and doubled
back through a valley until it read as twice the column it actually was, watch-calls passed at
intervals precise enough to suggest a discipline the crew hadn't yet fully earned.

The delegation's senior envoy remarked, on the second evening, that she had never heard an
undersupplied rebellion sound so thoroughly rehearsed. Neither the apprentice nor Kanja corrected her.

By the third day, the delegation had seen no battle, engaged no soldier, and verified nothing beyond
what their own ears had told them across two nights of camp sound carrying convincingly through open
country. Their report home recommended support.

"We didn't lie to them about anything that mattered," the apprentice said afterward, turning the
distinction over as if she needed to hear herself settle it. "We just let them hear more of us than
there currently is."

"That's still a lie," Kanja said. "It's just one that costs a foreign envoy nothing to have believed,
and costs us everything if the support that follows from it never actually arrives to make the sound
true."
