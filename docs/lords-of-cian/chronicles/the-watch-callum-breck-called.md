# The Watch Callum Breck Called

*Locked canon, Batch 286, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1515`). Captain Alias Chronicle XCV, wave 32. A detailed Long Mask-era combat showcase (the Forge-Coat, Ironfall Boots, Ironhand Gauntlets, and the Rexmar Machete) led under Callum Breck's chairmanship for the first time, testing his silent-signal command style at real stakes. The resettlement convoy was two days from Pier Nine and moving slow — forty wagons, mostly families, mostly the kind of people the war had never asked whether they wanted to be moved at all — when Breck's own scouts read the opportunist band shadowing its flank from the tree line. He didn't call for Kanja first. He called the formation first, the way a chair was supposed to, and let Kanja fall into the line like any other asset the plan required.

What he called it with was the same thing he'd built out of four silent months after Nev Torr died under his watch: hand signs, not shouted orders, a whole vocabulary developed originally because his voice hadn't been available and kept afterward because it turned out to carry farther than sound across open ground, and gave nothing away to anyone listening for a voice to target. Twenty wagons held position behind a bend where the tree line broke line of sight. Twenty kept moving at an unbroken pace, bait that didn't know it was bait, escorted by fighters trained to look like nothing more than tired outriders.

Kanja walked the second column in plain sight, in full gear, waiting on Breck's own signal instead of his own instinct — a discipline that cost him something visible in the set of his shoulders every time the raiders' approach quickened and his hand didn't move toward the Rexmar Machete's hilt on his own timing.

The signal came at the tree line's exact edge. The first volley of thrown weight — sling-stones meant to spook the wagon teams into a stampede — met the Forge-Coat's plating and the Ironfall Boots' grounding stance together, Kanja turning his own footing into the redirect: the collected force driven sideways into open ground instead of through canvas and children, the impact traveling harmlessly out through packed earth he'd already read half a second before the stones ever left their slings — the same instinctive Rexmar-Mar tactical sense for ground and load that had never needed anything but his own body to work. The Ironhand Gauntlets came next, one leveraged strike collapsing the loose scree the raiders had chosen for their firing position rather than the raiders standing on it, denying them footing without denying them their lives — leverage and trained technique through riveted plate, nothing discharged, nothing named. Then Kanja moved through eleven scattering footsteps with nothing but the Rexmar Machete and two hundred years of trained swordsmanship, reading the gap between one raider's committed lunge and the next's hesitation the way he'd learned to read a battlefield long before he'd ever touched a Talisman, and turned every one of his own strikes into a disarm rather than a kill, the way skill alone always could when the fight allowed for it.

All eleven raiders were down, unarmed and breathing, inside four minutes. Not one wagon so much as rocked off its wheels.

Breck found Kanja afterward at the treeline's edge, checking a scattered weapon's edge for a maker's mark out of old habit. "You held the column even when it looked wrong to hold it," Kanja said. "That's harder than anything the Forge-Coat and this blade know how to do."

"I kept waiting to freeze," Breck admitted. "Kept waiting for it to be Nev Torr's line again, one wrong second too slow." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't. Turns out the hardest part of the job was never the moment itself. It was trusting I'd earned the right to call it before I actually had to."
