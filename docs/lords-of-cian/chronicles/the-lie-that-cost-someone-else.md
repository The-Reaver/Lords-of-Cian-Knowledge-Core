# The Lie That Cost Someone Else

*Locked canon, Batch 157, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-494`). The Crow King Alias Chronicle X, first entry in
the fourth wave. Rebellion era, age 23. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material — the
first genuine failure entry in this alias's run. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new
named characters.*

---

The false signal was built to draw a Directorate patrol away from a supply convoy's real route,
seeded into a stretch of farmland the Hymn-Engine's resonance had used safely a dozen times before —
except this time, a family working the same field at dawn heard the false alarm meant only for
trained ears, and fled their own home believing an attack was imminent, straight into the path of the
very patrol the signal had been built to redirect.

Nobody died. The family was questioned, frightened, and released — but the harm was real, caused
entirely by a deception meant for someone else, landing instead on people who'd had no part in the
war at all.

"I built that signal to protect the convoy," Kanja told the crew afterward, the account of the
frightened family laid out plainly rather than minimized. "I didn't build it to consider a family
working that field at an hour I hadn't accounted for. That's a real gap in the method, not a
misfortune I can simply file away as unavoidable."

"The convoy made it through untouched," the crew's own tactician offered, not quite an excuse, more
an attempt to weigh the outcome fairly. "That's not nothing."

"It's not nothing. It's also not the whole account. A family got questioned and terrified because a
lie built for someone else found them instead." Kanja didn't accept the deflection, sat with the
discomfort of it instead. "I've spent years being careful about what a deception costs the people it's
aimed at. I hadn't spent nearly enough thought on what it costs the people who simply happen to be
standing nearby when it goes off. That's a blind spot in the whole method, and I don't think
Chronicle-worthy successes should crowd out the account of when it actually fails someone."

He spent the following weeks building a new verification step into every future signal — a mapping
pass to account for exactly the kind of bystander presence that had gone unconsidered this time,
imperfect but a genuine correction rather than a comfortable excuse.

"Will that catch everything?" the tactician asked.

"No. Nothing will catch everything. But pretending the gap doesn't exist because the last outcome was
mostly fine would be its own kind of dishonesty. I'd rather build the correction and still fail
sometimes than never look at the failure honestly at all."
