# The House Guard's Own Doubt

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1733`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle IV. Set in the same general span as Chronicle III, still strictly pre-Book-1 —
Aethelgard Verehimu alive, the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) still in the future. Narrated by Red
Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructing a story told to him about a cost
Draconis (`CC-085`) paid among his own peers for a devotion he could never fully explain, witnessed
firsthand by "the General" without his being able to intervene or repay it. No new named characters.*

---

Most of what Draconis gave up for the General, the General told me, he gave up in rooms the General
was never meant to see. This one he saw by accident, standing where he shouldn't have been standing,
young enough still to think that made it his fault.

By then Draconis had held the inner escort long past the point where anyone questioned his skill.
What they questioned, increasingly, in the low unofficial way soldiers question a thing they'd never
say to a commander's face, was his judgment — specifically, the plain fact that a man who could have
had his pick of postings kept choosing the same one, year after year, for a ward who by every visible
measure needed less protecting than half the household. The General trained like any highborn son
trained. He rode, hunted, sat through his lessons, took the occasional bruise sparring like anyone
his age, and gave, to the ordinary eye watching him do any of it, absolutely no sign of being worth
the weight Draconis put on keeping him alive. Other men in that guardhouse had wards of their own, or
hoped to. None of them understood why this one man had built his whole career, quietly and by choice,
around the least remarkable boy in the house.

It came to a point one evening in the guardhouse mess, over a posting the General only learned about
afterward, and only in fragments — a coastal command, a real one, the kind that made careers, offered
to Draconis on the strength of the road years before and left open for him to claim if he'd only ask.
He didn't ask. He let it go to a man beneath him in seniority instead, so that he could keep the inner
escort himself, and the guardhouse, which had watched him pass over better postings before without
much comment, finally said the thing out loud that it had been saying quietly for years. A senior man
— older than Draconis, senior enough to say it and be heard — put it to him plainly, in front of
enough of the others that it couldn't be taken back afterward: that a soldier owed his House his skill,
not his sentiment, and that whatever debt Draconis thought he was still paying off a road he'd bled on
once, years ago, he was paying it in coin the House hadn't asked for and didn't need spent that way.

Draconis didn't explain himself. That was the part the General dwelt on longest, telling it to me —
not that Draconis lost the argument, because by every account he didn't raise his voice long enough to
have one, but that he had nothing to offer in his own defense except the truth as far as he understood
it, which was that he had made the boy a promise on a road once and intended to keep it, and knew even
as he said it how thin that sounded held up next to a coastal command. He couldn't say the real reason,
because he didn't have it. He only had the shape of a conviction with the substance carved out of it,
and he stood there and let it look, to every man in that room, exactly as unconvincing as it was.

The General was in the doorway for the last of it, come looking for him over some errand long since
forgotten, and stayed exactly long enough to hear the quiet that followed rather than any answer at
all, and to watch Draconis take the silence the way a man takes a blow he's decided not to duck. He
told me he understood, even at that age, that something had just been spent that he could never see
the ledger for and could never pay back, because the very reason Draconis needed no repayment was the
reason he could never learn what the debt had actually cost. He couldn't stand in that doorway and say
you're not wasting your career on nothing, because saying it would have meant saying everything else
along with it. He could only watch a man he loved take a smaller life than he'd earned, for a reason
he wasn't allowed to give, and walk away before either of them had to acknowledge he'd seen it happen
at all.

He told me the coastal posting went to the other man within the month, and did, in fact, make that
man's career the way it should have made Draconis's. Draconis never once mentioned it again, to him or
to anyone, and stayed exactly where he'd chosen to stay. The General said that was the year he first
understood, in a way he hadn't quite let himself before, that loyalty built on a lie of omission wasn't
free even when it looked like devotion from the outside — that somebody was always paying for it, and
it was never going to be him.
