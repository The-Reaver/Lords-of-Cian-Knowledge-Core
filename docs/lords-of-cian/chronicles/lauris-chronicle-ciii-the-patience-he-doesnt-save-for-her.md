# Lauris Chronicle CIII: The Patience He Doesn't Save for Her

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1720`). One hundred third entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of the second 50-Chronicle wave (Chronicles LX-CIX), the eighth entry of the
"Strand W" (Witness / present-day, quiet-register) thread within this wave. A third, deliberately
non-training-deck entry with Valen (Sinisterblade) -- where `MCD-1664` dramatized their counted
sparring and `MCD-1668` their wordless sparring, this one moves entirely off the training deck to
show his Master-at-Arms patience (`MCD-291`) spent on an ordinary sentry rather than on her, putting
`CC-134`'s combat-joy trait on the page as an observer's pleasure rather than a participant's for the
first time in her Valen material. No new named characters -- the sentry is deliberately left unnamed.
Written under Abad's blanket authorization: "go."*

---

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Valen corrected the same stance twenty
times this morning, for someone who will likely never need it in earnest. He did not sound tired by
the twentieth correction. I watched the whole of it and found I was not tired either."*

I have written two accounts already of what passes between Lauris and Valen on a training deck, and
I said, closing the second of them, that I believed I had shown a reader both faces of what their
companionship actually is. I was wrong, and I am glad to have been wrong, because the morning I am
about to set down showed me a third face neither of the first two had prepared me for: Valen, patient
with someone who will never come anywhere near matching him, in a register I do not believe he has
ever once used with Lauris herself.

She had not come to spar. She had come, by her own account to me afterward, only to return a
whetstone she had borrowed, and found the courtyard already occupied by Valen and a young sentry I
will not name here, since I do not believe the account requires it and I have no wish to embarrass a
man whose only fault that morning was being ordinary in the company of someone who is not. He held
his guard the way a nervous, earnest person holds a thing they have been told is important and do not
yet trust themselves to understand why. Valen circled him once, said nothing, and corrected the angle
of his forward foot with two fingers, without comment.

Lauris did not announce herself. She has never needed to. She simply took a seat on the low wall at
the courtyard's edge, the whetstone still in her hand, and watched.

I want to be precise about what she watched, because I think the precision is the whole of what makes
the account worth keeping. The sentry was not good. He will likely never be good, in the sense the
word carries for anyone measured against Valen's own standard, and I do not believe Valen expected
otherwise going in. He corrected the same stance perhaps twenty times across the better part of an
hour -- the same shoulder drifting the same fraction wide, the same weight settling a beat too early
-- and I did not once hear his voice change register between the first correction and the twentieth.
No sharpness crept into it. No visible fatigue with the repetition showed in his own bearing, the way
I have watched even patient instructors elsewhere in this account eventually let slip.

"You do not sound tired of him," Lauris said, once the sentry had been dismissed to rest, in the flat
observational register I have come to recognize as her genuine curiosity rather than a compliment
dressed as a question.

"I am not tired of him."

"I have watched you grow tired of far less repetition, with me."

"You waste less each time I correct you," Valen said. "He wastes the same amount each time. That is
not his failing. That is simply where he is. I am not comparing him to you. I would not know how to
correct him if I were."

She considered this with the particular seriousness she gives most things she is turning over rather
than arguing. "Then what are you doing, correcting the same stance twenty times, if not moving him
toward anything you can measure."

"I am moving him toward the twenty-first correction," Valen said. "That is the whole of the measure,
for someone at his level. Tomorrow it may take eighteen. That is worth an hour of my morning."

I do not think I had ever, before that courtyard, seen the full shape of what `MCD-291` means when it
names him Kanja's Master-at-Arms rather than simply the crew's finest blade. A finest blade owes
nothing to a man who will never carry one competently. A Master-at-Arms, I came to understand watching
him that morning, owes exactly the same unhurried, unresentful correction to the sentry who will
plateau at ordinary competence as he owes to the one person on Cian who might someday meet his own
standard. I watched Lauris watch him understand this, and I watched her own particular pleasure --
the one I have described elsewhere as unqualified and competent, spent on nothing more dangerous than
a training deck she was not even standing on -- settle into something quieter than I usually get to
record of her: not the joy of exertion, but the joy of recognizing a discipline she shares, worn by
someone else, in a register she rarely gets to see him wear it in.

"You would correct me the same way," she said, near the end of it, "if I plateaued tomorrow at
something considerably less than what I am now."

"Yes," Valen said, without hesitation, the way he answers most things that are, to him, simply true
and not worth dressing further. "I would not correct you less carefully. I would only correct you
less often, because you would need it less often. The care does not change with the ceiling."

She held the borrowed whetstone in both hands for a moment, turning it once, and I understood, watching
her, that she had come to the courtyard to return a small tool and was leaving it instead with
something considerably larger than she had arrived carrying.

"I like knowing that," she said. "I do not think I needed to know it. I am glad I do now regardless."

He did not answer that, and I do not believe he needed to. I record the morning here not for anything
that was won or lost in it -- nothing was, in either sense the word usually carries in this account --
but because I believe a reader who has met Valen only across the deck he shares with Lauris deserves,
once, to see the same patience spent on someone who will never once make him work for it, and to watch
her find that, if anything, the more admirable use of it.
