# The Envoy Who Came to Provoke

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1814`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXV, House politics strand wave 3, sixth entry.*

---

The General told me he'd met a great many hostile men in his life by the time he told me this story,
and that almost none of them had taught him as much as Ser Dravot Skarne did in a single afternoon,
without ever once raising his voice or making a threat that could have been repeated back to anyone
as such.

House Skarne held a modest holding two days' ride south of Verehimu land, and carried, the General
explained, a grievance against House Verehimu old enough that neither side could any longer agree on
its origin — something to do, as best he ever pieced together, with a contested inheritance three
generations back that Skarne's family had lost and never stopped believing had been decided unfairly.
It wasn't an active feud. There had been no violence between the two Houses in living memory, no open
conflict of any kind. It was simply a cold, patient resentment that Skarne's family had apparently
kept alive across three generations the way some families keep a family Bible — carefully, and
without ever quite explaining to outsiders why it mattered so much to them still.

Ser Dravot Skarne arrived at House Verehimu, Ozmund twenty-six by then, ostensibly to discuss a
minor dispute over grazing rights along the shared border of their two holdings — a genuine matter,
though one small enough that the General said he suspected, correctly as it turned out, that it was
mostly a pretext for something else. Skarne was, from the first hour, a study in controlled
provocation: courteous in every literal sense, never once crossing into open insult, and yet
constructing, sentence by careful sentence, a conversation designed to extract either an apology for
the old inheritance dispute or a visible loss of composure, either of which he clearly intended to
carry home and repeat to his own household as proof of something.

He raised the old grievance directly, more than once, framing it each time as an aside rather than an
accusation — a passing reference to "what your family took from mine," delivered with a small,
pointed smile, immediately followed by a return to the grazing matter as though nothing had been
said. He praised House Verehimu's holdings, at length, in a manner the General said carried an
unmistakable undertone of *held illegitimately and held well regardless*. He asked, at one point,
whether Ozmund had ever actually read the original inheritance ruling himself, in a tone that made
plain he expected the answer to be no, and that the admission would let him claim, later, that the
current heir hadn't even bothered to understand the wrong his family stood accused of.

The General said what he found hardest, in the moment, wasn't the provocation itself but the
patience it demanded — Skarne never gave him a single clean opening to respond to, nothing so
direct that answering it wouldn't have meant either conceding ground he had no standing to concede on
a three-generation-old dispute he hadn't lived through, or losing his temper over something too
subtle to justify losing it over. He said he understood, sitting through it, that the entire
conversation was built as a trap with no honest way through the middle of it — only two ways to fail,
dressed up to look like a real choice.

So he didn't choose either. He let Skarne's references to the old grievance pass without
acknowledgment each time, neither denying them nor engaging with them, and answered every question
about the inheritance ruling with plain, unadorned honesty — yes, he had read it, some years before,
at his father's own insistence that he understand every unresolved dispute the House carried; no, he
didn't consider himself equipped to relitigate a three-generation-old ruling in an afternoon's
conversation about grazing rights; and if House Skarne wished to formally reopen the matter through
proper channels, House Verehimu would receive that request with the same seriousness it gave any
other. He said the words carried no warmth and no coldness either — he'd worked, he told me, very
hard in the moment to keep them entirely level, a plain statement of fact offered without the
satisfaction of a reaction attached to it.

Skarne left two days later with a modest, entirely reasonable adjustment to the grazing agreement and,
as far as the General ever learned, nothing else — no apology extracted, no loss of composure to
carry home, no fresh grievance to add to the old one. The General told me he never heard from House
Skarne again in any capacity beyond the ordinary business the grazing agreement occasionally required,
and that he took a particular, quiet satisfaction from that outcome specifically: not a victory, in
any sense Skarne himself would have recognized as one, but the plain, undramatic denial of the
victory the man had actually come for.
