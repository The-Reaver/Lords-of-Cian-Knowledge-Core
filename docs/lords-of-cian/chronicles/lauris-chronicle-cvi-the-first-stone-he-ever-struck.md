# Lauris Chronicle CVI: The First Stone He Ever Struck

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Orlok took me to the stone he struck first,
seventy-six thousand years ago by his own count, before he had a name for what he was doing to it. It
is still standing. He seemed surprised, each time he visits, that it still is. I did not tell him I
thought that was the least surprising part of the whole story."*

I have written one account already of Orlok on a rare visit to this crew, and I called it, at the
time, an evening in which nothing happened in the sense the word usually carries in a chronicle. I
want to set down a second account of him here in which considerably more happens, though almost none
of it in words -- because Lauris asked him, some months after that first evening, a question I do not
believe anyone else in this account has ever thought to put to him directly: whether the place still
stood.

He did not ask her what place she meant. I understood, watching the two of them, that he had known
immediately, the way a man knows immediately when someone has finally asked the one question he has
spent seventy-six thousand years expecting and not receiving.

The volcanic-ridge extraction site -- now, per the record I have already set down elsewhere, part of
Celestial Zenith's own territory -- is not, by any ordinary measure, a place worth a pilgrimage. It is
a scar of old rock and older ash, unremarkable to anyone who does not know what it once was, and I do
not believe either of them expected the journey to take as long as it did, since Orlok, for reasons he
did not explain and Lauris did not press, chose to walk the final distance rather than close it by any
faster means available to either of them.

We found the stone itself some hours after arriving -- a plain outcrop, weathered smooth in places
that had nothing to do with weather, marked by a shallow, ancient depression no larger than a
handspan, struck so many thousands of times across a childhood that the rock had simply, eventually,
begun to answer.

"This is where it started," Orlok said, and I want to note that his voice carried none of the
practiced stillness I have come to associate with him across the whole of this account -- something
closer, for once, to a boy's own memory surfacing in a body that has long since stopped being a boy's.
"I did not know, the first time I struck it, that I was doing anything at all. I struck it because
striking rock was the only work a miner's son was given, and I noticed, eventually, that my strikes
left more of a mark than the other boys' did."

Lauris knelt beside the depression and set her own palm flat against the stone, not striking it, only
resting against it, with the particular seriousness I have watched her bring to very few objects in
this account. "Seventy-six thousand years and no institution ever taught you this."

"No institution ever knew to look for it here. I was too unimportant to be found, and then too old to
need finding." He knelt beside her, an old man's motion in a body that carries no true age in its
bearing, and set his own hand near hers without quite touching it. "I have never shown anyone this
stone. I do not think I have ever wanted to, before now."

"Why now?"

He considered the question with the same unhurried patience I have watched him bring to every question
in this account, turning it over rather than answering it quickly. "Because you asked whether it still
stood, rather than asking me what it meant. I have been asked what it meant a great many times, by
people who wanted a lesson from it. I have never once been asked simply whether the stone was still
there."

She did not answer that directly. I want to record instead what she did, since I believe the account
is truer for it than any reply she might have given: she struck the stone once herself, with a
fraction of a fraction of what I have watched her spend on a burst water main or a fortified door,
calibrated so precisely that the sound it made was barely louder than the sound a miner's son's own
first strike must have made, seventy-six thousand years before either of them stood there.

"It held," she said.

"It has always held," Orlok said. "That was never the question I could not answer for myself. The
question was whether I would still recognize it, after so long, as the place I actually began. I find
that I do."

They stayed until the light changed, saying very little further, in the low conversational register I
have already written of as characteristic of the evenings he spends with her. I do not believe either
of them would call the day remarkable, in the sense the word carries for the rest of this crew's
story -- no battle was fought there, no secret surfaced that this account is not already permitted to
carry. But I watched a man who has kept seventy-six thousand years of memory, quietly, without ever
once setting the weight down in company, show one person the exact place the weight began, and I
watched her receive it the only way I believe he actually wanted it received: without ceremony, without
a lesson drawn from it, simply as a true thing that had happened to a boy who did not yet know what
striking rock would eventually make of him.
