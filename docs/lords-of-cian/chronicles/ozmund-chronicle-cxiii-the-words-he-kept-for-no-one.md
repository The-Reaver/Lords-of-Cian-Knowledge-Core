# The Words He Kept for No One

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1842`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CXIII, eighth and closing entry of part A of the coming-of-age strand's second wave
(part B continues the wave separately), set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard
alive and the succession entirely uncontested throughout. A private, undramatic habit — drafting,
periodically, a letter addressed to no one and never sent, an attempt to articulate for himself alone
what kind of man he wanted to be — distinct from the House's formal copied lineage record shown in

I will close this part of the account the way the General closed it for me, with a habit rather than an
event, because by the time he had told me enough of these small private things I had come to understand
that the events were never really the center of him at all. The events were what other people saw. The
habits were what he actually was, in the hours no one else was present to witness them, and this is the
last of the ones he ever gave me from that stretch of his life, and perhaps the plainest.

He told me he had already described to me, once, the copying of the House's line — the formal record
every heir of Verehimu sets down by hand once in their youth, back to Drakmund, in the old script, on
paper meant to outlast its ink twice over. What he had not told me, he said, was that somewhere in the
years around that same copying, he began a second kind of writing that no custom of the House had ever
asked of him, and that no one but himself ever read a single word of.

It had no fixed form, by his own account, and no fixed occasion either — some quiet evening, once every
season or two, when whatever he was turning over in his mind had gotten too large or too unsettled to
simply sit with unexamined, he would take a single sheet of paper, unmarked by any House seal or
ceremony, and begin it the same way every time: *to whoever asks, someday, what kind of man I actually
was.* Not to his father, who would ask him such things directly and receive an honest answer without
needing a letter for it. Not to his mother, whose own disciplines had no patience for anything so
indirect. Not even, he told me, to himself exactly, though it was himself alone who ever read it — to
some imagined someone, far enough removed from his actual life that he could tell them the plain truth
of a thing without weighing how the telling would land on anyone who actually knew him.

He told me what went into those pages changed from one sitting to the next, and that most of it, read
back later, struck him as ordinary and a little foolish — the same worries a hundred young men his age
were likely working through in a hundred different forms, dressed up in slightly more careful language
because careful language was the only tool he had ever been given for anything. But underneath the
ordinary worry, he said, a few things recurred often enough across those years that he came to recognize
them as something closer to a true accounting of what he actually valued, once the performance of being
watched was entirely removed from the page: that he wanted, whatever else became of him, to be a man
whose word to the people beneath him meant something he did not have to enforce; that he wanted to be
trusted for reasons that had nothing to do with what his blood could do to anyone who crossed him; that
he was frightened, more often than he let anyone see, of becoming a man remembered only for a power he
had never once chosen and never would.

He told me he burned most of those pages, over the years, once whatever had prompted them had settled
enough in his own mind that he no longer needed the paper to hold it for him. He kept none of them
deliberately, he said — the writing was never meant to be a record for anyone else to find, and he would
have been mortified, he told me with real feeling, to imagine some future steward discovering a drawer
full of a young lord's private uncertainties and treating them as anything but exactly what they were:
one man's honest attempt to know his own mind, written for an audience of no one, and burned once its
purpose was served.

He told me, giving me this last of them, that he did not know why he had never once, in all those years,
thought to address a single one of those letters to a name instead of a stranger — not his father, who
would have welcomed it, not Osric, not anyone he actually trusted. He said he supposed, thinking about
it honestly for the first time in my hearing, that the whole use of the habit had depended on there
being no one real on the other end of it at all — that only a page addressed to no one could ever hold
the full truth of a young man still working out, quietly and privately and without any of it settled
yet, exactly what kind of man he meant to become.
