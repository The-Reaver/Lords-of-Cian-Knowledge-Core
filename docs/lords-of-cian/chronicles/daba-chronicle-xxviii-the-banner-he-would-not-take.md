The Ashfield Banner numbered close to six hundred fighters by the time Bramm Oskell came to find Daba himself, which made it, by a wide margin, the largest organized resistance force operating anywhere near the districts 1804 had spent years learning to move through unseen. Oskell was not a subtle man, and he did not pretend to be one during the two hours he spent laying his offer out across a borrowed table -- his Banner would take 1804's name, his fighters would answer to 1804's discipline where it made sense to, and in exchange 1804 would gain, overnight, six hundred bodies, a dozen fortified positions, and a reputation loud enough that the Trust would have to answer it with something more than a clerk's shrug.

"You'd be the largest resistance force in the Sovereign Trust's whole northern reach," Oskell said. "Not the quietest anymore. The largest. There's a version of fighting them where quiet stops being the smartest thing to be."

Nessa argued for it that same night, in the cellar that had stopped being a cellar in any literal sense years before but had kept the name out of habit, and argued for it harder than Daba had heard her argue for anything since the naming. Six hundred fighters meant districts that could finally be held rather than merely visited. It meant grain moved by the wagonload instead of the handcart, wounds treated in real infirmaries instead of borrowed back rooms, a hundred families protected openly instead of a dozen protected in secret. She did not accuse him of anything. She only laid the arithmetic in front of him, plainly, the way she laid every hard number in front of him, and let him sit with what it cost to refuse it.

"You've spent years telling me small is how we survive," she said. "Tell me honestly this isn't just fear of the thing getting big enough to matter."

Daba did not answer quickly, because it was not a small question and he had learned, long before Nessa ever asked him anything, not to give easy answers to questions that deserved better. He thought of Marn, discovering a role he'd never known he held. He thought of Rhyne, learning a second craft at real cost to the one she loved. He thought, longer than either of those, of the number the network carried as its own name, and of how easily six hundred fighters under one recognizable banner became, to anyone counting from the Trust's side of the ledger, a target with a return address.

"Oskell's Banner is six hundred people who can be seen," he said finally. "Everything we've built is built on the fact that nobody can find the whole of us in one place, because there isn't a whole of us anywhere to find. The day we take his name, we stop being a hundred separate nights that never look like anything, and we start being one army with a known shape. Every position he's fortified is a position the Trust already knows to watch. Every fighter who answers to us by name is a fighter whose capture tells them something true about who we are. We wouldn't be absorbing his strength. We'd be inheriting his exposure."

"So we let six hundred fighters go on getting hunted the way they've always been hunted, because folding them in might one day cost us something worse."

"Yes," Daba said, and did not soften it, because softening it would have been its own kind of lie. "I won't pretend that's not a cost. It is one. Families who might have been safer under a bigger name will go on being only as safe as we can manage in pieces too small to be worth the chasing. I've made that trade before and I'll go on making it, because the one time I build something large enough to be seen is the one time everything else I've built becomes findable through it."

What he offered instead, and what Oskell eventually took, though not without making plain how much smaller he judged it than what he'd come asking for, was intelligence shared freely in both directions, routes and warnings passed without either force ever answering to the other's name, and a standing promise that 1804's own dispersed cells would, when it cost them nothing to do so, quietly steer trouble away from the Banner's fortified ground rather than toward it. It was not the alliance Oskell had wanted. It kept both of them, in the end, alive and separately unremarkable, which was, Daba told Nessa afterward, the only kind of alliance he had ever learned how to trust.

She did not tell him he was wrong. She only said, some weeks later, watching a wagon of grain move in six small pieces along six separate roads the way it always had, that she still thought about the infirmaries they might have built, and Daba said that he thought about them too, and that thinking about them was the price, paid honestly, for everything the smallness had bought them instead.
