# The Reckoning at the Dock

*Locked canon, Batch 263, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1347`). Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle LXXIV, wave 25. A hostile Trust administrator, using the expensive false positive as leverage, moves to strip the school of the regulatory standing written into naval law -- a purely institutional confrontation with no combat.*

---

The administrator had never liked the storm-interval regulation, had said as much when it was written, and the false call gave him the first real opening he'd had in two seasons to unmake it. He convened the hearing in the same dockside hall where the liaison had once read the student's ledger on his own, except this time the hall was full, and the tone was not curiosity.

"Three fleets," the administrator said, laying the numbers out plainly, without exaggeration, because the true numbers were damning enough without help. "A broken contract. A lost season for men who can't afford a lost season. And a school that wants us to keep writing its word into law as though it were incapable of this."

The student didn't argue the numbers. She'd logged them herself, honestly, days before he ever stood up to read them back to her. "Everything you've said is true," she said. "I called it. It didn't come. People lost real coin and a real season on my word."

"Then why," the administrator pressed, "should this hall continue writing your school's authority into binding regulation, when a single miss cost this much?"

It was the retired successor who answered, not the student, standing from where she'd been sitting quietly at the back of the hall. "Because the alternative isn't a system that never misses. There isn't one. The alternative is a system that hides its misses, and you'd never know the cost until it was a hundred lives instead of a broken contract." She held up the open ledger -- decades of entries, hits and misses both, circled and dated in three different hands going back to Sephtis's own. "Every miss in this book is right here, in the same ink as every hit. Find me another tradition on this coast that keeps that book."

The administrator had no answer for the book itself, because there wasn't a rival record anywhere on the coast that matched it -- not because no one else had ever been wrong, but because no one else had ever written it down where it could be checked. The silence that followed wasn't concession. It was the sound of an argument that had nowhere left to go.

The hall didn't vote to strip anything that day. It voted, instead, to require the same transparent misses-included standard from anyone who wanted regulatory standing -- a bar the school had already been clearing for three generations, and one the administrator's own preferred alternative, a purely instrument-based forecasting office with no ledger tradition at all, couldn't meet.

Kanja, watching from the back of the hall in no capacity anyone had asked him to fill, thought the whole exchange had turned on something smaller and stranger than storms: not whether the school was ever wrong, but whether it could be trusted to say so.
