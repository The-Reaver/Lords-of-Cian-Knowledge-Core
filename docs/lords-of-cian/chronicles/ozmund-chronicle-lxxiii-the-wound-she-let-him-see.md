# The Wound She Let Him See

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1802`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXXIII — fourth entry in the Val Mirel strand's third wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not
present in this entry. Ozmund is roughly sixteen. Reverses the strand's established caregiving
dynamic for the first time — Val Mirel returns genuinely injured and lets her son tend to her, a
register of unguarded maternal vulnerability distinct from Chronicle XVI's deliberate gift-giving.
Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story Ozmund
told him years later. No new named characters.*

---

The General gave me this story last among the ones from his sixteenth year, and told me, handing it
over, that he'd always thought of it as the quietest thing his mother ever gave him — quieter than the
Stone Count, quieter even than the Seventh Cord, because it wasn't a lesson at all. It was simply her,
worn down further than he'd ever seen her, letting him see it.

She arrived without warning, as she always did, but he told me he knew within the first few steps of
watching her cross the yard that something was different this time. She favored her left side, faintly
enough that anyone who hadn't spent seven years learning to read the smallest shifts in her bearing
would never have noticed it at all. Her color was wrong. And she did not go, as she always went first,
to find him in whatever corner of the estate he happened to be — she went instead to her rooms, and it
was he who had to go looking for her, which had never once happened before in all the years he could
remember.

He found her sitting rather than standing, which unsettled him more than the wound itself did once he
finally saw it — a long, half-healed gash along her ribs, deep enough that he understood, looking at
it, that whatever density and durability the Kareth War-Order's centuries had built into her had not
been enough to stop it from costing her something real. She let him look at it without flinching away,
which he told me was itself unlike her. She had never once, in sixteen years, let him see her as
anything less than complete.

He asked her, carefully, whether he should send for a physician. She told him no — that it was already
healing, that Kareth physiology managed most things given time, and that what she actually needed, more
than any physician's hands, was simply to sit still somewhere for a few days and let her body finish
what it had already started. He told me he understood, hearing that, that she had come to him not for
treatment but because there was nowhere else in the whole of the world she trusted enough to be seen
this way.

He did not ask what had wounded her. He'd learned that discipline from her own example years before —
some things a person hands you when they're ready, and asking early only closes the door faster. What
he did instead, he told me, was simply stay. He brought her food she didn't have to ask for a second
time. He sat with her through the worst of the first night, when the wound's healing pulled at her
sleep in a way she was too proud, even wounded, to name aloud, and he held nothing for her the way she
had once held the count for him at seventeen when a dispatch broke her rhythm — quietly, without
comment, simply present until she found her own way back to steady.

She told him, on the second evening, once the worst of it had passed and she could sit upright without
the careful stillness costing her something visible, that she had never once let anyone tend to her
like this — not since before he was born, she said, not in longer than his father's entire bloodline
had existed to count. He asked her why. She told him, and this was the part he gave me twice, unchanged
both times, that a Seventh Wing tactician who let herself be cared for was a tactician who had to trust
that the person doing the caring wouldn't mistake it for weakness, wouldn't use the knowledge of it
against her later, wouldn't ever, even once, look at her differently for having needed it. She told him
she had never, in all her centuries, met anyone she trusted with that particular vulnerability until
she was sitting across from her own son.

He told me he understood, hearing that, something he'd never quite let himself consider before — that
every lesson she'd ever given him, every discipline, every careful distance she'd kept from the rest of
his life, had all been in service of exactly this: becoming, eventually, someone she could actually
lean on rather than only someone she had to protect. He said it changed, quietly and permanently, how
he understood every visit that came after. He had spent sixteen years learning to be his mother's
student. That week, briefly and without either of them naming it aloud, he had also become something
closer to her keeper.

She was gone again within the week, healed enough to travel, and she never spoke of the wound again in
any of the years after. But he told me she came back to him differently from then on — not less
guarded exactly, she was never, in all the time he knew her, anything close to unguarded with the rest
of the world. Only that some small, permanent door had opened between the two of them on that visit
that never again fully closed, and that he understood, decades later, it had cost her more to let him
see her that way than almost anything else she'd ever given him.
