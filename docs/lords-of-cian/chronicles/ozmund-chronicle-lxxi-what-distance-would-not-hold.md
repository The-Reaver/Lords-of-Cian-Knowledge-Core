# What Distance Would Not Hold

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1800`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXXI — second entry in the Val Mirel strand's third wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, present at the close
of this entry. Ozmund is roughly thirteen. Directly tests Val Mirel's established "chosen distance"
parenting philosophy (Chronicle XIV, `MCD-1743`) against a real external threat to Ozmund's life for
the first time in the strand — the one condition under which the distance does not hold. Narrated by
Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years
later. No new named characters.*

---

The General told me this one carefully, choosing his words the way a man chooses footing on ice he
already knows is thin, and I understood, listening to him, that he was giving me something he'd
decided long before that night to keep mostly to himself. I set down here only what he judged safe to
give me. I have never gone looking for the rest of it.

He was thirteen, riding back from a minor errand in the market town with two House Guard escorts, when
the ambush came — not, he was careful to tell me, an especially sophisticated one. Six or seven men,
armed for robbery rather than war, the kind of opportunistic banditry that occasionally tested the
roads within a day's ride of any great House and usually regretted it within the hour. What made this
one different, and what he did not understand until much later, was that they had not come for coin at
all. They had come for him, specifically, on the word of someone House Verehimu never fully identified
afterward, and the two escorts, brave as they were, were not enough men to hold a road against seven
who knew exactly which rider in the party mattered.

He told me the fight itself he remembered only in pieces — a horse going down under him, one escort
falling back to shield him bodily while the other tried to break through for help, the particular
clarity that comes, he said, from being thirteen and understanding for the first time in his life that
the people around him might actually die protecting him and there was nothing he could do about it
except not make their dying for nothing. He held the Stone Count, he told me, without deciding to. It
was the only thing his body knew how to do with fear that large.

Val Mirel was not at the House that week. She was a day's ride south, touring a Seventh Wing muster she
had no reason to leave early for, and by every account House Verehimu's own couriers could later
reconstruct, no rider reached her before she was already moving. He never learned, and told me
plainly he'd stopped trying to learn, exactly how she knew. What he knew was that she arrived on that
road in less time than a message could have traveled it twice over, and that the last two bandits
still standing when she reached them did not survive contact with her by more than a handful of
seconds each.

He told me what unsettled the House afterward was not that she had come. It was how she had come — in
full view of the surviving escort, of the market town's own outriders who arrived moments later, of
anyone who cared to ask what they'd seen on that road that week. Every year of careful, deliberate
distance she'd kept from Verehimu court life, every reception stood through in silence, every visit
timed and spaced to keep the court from ever quite being able to say what she truly was — all of it,
spent in less time than it took the last bandit to fall, because thirteen years of discipline about
what the world was and wasn't permitted to see of her had not survived contact with the actual
possibility of losing him.

He found her afterward kneeling in the road beside him, checking him for wounds with hands that were,
he told me, the only unsteady thing about the whole encounter. She did not speak of what she'd just
done, not that day and not for a long while after. What she told him, once she was satisfied he was
whole, was quieter than anything he'd heard from her before: that everything she'd ever told him on
that terrace about why she stayed away had been true, and would go on being true, every day it didn't
matter this much. But that the distance had never once been a wall between them. It was a door she
kept closed on purpose, and doors that are kept closed on purpose are not the same as doors that
cannot open. He understood, hearing it, that she had just shown him the one condition under which it
always would.

Aethelgard reached them within the hour, having ridden the moment word found him, and the General told
me he never forgot the look that passed between his parents on that road — not relief alone, though
there was plenty of that, but something closer to a shared reckoning, two people who had built an
entire careful architecture around keeping one part of their family's truth contained, watching it
come apart in front of witnesses neither of them could fully control. Neither of them said a word about
it in front of him that day. What it cost them both, privately, in the weeks after, he only came to
understand much later, and gave me only in fragments even then.

He told me, decades on, that he never again doubted the shape of his mother's love, whatever else he
came to doubt about the distance she kept. He had watched, at thirteen, exactly how far that distance
was willing to travel, and how fast, the one time it truly counted.
