# The Challenge He Wouldn't Answer With Steel

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle XXXII, wave 11.

Ruven Calx ran the loading trade on the canal's lower stretch the way a duelist runs a bladeline —
by being the last man standing whenever anyone contested it — and he sent his challenge the same way
he'd sent every challenge in eleven years of holding that territory: a formal, honest demand to meet
at the old grain scales at dawn, winner to hold the lower stretch outright.

"He's not lying about wanting a fair fight," Corren Halst said, reading the message twice. "That's
almost worse. A trick, you can refuse without it costing you anything. An honest challenge, refusing
it looks like fear, whatever the actual reason is."

"I know how it'll look." Kanja was already drafting his answer. "I'm refusing it anyway."

He went to the grain scales at dawn regardless — unarmed, Onyx left behind entirely, which Calx
clearly had not anticipated from the way his expression shifted when Kanja arrived carrying nothing
but the same tally books he carried everywhere else.

"You came to forfeit in person," Calx said. "I'll give you that much credit. Most men send word."

"I came to tell you I won't fight you for the lower stretch, and to tell you why, since you're owed
the reason after an honest challenge." Kanja set the tally books down between them. "If I beat you in
a duel, the lower stretch changes hands because I'm better with a blade than you are. That doesn't
make the wage figures on those sites any more honest than they were yesterday. It just replaces one
man who controls the territory with another. I'm not interested in controlling territory. I'm
interested in the numbers on it being true. Those aren't the same fight, and I won't let you make me
fight the wrong one."

"Then how do you propose to settle it, if not the way territory's always been settled down here?"

"The same way I've settled everything else. Publish both our sites' figures, side by side, cross-
checked by the same independent clerks who verified the five-district standard. Let the workers on
both our stretches decide which arrangement actually serves them better, once they can see the real
numbers instead of guessing which of us runs the harder territory."

Calx studied him for a long moment, visibly recalibrating an opponent he'd expected to either fight or
flee, and found himself facing a third option he had no ready answer for. "That's not a contest I
know how to win the way I know how to win a duel."

"No. It's a different kind of contest. I'd rather win that one, or lose it honestly, than win a duel
that settles nothing about whether either of our territories is actually run fairly."

The figures were published within the week. Calx's held up better than either of them expected — not
a win for Kanja's method exactly, but not a loss either, and the lower stretch settled into an uneasy,
genuinely fairer arrangement neither blade nor chalk had decided.
