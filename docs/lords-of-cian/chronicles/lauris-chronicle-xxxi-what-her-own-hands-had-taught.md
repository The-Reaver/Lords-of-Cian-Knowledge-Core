# Lauris Chronicle XXXI: What Her Own Hands Had Taught

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1648`). Thirty-first entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, continuing "Strand D" through the Specialist sub-period. Full-scene treatment of
Operation 28 (previously only summarized at `MCD-186`): the cluster of seven engineered Karesian
subjects, the last of whom fought using Lauris's own Karth-Sera curriculum (`MCD-169`-`170`) rather
than any cooperative-era or Sister-Hold-era form, closely mirroring her own development around age
2,400. Dramatizes the working conclusion `MCD-186` already locks -- that the apparatus had been
observing her Cian operations closely enough to reverse-engineer Karth-Sera from behavior alone,
rather than possessing an independent copy from Kares Prime -- and the fact that she "has never
resolved, in writing, how to feel about it." Extends directly from Chronicle XXX's dawning realization
that the apparatus studies her specifically. No new named characters; the subject stays unnamed,
matching `MCD-186`'s own phrasing. Zero collisions.*

-----

*Archive fragment, Specialist designation LL-001-S-028: "Six were strangers to me. The seventh
moved like someone who had trained under Veska Karth-Ven, which is to say, moved like someone who
had trained under me. I terminated her as contracted. I have not yet decided what else that
sentence requires me to feel."*

Operation 28's contract described a cluster of seven engineered Karesian subjects, held in a
facility roughly a week's travel from the Korren Highlands, whose combined capability had already
cost the Directorate an entire recovery team before the contract reached her. Six of the seven, her
archive records without particular weight, fought exactly as she had by then come to expect from the
engineering tradition's other subjects: capable, disciplined, drawing on forms she recognized as
cooperative-era Karesian combat doctrine, the kind of training any sufficiently resourced program
with access to old Kares Prime source material might plausibly produce.

The seventh did not fight that way.

I want to be careful in describing what she recorded, because the precision of the account is, I
think, the entire point of it. The seventh subject's opening stance was not a cooperative-era form.
It was Karth-Sera — the curriculum developed under the Karth-Ven Sister-Hold between her own ages of
1,800 and 2,400, the discipline she herself had spent six hundred years absorbing before it had ever
been exported anywhere, let alone to a facility on a world the curriculum's own architects had never
set foot on. The subject's weight transfer between strikes, the particular economy of motion Karth-
Sera trains into a combatant specifically to conserve energy across sustained engagements rather than
spend it in a single opening exchange — Lauris recognized all of it, because she recognized herself
in it, calibrated to a stage of development she placed, in her own written assessment, at roughly
where her own capability had stood around age 2,400.

The engagement itself lasted, by her account, considerably longer than any of the preceding six —
not because the subject matched her present capability, which nothing yet living on Cian could
plausibly claim, but because fighting a mirror of her own training required her to unlearn, in real
time, every instinctive counter Karth-Sera itself had spent centuries teaching her to expect from an
opponent who did not share it. She terminated the subject as contracted. She has told me, in one of
the few conversations we have had that she did not direct back toward operational fact, that the
final exchange felt less like combat and more like an argument conducted with her own younger self,
one she was required by contract to win in the most permanent way available.

Her working conclusion, recorded plainly in her archive and never revised since: the apparatus did
not possess an independent copy of Karth-Sera smuggled somehow from Kares Prime. It had watched her.
Closely enough, across whatever span of operations preceded this one, to reverse-engineer the
curriculum's shape from her own recorded combat behavior alone — which meant that every engagement
she had fought since Operation 1 had been, without her knowledge or consent, a lesson delivered to
an unseen student.

"I do not know," she wrote, "whether to be more disturbed that they built her, or that they built
her out of me."

I have asked her, since, whether this is the operation she considers the true beginning of her
Disillusionment — a question I have learned to ask carefully, because she resists single origins as
a matter of principle. She gave me, this time, an answer closer to agreement than I have heard from
her on the subject before: not the beginning, she said, but the operation after which she stopped
being able to tell herself the apparatus's interest in her was merely tactical. A tactical opponent
studies your methods to defeat them. What Operation 28 proved was closer to something else, a word
she used once, in the privacy of her own archive, and has not used again since: *inheritance.*
Something was being built to carry forward what she carried, whether or not she consented to being
its source.

Her closing note, unresolved by her own explicit admission, closes this account as well: *I have not
decided how to feel about this. I record the absence of a decision as faithfully as I would record
the decision itself, because I suspect the not-deciding is, itself, the truer answer.*
