# The Promise for After He's Gone

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Captain Alias Chronicle XLVIII, wave 16, closing the
wave. Rebellion-era aftermath, later years. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters beyond the already-locked Garren Hask.*

---

Garren Hask was well past the age most of the crew's founding generation had already stepped back
from active duty, and he had taken, in the last year, to spending his evenings not with the ledgers
he'd kept for decades but with a single blank notebook he wouldn't let anyone else read. Kanja found
him with it one night on the aft deck, and for once didn't ask what it was.

"You can ask," Hask said, not looking up. "I know you've been not-asking on purpose."

"I figured you'd tell me when it was finished."

"It's not for you to read. It's for whoever counts the crew after I'm not the one counting them
anymore." He closed it and set it on his knee. "I've been thinking about something I never let
myself think about while I still had the energy to outrun it. You're going to outlive every name in
this book. Not might. Will. And I've spent thirty years building something — the ledgers, the naming
customs, the dispute council, all of it — that only works because people like me are standing behind
it making sure it does. One day there won't be a me. Or a Halst, or a Vos, or half the names in this
notebook. And I want to know what happens to the name after that. Not to you. To the word itself."

Kanja was quiet long enough that Hask looked up.

"I don't have a clean answer," Kanja said finally. "I've thought about it too, longer than you'd
guess. I could tell you the name outlives its people the way anything does — becomes a story, gets
retold, eventually gets it wrong in small ways nobody left alive can correct. That's true of most
things eventually. I don't think it's the whole truth of this one, though."

"Then what is?"

"You didn't build the ledgers and the council and the customs because you needed me around forever
to make them work. You built them so they wouldn't need me around forever. That was the whole point,
wasn't it — Halst training officers who don't need him standing over them, the council settling
disputes without waiting on my word, the naming spreading to new hands who never even met the people
who coined it first. None of that depends on any one of us lasting. It depends on it being worth
carrying. If it's worth carrying, someone always will."

Hask turned that over for a while. "And if you outlive everyone who remembers why it started?"

"Then I'll be the one telling the story instead of living inside it, the way Efa Gol tells it to the
new hands now. That's not nothing either. Someone has to remember it right, even after everyone who
built it is gone. Maybe that ends up being the last job the name ever gives me."

Hask opened the notebook again, uncapped his pen, and wrote something Kanja didn't ask to see.

"I'm not writing your promise down," he said. "I'm writing mine. That whatever's in this book gets
handed to someone who'll keep adding to it after I can't, so there's always a next person holding it
when you need one. That's the part that was always ours to guarantee. Not yours."

"Then I'll hold up my end," Kanja said. "I'll still be here to hand it to them, whoever they turn
out to be, for as long as it takes."

Hask closed the notebook a second time, this time for good, and didn't try to have the last word.
Some things, he'd learned, were better left sitting quietly between two people than said all the way
to their end.
