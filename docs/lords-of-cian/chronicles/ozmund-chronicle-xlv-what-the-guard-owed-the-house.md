Sergeant Oswin Kade had worn the Verehimu colors longer than Draconis had been alive when I found
him, a man well past active duty by then, spending his retirement the way old soldiers of his sort
often do — sitting near a gate he no longer had any duty to watch, watching it anyway. He told me he'd
trained under three House Guard commanders before Draconis ever took the post, and that he'd outlived
two of them, and that he had opinions about what the Guard was for that he suspected the newer men,
Draconis included, had never had reason to think through as carefully as he had, because they'd never
served under a commander who got it wrong.

He didn't want to talk about any one man, he told me early on, not Draconis, not the boy who'd become
the General, not even the commander he'd served under whose failures had shaped his own thinking most.
He said the story worth telling wasn't about a man at all. It was about an arrangement — the plain,
unglamorous obligation running both directions between a House and the men sworn to die defending it
— and he'd spent forty years watching that arrangement get honored, and occasionally watching it get
broken, and he'd come to believe the breaking mattered more than anything any single guardsman ever
did.

He gave me the example of Commander Rell, two postings before Draconis, a man Kade said had understood
the Guard's obligation to the House perfectly and had never once understood the reverse. Rell ran the
tightest drills Kade had seen in his career, demanded absolute discipline, and treated every guardsman
under him as interchangeable — replaceable the moment injury or age made them less useful, with no
accounting made for what they'd given the House in the years before that. Kade said he'd watched men
serve twenty years and leave with nothing but a formal thanks and a walk to the gate, and he said that
kind of arrangement, however disciplined it looked on a drill yard, was rotten at its foundation,
because an oath sworn in one direction only was no oath at all — it was simply obedience, dressed up
to look like something nobler.

What changed, Kade said, happened gradually enough that he couldn't point to a single order that did
it, only to a slow accumulation of small corrections that started arriving from Aethelgard's own
household once the young heir was old enough to have a hand in how things were run — quiet policy, not
proclamation: guardsmen injured in service kept on House pension in full, not reduced; families of men
killed on duty housed and provided for as a standing obligation rather than a discretionary mercy;
retirement treated, for the first time in Kade's memory, as something the House owed a man for decades
given rather than something granted out of generosity it could just as easily have withheld. He said
he'd never had it confirmed directly that the boy's hand was behind those changes, only that they'd
started arriving around the years everyone agreed the young heir had begun genuinely involving himself
in the House's daily governance, and that the timing had never once struck him as coincidence.

He told me what mattered to him most wasn't the specific policies, which he admitted freely he
couldn't recite in full any longer. It was the underlying instinct they came from — a House that
understood the arrangement ran in both directions, that a guardsman's loyalty was worth something
real and owed something real in exchange, not simply extracted because rank and oath made extraction
easy. He said he'd served under commanders who never grasped that at all, and he'd come, by the end of
his career, to measure every House he'd ever heard of by whether it understood the difference.

I asked him, near the end of our conversation, whether he thought the Guard itself had ever fully
understood what it was protecting in those years — whether the ordinary men on the wall knew anything
of what the heir actually was. He said no, plainly, without hesitation, and added that he didn't think
it had mattered to any of them one way or the other. A guardsman's oath, in his experience, was never
really sworn to a bloodline or a title or whatever lay hidden beneath either of those things. It was
sworn to an arrangement — protection given, obligation honored in return — and Kade said the House
he'd served had kept its half of that arrangement more faithfully than any other he'd known of, long
before anyone on the wall had reason to suspect what, exactly, they were being trusted to protect.
