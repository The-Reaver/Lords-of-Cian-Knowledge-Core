# What the Wind Carried Instead of Sound

The high desert crossing carried wind constant enough to make ordinary conversation difficult at ten
paces, and the Aegis-Talisman's lens, built to catch resonance carried faintly through open air,
returned a reading buried so deep in ambient noise that Kanja couldn't trust a single frequency it
offered him.

He'd faced interference before — ice that muffled, felt lining that swallowed — but this was
different again: not a structure resisting the read, but the air itself too loud to carry one
cleanly across any distance at all.

"I can't read this from here," he admitted, lowering the Talisman rather than trust a signal he
couldn't verify. "Not accurately enough to act on."

He crossed the remaining distance to the watchtower's iron reinforcement bracing on foot, wind
tearing at him the whole way, and set his bare palm against the metal directly rather than rely on
the lens at all — touch carrying what air currently couldn't, the resonance traveling through direct
contact with a clarity the wind had no way to interfere with.

It took longer. It required closing distance under a garrison's watch rather than diagnosing from
cover, a genuine tactical cost the wind imposed that no countermeasure ever had. But the reading came
back clean the moment skin met iron, the bracing's full tension signature as legible through touch as
it would have been through the lens on a still day.

"You didn't need the Talisman at all, in the end," the rebel scout beside him observed, once the
bracing had come down and the tower's defensive wall had folded inward without it.

"I needed it less than usual. I still needed the years it took to learn what the reading actually
feels like, with or without an instrument translating it for me. The Talisman makes the doctrine
faster and safer to use from a distance. It was never the only way to listen. Today just reminded me
of that the hard way, with the wind doing what four alloy variants and a countermeasure corps never
quite managed on their own."
