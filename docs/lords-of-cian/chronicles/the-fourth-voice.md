# The Fourth Voice

*Locked canon, Batch 236, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1046`). Crow King Alias Chronicle LX, wave 20. Not a
territory Chronicle. Closes wave 20.*

---

The third generation did not take on a student the way her own teacher had taken on her — in secret,
without asking, presenting the choice already made and daring anyone to object. She came to Kanja
first, which was itself the difference she meant to make.

"I want to teach someone," she said. "I want you to tell me if you think I'm wrong before I start,
not after."

Kanja considered the boy she had in mind — a runner who had carried messages for the crew for two
years without once being asked to originate one, whose only qualification anyone could point to was
that he never repeated a detail wrong, not once, not under any pressure anyone had tested him with yet.
"Your teacher didn't ask me," he said. "I didn't ask mine, when it was my turn to decide who'd carry
this past me. Why are you asking?"

"Because I watched what happened when the last two generations started without asking anyone,"
she said. "Mine held. Hers held too, eventually. But I don't think either of us would say it was the
safest way to begin something this many people are now depending on. I'm not asking permission because
I think I need it. I'm asking because I think the craft is old enough now that someone should be
allowed to say no before the teaching starts, not just live with it after."

Kanja didn't say no. He didn't fully say yes, either — he asked to meet the boy first, and spent an
afternoon with him that had nothing to do with the Hymn-Engine at all, just questions about what he'd
seen and heard on two years of runs nobody had thought to ask him about before. What came back was
exact, unembellished, and entirely unconcerned with sounding impressive. "He listens like he's already
been trained," Kanja told her afterward. "You'd just be the first one to notice."

The teaching itself began the same plain way it always had — not with the marsh trick or the Braid or
anything that had ever made the legend, but with the discipline underneath all of it, the same lesson
the apprentice had once learned from Kanja and passed whole to the third generation in turn: listen
before you decide there's anything worth saying. The boy took to it faster than either of the two
generations before him, not because he was more gifted, the third generation was careful to say, but
because two years of carrying other people's exact words had already taught him most of the hard part
before anyone put a name to what he was learning.

When the apprentice heard, she asked her own student only one question, the same one she'd once been
asked. "Does it feel strange, watching it pass to someone you're not standing over yourself?"

"It felt strange asking first," the third generation admitted. "It doesn't feel strange watching him
learn. I think that's supposed to be the part that's supposed to feel strange, and it isn't, and I
don't entirely know what that means yet."

"It means you did the part that was yours to do," the apprentice said. "The rest was always going to
take care of itself. It did for me. It will for him."

Four generations now carried some working piece of what had started, decades earlier, as one man
learning to buy a few minutes of confusion from a handful of scarecrows in a marsh no one had thought
worth defending.
