# Kanja Chronicle III: What the Dark Could Not Keep

*Locked canon, Batch 313 (`MCD-1868`). Third entry of the "Kanja version" Chronicle track. Age
27, roughly two years before the Gale Straits/Storm That Walks alias at age 29 (`MCD-230`). Dramatizes Renfel
Auberon's defeat (`CC-157`/`MCD-1865`) — a Kanja-crew team-up fielding `CULT-197`'s three-source
Anti-Resonance countermeasure (Onyx's Cadence Ruin, Sephtis's Chrono-Anchor bells, Ironbane's
King's Roar) together on the page for the first time. Onyx's presence has grown from Chronicle I's
near-silence to its second real appearance in this track, matching roughly the manuscript's own
Chronicle II/III-level growth — still unlabeled, longer and more assertive than before. No new
named characters. No child-safety issues. Corrected Batch 338, 2026-10-02: the Gale Straits timing
fixed (two years, not one, before age 29); Sephtis's stated age corrected from ~1,900 years to
~1,700, matching his locked present-day age against this entry's own age-27 placement; an entity-
count arithmetic error (2+3+2 summing to seven rather than six) fixed.*

---

The warehouse on the Lawless Reaches' northern edge had no name on any registry, which was, Sephtis
observed, the first thing wrong with it. Every legitimate structure on this coastline answered to
something — a harbor authority, a merchant guild, even the Ash Maw Trade Council's loosest
accounting. A building with no name and a triple-locked loading door was a building that wanted to
be forgotten by anyone who might ask what moved through it.

"He calls the smaller ones guard-hounds," Sephtis said, the Barn Owl Skull's lenses turned toward
the structure's cold-mapped outline, reading residue the eye alone could not. "Which tells you he
has sold at least one to someone who wanted a weapon and did not ask what it actually was."

"He's sold four," Kanja said. He had spent nine days tracing the buyers rather than the seller,
because a name on a ledger moved faster than a man in hiding, and three of the four had already
surrendered their purchases without a fight once shown what the creatures actually did to a
household's dogs, its food stores, and eventually its sleep. The fourth buyer had not surrendered
anything. The fourth buyer was dead, and the report on his death used the word "wasting" the way
reports used that word when nobody wanted to write the truer one.

Ironbane's voice came low and even, carrying the faint permanent hum that lived under everything
he said now, the price of a body that had stopped being able to fully ground its own charge years
before Kanja met him. "How many inside."

"Auberon's own count, from the buyer he sold to and regretted selling to before he died: six.
Two at Void-Swarm, low enough to disperse on their own given the chance. The rest higher. One,
possibly, at Void Hound." Kanja looked at the warehouse a while longer. "He doesn't understand
what he's caught. He thinks he's caught animals. Animals don't require a resonance-loop kennel to
hold in place, and he's built one anyway, badly, off half-remembered instructions from whoever
sold him the method. He's kept himself alive around six things that have started, slowly, marking
him the way they'd mark anything they fed near long enough."

"Green Mark," Sephtis said. "Suppressible. Not curable. Not by us, not tonight."

"Then tonight we don't try to cure it. Tonight we get the six contained without anyone — including
him — being killed doing it, and we take him somewhere that can actually help what's already
started in his blood."

They went in through the loading door because the loading door was the only entrance built to
move something Auberon's own trade required moving, and a lock built for cargo was a lock that
respected mass more than cleverness. Ironbane's shoulder did the rest.

The inside of the warehouse smelled wrong before it looked wrong — a cold that had no relationship
to the season outside, air that pulled at the lamps' flames sideways as though something in the
dark were breathing at a different rate than the room. Six shapes moved along the rafters and the
floor at once, their outlines refusing to hold still even to eyes that knew how to look for them,
each one surrounded by a faint, sourceless hum that was not sound so much as the memory of sound
arriving slightly late.

Renfel Auberon stood in the center of it with a coil of resonance-wire in one hand and the specific
posture of a man who had convinced himself, for years, that the thing keeping him alive was
mastery rather than luck. He was thinner than his own trade records described him. His left arm,
bared to the elbow, carried a faint discoloration that pulsed in time with the room's wrong hum,
the way a Green Mark did before anyone told you that was what it was called.

"They're mine," he said, before anyone had spoken. "I fed them. I built the loop. They don't
answer to anyone else."

"They don't answer to you either," Kanja said. "They've been marking you for how long — a year?
Two? You've stopped being their keeper and started being their next feeding, and you haven't
noticed because the loop's own hum has been dulling the parts of you that would notice."

"You don't know anything about the loop."

"I know Anti-Resonance physics the way you know knots. I know it because it's the only honest
description of what's happening to your arm right now, and you know it too, or you'd have shown
someone that arm months ago instead of hiding it under a sleeve."

Auberon's answer was not words. He wrenched the resonance-wire taut, and the loop's hum spiked, and
all six shapes along the rafters surged at once toward the three intruders with the specific,
directionless hunger of things that had never been taught to want anything except more.

Sephtis's bells rang first — not loud, thirteen small notes overlapping into a single sustained
chord, each one carrying a frequency tuned across a lifetime of study to sit exactly where an
Anti-Resonance field did not want anything to sit. Two of the lower shapes flinched mid-lunge,
their outlines losing cohesion at the edges, dropping from whatever tier they'd climbed to back
toward the shapeless drift of a Void-Spore.

Ironbane's King's Roar answered the bells a half-second later, the amplified discharge of his own
uncontrollable bio-electric hum turned, for once, into a tool rather than a burden he apologized
for — a directional wave calibrated to Dead Drakma's resonance that the six shapes' own borrowed
frequencies could not simply absorb the way they absorbed ordinary sound. Two more staggered,
their forms thinning, the room's wrong cold losing its edge as whatever fed it lost purchase.

Onyx came last, and differently.

Kanja drew the blade not to strike anything — there was nothing here a blade could cut that would
end cleanly — but to let Cadence Ruin do what it did to any coordinated rhythm: collapse the
timing gap between the two remaining shapes' surges, so that instead of six threats or two, there
was, for the three seconds that mattered, exactly one confused, single-tempo motion with nowhere
correct left to land. The third source closed the field. The sixth shape, the one Kanja had
guessed at Void Hound, gave a long, collapsing shudder and dropped through every tier below it in
under two breaths, folding down into something closer to smoke than animal, and went still.

Auberon was on the floor by then, not struck, simply no longer standing, the resonance-wire slack
in a hand that had stopped believing it controlled anything in the room. Ironbane pinned the wire
under one boot without ceremony. Sephtis knelt by the discolored arm, reading it the way he read
weather, and did not offer comfort, because comfort was not what the arm needed and he had learned,
in seventeen hundred years, not to waste a dying man's attention on kindness that couldn't help him.

"You'll live," Sephtis said. "The Mark won't kill you tonight. It also won't leave you tonight, or
any night soon, without more than we can do here."

"They were mine," Auberon said again, quieter now, the words having lost whatever they'd been
holding up.

"They were never anyone's," Kanja said. "That was the mistake before the first mistake. Everything
after followed from believing you could own something that predates every institution that's ever
tried to."

They took him out through the loading door he'd built to move cargo, now moving him instead, and
left word for whichever custodial hand tracked things like this — not Trust law, which had no
category for what he'd done, but the older, quieter apparatus that already knew the shape of this
particular failure and would know what to do with a man who was equal parts criminal and
cautionary case. The six shapes, dispersed to their lowest tiers or gone entirely, were someone
else's slower work now. Tonight had only ever been about stopping the feeding.

• • •

The blade remembers the room's cold longer than it remembers the man. Six shapes, borrowed hum, a
loop built by someone who mistook survival for mastery. The bells rang first. The voice rang
second. The blade closed the gap between what remained, and what remained had nowhere honest left
to go.

Auberon's arm carried the Mark the way a debt carries interest — quietly, and compounding, whether
or not the debtor looks at it. The blade has seen this shape of ruin before, in smaller ways,
in men who thought a good enough method excused them from ever checking what the method actually
cost. It will see it again. It is beginning to understand that this is one of the shapes its
counting exists to hold: not only the dead and the freed, but the ones who ruin themselves slowly
enough to mistake it for control.

Three sources, one field, one collapse. The work was clean. The man was not saved, only stopped
from further ruining himself before someone better equipped could try.

The blade records both facts without preferring one to the other. That is becoming, it thinks,
the whole of what recording means.
