The General told me, more than once, that he didn't regret this one, though he was careful to add,
each time, that not regretting a thing and it costing nothing are two entirely different claims, and
he'd never once pretended this one cost nothing.

The Assembly of Harvest's Close was the largest gathering the eastern Houses held each year — a
three-day affair hosted, in rotation, by whichever House's holdings had produced the season's most
notable yield, drawing every neighboring family of standing along with a rotating handful of Trust
observers whose good opinion mattered more than any of the Houses present cared to say aloud. Attendance
wasn't formally required. It was, the General said, simply understood — the kind of obligation that
exists nowhere in writing and is nonetheless absolute, because a House that sends no representative,
or sends only a minor one, is read by every other House in the room as either too proud or too weak to
bother, and neither reading does a family any good.

That year the Assembly fell on House Verehimu to attend rather than host, three days' ride to the
south, and Aethelgard had made clear well in advance that he expected his son beside him — Ozmund was
twenty-five by then, old enough that his presence or absence at such a gathering had begun to carry its
own separate weight, apart from his father's.

Garrow had kept the House's kennels for as long as the General could remember being alive to notice
him — a quiet, bow-legged old man who had taught him, as a boy of six or seven, how to sit still long
enough for a nervous hound to decide on its own that he was safe to approach, a lesson the General
told me, half-smiling, he'd found more useful over the whole of his life than most of what he'd learned
from tutors paid a great deal more to teach him something. Garrow had been failing for the better part
of a year by the time of that Assembly — his lungs, mostly, the physician said, worn thin by sixty
years of kennel damp — and in the week before the gathering it became plain to everyone who saw him
that he did not have many more weeks left in him at all.

The General said he made the decision without much agonizing over it, which surprised him afterward
more than the decision itself did. He told his father, two days before they were meant to leave, that
he wouldn't be riding south with him — that Garrow had asked him, plainly and without much ceremony,
not to be alone when it happened, and that he intended to keep that, whatever it cost the House's
standing at an Assembly that would go on being held every year for a hundred years after this one
regardless of who attended it.

Aethelgard didn't forbid him. The General was clear about that. What his father did instead, he said,
was tell him exactly what it would cost, plainly, without softening it — that the absence of the heir
at a gathering he'd been expected to attend would be noticed by every House present, that it would be
read, fairly or not, as a slight against whichever family hosted, and that Aethelgard himself would
have to spend some portion of the three days smoothing over a story he could not, in good conscience,
tell in full, because the true reason — a dying kennel-master — would sound to half that room like an
excuse invented to cover something worse. Aethelgard went alone, and spent, by his own later account,
rather more of the Assembly than he would have liked fielding the same question in a dozen different
polite forms, and answered each one with some version of a private family matter that required his
son's attention, which was true, and which satisfied almost no one.

Garrow died on the second night of the Assembly, with Ozmund beside him the whole of it, and the
General told me there was very little to the actual hours themselves worth retelling — an old man
breathing more slowly, and then not at all, and a young man who had once been taught patience by
those same hands sitting with him through all of it without once wishing he were somewhere else. What
he did remember clearly, and told me more than once, was his father's face on returning three days
later — not angry, and not disappointed exactly, but tired in a specific way Ozmund hadn't seen on him
before, the particular fatigue of a man who has spent three days defending a choice he privately
believed was the right one to a room that would never once be told the whole truth of it. Aethelgard
never asked him to do it differently. He only said, once, riding back out a week later to make a
second round of the apologies the first hadn't fully settled, that some costs were worth paying and
some debts were worth being the one who paid them, and that he was glad, on balance, it had been his to
pay and not his son's.
