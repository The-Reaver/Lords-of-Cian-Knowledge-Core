# The Question He Kept for Himself

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1837`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CVIII, third entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave (part A), set strictly
pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive and the succession entirely uncontested
throughout. A moment of genuine, ordinary uncertainty about his own future role — not doubt about his
fitness to inherit, but a private, recurring daydream of a life built around something other than
inheritance at all, entertained and then set down again, never acted on and never resolved either way.
Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. No new named characters.*

---

I want to be careful with this one, because it would be easy, telling it now, to make it sound like
more than the General ever told me it was — to dress an ordinary boy's idle wondering in the costume of
a decision that was already forming, years ahead of itself, toward something neither of us could have
known was coming. He warned me against that himself, when he told it to me, more than once in the
telling. He said it was not a plan. It was not even, most days, a real wish. It was only a door his
mind wandered toward sometimes, the way anyone's mind wanders toward doors it has no intention of
actually opening, and he wanted it told that way or not told at all.

He was perhaps sixteen, by his own reckoning, in the particular stretch of a young man's life when the
shape of everything ahead of him has become clear enough to see and not yet close enough to feel real —
old enough to understand, in the abstract, every duty that would eventually be his, the House, the
lands, the name, the whole long inheritance stretching backward to Drakmund and forward, presumably,
past himself; and young enough still that none of it had yet closed its hand around him the way it
would once he was a man grown with a seat at his father's table instead of a place beside it. He told me
it was in exactly that gap — old enough to see the shape, too young to be caught inside it yet — that
the question first arrived, uninvited, and kept arriving afterward whenever his mind had nowhere urgent
to be sent.

What if, he wondered — and he was careful, telling me, to say it exactly that plainly, without
dressing it up — what if there were a version of his life that belonged to nothing but the work in
front of his own two hands, and not to a name. He told me he had thought, more than once, of Joren's
row at the grain markets, of a trade a man could actually finish in a day and hold up afterward and
call *done* — a wheel, a hinge, a length of good rope — instead of the slow, unfinishable work of being
responsible for people he had not chosen and would never stop being responsible for. He told me he had
imagined, in the particular detail a daydream gets only when it is being returned to rather than
invented fresh each time, what it might be to learn a single honest craft well enough to be worth
something for it alone, and to walk into a room as nothing more than a man who was good with his hands,
the way Joren had once known him before either of them understood what he actually was.

He told me it never became anything more than that. He did not run any inventory of it, did not test
it against what leaving would cost his father or the House or the people already depending on the name
he carried; he simply let it visit, the way you let a familiar weather pass through a room, and then
went back to whatever lesson or duty had been waiting for him before it arrived. He was honest with me
about why, in the end, he never let it grow into anything larger than a passing wish. It was not, he
said, that duty had crushed the thought before it could take proper shape, and it was not that he
lacked the courage to want something plainly for himself. It was that, turning the question over enough
times to be sure of his own answer, he found he did not actually want the other life half so much as he
wanted, simply, to know that some version of himself somewhere might have chosen it freely — that the
inheritance waiting for him would be worth more, carried, if he could tell himself honestly that it was
a thing he had at least once considered setting down, and had picked back up anyway, rather than a
weight he had never once been permitted to imagine putting aside at all.

He told me that question came back to visit him, in one quiet form or another, for years afterward,
never with any more urgency than it had the first time, never resolved and never entirely gone either.
He did not know, telling it to me, what became of it in the end, or whether a man ever truly finishes
answering a question like that for himself. Neither, in honesty, do I. I have only what he gave me: a
boy, some quiet evening long before I knew him, turning a door over in his mind that he never opened,
and setting it down again each time no lighter than he'd found it, but no heavier either.
