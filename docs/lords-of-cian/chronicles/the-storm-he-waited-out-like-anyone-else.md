# The Storm He Waited Out Like Anyone Else

*Locked canon, Batch 200, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-826`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle XLI, wave 14 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The storm closed the strait for four days, stranding his own ship alongside a dozen ordinary merchant
vessels in the same sheltered cove, and for the first time in longer than he could easily place, there
was nothing extraordinary about him to bring to bear against it — no gear built for this, no
Trinity relic that mattered against weather, just a man waiting out bad water the same as every other
sailor sharing the anchorage.

A merchant crew's cook, short-handed and unbothered by the stranger's quiet presence, put him to work
peeling roots for a shared stew rather than let an idle pair of hands sit useless through the
boredom, and he accepted without complaint or comment, working the blade with the same unhurried
attention he gave anything worth doing properly.

"You're better at that than most passengers," the cook observed, mildly impressed.

"Had a lot of practice, one way or another." He didn't elaborate, and the cook didn't press, and the
four days settled into a rhythm that had nothing to do with reputation or rescue — card games played
for coppers he mostly lost, a sailor's off-key song that got worse the longer the storm dragged on,
an argument over the fairest way to split a diminishing store of dried fish that he mediated not
through any authority but by simply being the only person in the circle both sides trusted to divide
it evenly.

"You'd make a decent quartermaster," one of the card players told him on the third night, only half
joking. "Fair hands. Good at math. Wasted talent, whatever you actually do for a living."

"I've been told that before, by someone who'd know." He thought, briefly and without comment, of
Garren Hask's own ledgers, three centuries of exactly this same fairness applied to something far
larger than dried fish. "It's not a bad way to spend a life, keeping the count honest for people who
need to trust it."

The storm broke on the fifth morning, and the anchorage scattered back to its separate business
without ceremony, the merchant crew never learning who they'd shared four idle days with, and he
carried nothing away from it but the plain, restful fact of having been, briefly and completely,
nobody in particular — no legend, no gear, no debt owed or collected, just a man who peeled roots
competently and split dried fish fairly until the weather let everyone go back to being whoever they
actually were.
