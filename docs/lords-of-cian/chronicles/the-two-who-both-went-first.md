# The Two Who Both Went First

*Locked canon, Batch 232, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1032`). The Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LVIII, wave 20. The worst-off-first discipline forces a real, on-the-spot triage between two equally desperate claimants, and the one documented second never fully forgives being ranked behind the other. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The district had two candidates for worst-off, and both of them knew it before Kanja did.

One was a loom-tender whose hand had been crushed eight months earlier and never properly set, working one-handed for two-thirds pay because the overseer called it charity to keep her on at all. The other was a boy of maybe fourteen carrying his family's entire wage debt alone, his father dead in a rockfall the district's own ledger listed as an unrelated absence. Both stood at the front of the yard when the tally table went up. Both had been waiting, by separate accounts, since before dawn.

Ezio looked between them and then at Kanja, the question unspoken because it didn't need to be. The method didn't have a rule for this. Worst-off-first had always meant finding the person the district itself had pushed furthest to the back — it had never meant choosing between two people who'd both already been pushed there, together, by nothing but the order they happened to be standing in.

Kanja called the boy first. He gave his reasons out loud, to both of them, rather than making the choice silently and hoping it went unnoticed: a debt inherited without consent weighed differently than a wage cut absorbed by someone who'd at least been asked to accept it, however unfairly. It was the only distinction he could defend, and he said so plainly — not that it was the right answer, only that it was the one he could stand behind if asked to explain it later.

The loom-tender accepted it without protest. She sat through the boy's testimony, gave her own an hour after his, and her case went into the ledger with the same rigor as anyone's. The settlement, when it came weeks later, restored her pay in full and back-dated it six months.

She never once, in all the time the crew remained in the district, treated Kanja as anything but correct to have done what he did. She also never quite let go of having stood second. It showed in small things — she answered follow-up questions to Ezio rather than to him, thanked the loom-inspector who verified her injury by name in a way she never thanked the man who'd ordered her case documented at all, and when the district later put up a plaque naming the boy as the first testimony taken that season, she was the one who suggested the wording.

"I'm not angry," she told Ezio once, when he asked her directly, months after the fact. "I want to be clear about that. I'd have made the same call, probably, if it'd been mine to make. I just don't think being right stops it from being a thing that happened to me. Somebody had to go second. It happened to be me."

Ezio carried that back to Kanja, and neither of them tried to resolve it further. The ledger recorded both cases as fully and fairly settled. Nothing in it recorded what it had cost the second one to be second, because there was no column built to hold it, and neither of them pretended there should be.
