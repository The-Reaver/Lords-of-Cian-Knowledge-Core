# The Man He Was Becoming

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1779`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle L — the closing entry of the coming-of-age strand (Chronicles XLVI–L) and of this
30-Chronicle wave, bringing his series to 50 Chronicles total. Set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony
(`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive and present throughout, with no foreshadowing of the Ceremony or anything
after it. A quiet, reflective, synthesizing entry — Ozmund, essentially grown, taking stock of the
inheritance from both parents (`MCD-1736`-`1745`), Draconis's loyalty (`MCD-1730`-`1735`), the wider
household, and his own private discipline (`MCD-1775`-`1778`) — without resolving or advancing any

---

I want to close this the way he closed it for me, which is to say without an ending, because nothing
he ever told me about those years came with one attached, and I have come to think that was the point
of telling it to me at all. A man doesn't hand you thirty small stories across twenty years of
friendship because he wants them arranged into a single neat account of how he became who he is. He
hands them to you because he trusts you to understand that a person doesn't become anything in a
single motion — that he is always, at every age I ever knew him, still in the middle of becoming it,
and that the stories are the only honest record of a process that never actually finished.

So: an evening, no crisis in it, nothing that would earn a place in any history of the House. He was,
by his own account, close enough to grown that the household had stopped treating him as a boy who
needed watching and had not yet started treating him as a lord who needed obeying — that strange, brief
country between the two, where a young man belongs to no clear category and is, for exactly that
reason, most fully himself. He walked the outer wall that evening the way his father had once walked
him past it as a boy, on the theory that a lord who never left his own walls forgot what they were for,
except this time he walked it alone, at dusk, for no reason he could name to me beyond the simple fact
that it was where his thinking seemed to go when it had nowhere urgent to be sent.

He told me he thought, that evening, about Draconis — who by then had commanded the House Guard's inner
ring for some years, who still did not know what he was guarding and never would from Ozmund's own
telling of it, and whose loyalty had become, without either of them ever naming it as such, the one
relationship in his life built on a foundation he had chosen never to correct. He did not regret the
silence, he told me. He had turned it over often enough by then to trust his own answer to it. What he
felt, walking that wall, was something closer to gratitude — that a man could be given proof, once,
early, that his own worth to someone else had nothing to do with what his blood could do, and that
keeping that proof intact, uncorrected, unclarified, had turned out to be one of the few genuinely
selfless things he'd managed in those years, even if no one but himself would ever know to call it
that.

He thought about his father and his mother, he said, and about the particular strangeness of being
raised by two people whose ideas of what mattered most in a life had almost nothing in common and had
somehow, in him, stopped arguing with each other. Aethelgard's inheritance was patience — the coin held
on its edge, the willingness to be embarrassed in front of the people you'd failed rather than defend
yourself at their expense, the conviction that a lord's authority was a debt owed outward and not a
gift owed to himself. Val Mirel's inheritance was discipline of an older, harder kind — the Stone
Count's willingness to sit inside an unanswered question without flinching from it, the Hollow Stand's
insistence that grief be carried through rather than folded away and forgotten, the whole cold clarity
of a woman who had commanded a Seventh Wing for longer than House Verehimu itself had existed. He told
me he used to think, as a much younger boy, that he would eventually have to choose between them — that
a man could be his father's son or his mother's, but that trying to be both at once would eventually
split him down some seam neither parent could see coming. What he understood now, he said, walking a
wall neither of them had built and both of them had, in their own ways, taught him to value, was that
the seam had never opened. He had simply become a third thing, made out of both without belonging
entirely to either — patient like his father when patience served the people in front of him, and
disciplined like his mother when nothing but discipline would hold; and that the joining of the two,
rather than the choosing between them, might be the truest inheritance either of them had actually
given him.

He thought, he said, about the household itself, which he had come to understand, somewhere in those
years, was not the same thing as the House — that Osric's caution and the hamlet at Greyfen and every
servant whose name he'd bothered to learn were not the backdrop to his life but a real part of what he
was responsible for, in a way no ceremony had ever formally granted him and none, he suspected, ever
fully would. And he thought, last, about the quarry below Aldenmoor, and the nights no one but himself
had ever witnessed, and understood — he told me this plainly, without embarrassment, as though it had
taken him a long time to be able to say it without embarrassment — that the private discipline and the
public one were not separate projects. Every ounce of restraint he had ever shown a friend he was
careful not to hurt, every order he had given a steward without needing to raise his voice, every year
he had spent learning to stop asking a question that had no answer and start asking one that did, had
been built, quietly, out of the same practiced stillness he had first found alone, on black ice, in the
dark, with no one to prove it to.

He did not, that evening, know what was coming. I want to be honest about that, because it would be
easy, telling this now, to let the shape of everything that came after leak backward into a night that
never contained it — to make the wall he walked, the peace he described, into something heavier than it
was, a last calm before a storm he could not have seen. He couldn't. None of them could. His father was
at that hour somewhere inside the walls behind him, alive, unremarkable, ordinary in every way that
mattered that evening, and the future the General described to me, remembering it, was not a countdown
to anything. It was simply a young man, on a wall, taking honest stock of who he had become out of
everything I have told you across this whole account — the boy who let a soldier believe a lie for the
soldier's own sake, the boy who let a friendship go rather than risk what he couldn't be certain of, the
boy who stopped demanding an answer the world was never going to give him and started building
something out of the silence instead, the boy who practiced alone in the dark because there was no one
else he could practice with — and finding, in all of it together, not a finished man, but the honest,
unfinished shape of one still becoming himself, on ordinary terms, on an ordinary evening, with nothing
yet asked of him that the years behind him hadn't already, quietly, prepared him to give.

That is where he left it, the night he told it to me, and it is where I will leave it too.
