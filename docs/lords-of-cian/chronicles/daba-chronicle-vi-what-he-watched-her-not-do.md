# Daba Chronicle VI: What He Watched Her Not Do

He had watched Sael for eleven days before he spoke to her.

She did not know she was being watched, which was itself part of what he was watching for. She moved
through the ruined blocks near the old tenement rows with the particular carefulness of someone who
had learned, recently and completely, that the ground could not be trusted to stay where it was left.
She counted things. Daba noticed that first -- how her eyes went to doorframes and rooflines and the
gaps between them, cataloguing sightlines the way he himself had once catalogued them, in the weeks
after, when counting had been the only thing that kept his hands from shaking.

She had lost two younger cousins in the fire. He knew this the way he knew most things now, by asking
quietly and remembering exactly, never twice. What he did not know, what no one could tell him, was
whether grief had made her reckless or careful. Both were common. Only one was useful to what he was
trying to build, and he had not yet decided what that was, only that it needed to survive being built.

So he set the test the only way he trusted -- not by asking her anything, but by letting her find
something and watching what she did with it.

He let her see the Trust patrol schedule. Not directly -- he arranged for a courier's satchel to pass
through the alley behind the grain stores at an hour he knew she would be there, dropped by a boy paid
to look careless and be gone before anyone could catch him at it. Inside were three real routes and
two real names, the kind of information that, in the hands of someone burning to answer 1804's death
toll with a body count of her own, could have gotten four people killed inside a week.

She read it. He watched her read it, crouched behind a collapsed cistern wall with the patience of a
man who had nowhere else he needed to be. He watched her hands go still over the page the way hands go
still over something that matters too much to hold carelessly. And then he watched her fold it back
along its own creases, tuck it inside her coat, and walk directly away from the two named patrol
officers rather than toward them -- away from the temptation, not toward the opportunity.

She did not act. That was the whole of the test, and she passed it by doing nothing at all.

He found her three streets later, sitting on a broken cart axle with her knees drawn up, the satchel
still inside her coat, her face doing the complicated work of deciding whether to be afraid of him.

"You could have used that," he said. It was not a question.

"I could have gotten someone killed," she said. "I don't know these streets like the men who wrote
that schedule know these streets. I know what I don't know. That's not the same as knowing what to do
with it."

He sat down across from her, not close, and did not reach for the satchel. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody taught me that," Sael said, and something in her voice cracked open just slightly, an old wound
finding its familiar shape. "I watched people who thought knowing was the same as being ready. I
watched what happened to them."

He did not ask her to say more. He had learned, in the months since, that grief told on its own
schedule, and that pulling at it before it was ready only closed it tighter. Instead he told her, in
plain words and without ornament, what he was building -- not an army, not yet a name, only a
principle: that the dead did not need avenging so much as the living needed protecting, and that
protecting the living meant never handing an enemy a target large enough to be worth their trouble.

"Small," he said. "Small enough that losing one of us doesn't cost the rest of us the thing we were
trying to do."

Sael was quiet for a long moment, turning the satchel over once in her coat pocket without taking it
out. "And if I'd used it? If I'd gone after those two names?"

"Then I'd have known you weren't ready," he said, "and I'd have let you walk away thinking you'd
stolen something useful, and I'd have started this with someone else."

She looked at him then, really looked, the way people looked at each other only after deciding a thing
had already been settled between them without either saying so aloud. "You still might," she said.
"Start with someone else."

"I might," Daba agreed. "But not today."

He held out his hand for the satchel, and she gave it to him without hesitation -- the second test,
unspoken, already passed before either of them had named the first.
