# The First Time He Led

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1775`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XLVI — opens the closing, internal coming-of-age strand of this wave (Chronicles
XLVI–L), set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard Verehimu alive and away from
the estate at the time of the events shown. Dramatizes a flood emergency along the already-locked
Verehimu Wetlands near Greyfen (`MCD-1738`) in which young Ozmund, present without formal rank or
title, takes real operational responsibility for the first time — a leadership instinct distinct from

I have heard the General called a great many things by people who believed they were the first to
notice something true about him — steady, unhurried, kind in a way that never announced itself as
kindness. None of them ever seemed to wonder where a man learns to be steady before the world has
given him any reason to practice it. I asked him once, carelessly, the way you ask a serious question
at the tail end of a long night, and what he gave me back was not a battle and not a betrayal. It was
a flood at Greyfen that no history of House Verehimu has ever thought worth recording, because nothing
was lost there that anyone with a ledger would have thought to count. He counted it. He told me he
counted it for the rest of his life, as the first time anyone had ever needed him to decide something,
rather than merely survive it.

He was young enough that his presence in the Wetlands that week was an accident of scheduling and not
of duty. Aethelgard had gone upriver to settle a dispute among the grain markets — the same circuit,
more or less, that had nearly gotten his son killed on a different road entirely, though neither of
them spoke of that when they spoke of this — and had left Ozmund behind at Greyfen to finish a
governance visit his father had begun months earlier, walking a levee, hearing a warden's grievances,
learning by doing the small, unglamorous work of being trusted with people rather than merely owed
their obedience. He had Osric with him. Osric had run the household's granaries and stores for longer
than Ozmund had been alive, and he had the particular caution of a man who has watched more than one
young lord mistake urgency for wisdom, and had made it a private policy never to help one do it twice.

The storm that broke the upstream dyke came in the deep of the night, the kind of rain that doesn't
announce itself as dangerous until the sound of the river has already changed underneath it. By the
time anyone at Greyfen understood what was happening, the water was moving toward the low cottages and
the granary both, and the standing protocol — the correct protocol, the one that had never failed
before and would not have failed this time either, given world enough — was to send a rider back to
the House seat for instruction and wait. A rider would take the better part of a day there and back.
The water would not wait a day.

Osric did what a man of his caution does when the correct procedure and the available time have
stopped agreeing with each other: he hesitated, and looked, without quite meaning to, at the boy who
had no rank to give the order he was about to ask for anyway.

What the General told me was that he understood the shape of the problem before he understood that he
was the one who was going to have to solve it — that the grain had to move first, uphill, to the raised
store on the ridge, because a hamlet that survived the night and lost its winter stores would only be
surviving a longer, crueler version of the same disaster; and that the causeway to the ridge would
itself be underwater within two or three hours, which meant the choice was not *grain or people*, it
was *both, now, in an order that could not be argued about once it was moving*. He said it to Osric
plainly, without raising his voice, the way you'd note the hour. Osric — who had every reason in the
world not to take instruction from a boy with no command to his name — looked at him for the length of
one breath and then began shouting orders in his own voice, as though the idea had been his all along.
The General told me he understood, even then, that this was the greater gift: not that Osric obeyed
him, but that Osric spent his own authority doing it, so that no one in that yard that night ever had
to choose between trusting a child and trusting a steward. They were, for the length of the flood, the
same instruction.

He worked the grain line himself. That was the part he seemed to think mattered most in the telling,
more than the plan, more than being right about the causeway — that he did not stand at the edge of
the yard directing traffic with a dry coat while other men's backs went to the sacks. He carried what
everyone else was carrying, ate last, when anyone thought to eat at all, and did not once, in the six
hours it took to move both the grain and the last of the hamlet's children across the causeway ahead of
the water, so much as gesture toward what his name might have compelled people to do faster. He told
me he understood, standing wet to the chest on a ridge he had never expected to be responsible for,
that this was not his father's kind of authority, which persuaded, and it was not his mother's kind,
which was owed. It was something quieter that he had apparently always half-known how to do and had
simply never before been asked to: people will follow a man carrying the same weight they are, without
being told to, faster than they will follow any command shouted down at them from somewhere dry.

Aethelgard returned two days later to find it already finished — the levee shored well enough to hold
through the next storm, the granary intact on its ridge, the hamlet's families accounted for down to
the last child — and heard the whole of it first from Osric, in the steward's own careful, unembroidered
report, because Ozmund had said almost nothing about it to anyone. There was no speech that evening,
the General told me — no column assembled to hear a title given or a debt of loyalty declared, the way
there had been for another man on another road. Aethelgard simply found his son afterward, alone by
the granary stores, and said only that he understood now what had happened without needing to be told
twice, and that he was glad the boy had not waited for permission to be right. It was, the General
said, the first time his father had ever thanked him for something that had nothing to do with being
his father's son at all.
