# The Rebuild That Broke Someone

*Locked canon, Batch 202, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-864`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle XIX, wave 7 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The support beam gave without warning on the ninth hour of a rebuild pushed to match the campaign's
own fastest recorded pace, a joint cured too quickly under pressure Kanja himself had set, and a
young worker fell eleven feet onto scaffolding that hadn't finished going up beneath him. He lived. He
would not use his left arm the same way again.

Kanja was at his side before the dust settled, and stayed there through the worst of it, and did not,
afterward, tell anyone the fall had been unavoidable.

"You set the pace." Kanja said it aloud to no one in particular, standing over the joint that had
failed, as much an accounting to himself as to anyone listening. "You knew what the reheat needed and
you rushed it anyway, because the number mattered more in that moment than the metal did."

"It was a bad joint, not a bad campaign," the site foreman said carefully, watching him. "Could've
happened on any rebuild, fast or slow."

"It happened on this one, at this pace, because I let 'fastest recorded' become a thing worth chasing
for its own sake." He crouched by the cured joint, running a thumb along the crack that had started
it. "I taught a girl to true a hinge once. Told her rushing the reheat doesn't save time, it just moves
the cost somewhere you're not watching. I forgot my own lesson the first time the number got good
enough to be proud of."

The rebuild finished four days behind where it would have, at the old pace — the crew rechecking every
joint twice, curing every beam the full time it actually needed rather than the time a record wanted
it to take. Kanja visited the injured worker every day the campaign remained on-site, and paid for a
prosthetic fitting from the crew's own stores before anyone asked him to.

"You didn't have to slow the whole campaign down over one bad joint," the worker told him, on the last
visit, his splinted arm resting in his lap.

"I didn't slow it down over one joint. I slowed it down because one joint proved I'd stopped checking
for the thing that actually matters." Kanja meant it plainly, without performance. "Faster's only
worth anything if it's still standing afterward. I let myself forget that for exactly long enough to
cost you your arm. That's not a mistake I'm interested in making twice."
