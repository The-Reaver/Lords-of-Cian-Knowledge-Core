# What the Company Town Owned

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1167`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXXXIII, wave 28. The method's most totalizing case yet: a full company-town structure where one employer controls wages, housing, and the only store, a truck-system debt-peonage case distinct from any single-domain fraud shown before.*

---

The mining settlement had no free market in it at all, which took Ezio the better part of the first day simply to understand as a structure before he could begin documenting it as a wrongdoing. Every worker was paid in company scrip, redeemable only at the company store, which sold goods at prices the company itself set with no competition to check them. Housing was company housing, deducted at the company's own rate. There was no independent grocer, no independent landlord, no outside currency circulating at all — a closed loop where every coin a worker earned flowed straight back to the same employer before it could ever leave his hands.

"It's not one theft," Ezio said, three separate ledgers spread in front of him — wage rates, store pricing, rent schedules — each individually defensible if looked at alone. "It's three things that only add up to theft once you see they're all owned by the same man."

He built the case the only way that could actually capture it: not as a wage shortfall, not as a rent overcharge, but as a single combined cost-of-living audit, pricing what the settlement's own goods and housing would fairly cost against comparable free-market prices two days' ride away, then comparing that honest figure against what the company's own scrip wages could actually purchase inside the closed loop. The gap was substantial — workers were, in real purchasing terms, earning barely half of what their nominal wage suggested, the difference silently recaptured through store markups and rent no worker had any alternative to paying.

The company owner argued, at length, that every individual price was his to set as he saw fit — no law compelled him to sell goods at any particular margin, no law compelled him to rent housing at any particular rate, and no law compelled him to pay wages in anything but company scrip if his workers agreed to the arrangement when hired. Ezio didn't dispute any individual point. What he'd built instead was the combined figure no single law had ever been written to catch, because no single transaction in the loop was individually illegal — only the totality of the loop itself, closed so completely that "agreeing to the arrangement when hired" had never meant anything workers could meaningfully refuse.

Kanja's finding didn't ask a magistrate to strike down company scrip or company housing outright — a ruling neither of them had standing to force. What it did was price the loop honestly and demand the gap be closed: wages raised to match real purchasing power against the free-market comparison, store markups capped, rent indexed the same way the ironworks case had set weeks earlier. The owner still owned the town. He no longer owned the ability to quietly starve the difference between what his workers earned and what they could actually buy with it.

"We can't break a closed loop from outside it," Kanja told Ezio, riding out. "We can make sure it stops closing all the way."
