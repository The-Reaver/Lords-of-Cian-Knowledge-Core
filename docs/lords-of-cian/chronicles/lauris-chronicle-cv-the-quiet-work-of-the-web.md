# Lauris Chronicle CV: The Quiet Work of the Web

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1722`). One hundred fifth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of the second 50-Chronicle wave (Chronicles LX-CIX), the tenth entry of the

distinct from `MCD-1666`'s game of stones -- an evening spent alongside the administrative,

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Anansi sorted four hundred reports
tonight, most of them nothing. I sorted beside him. I did not ask which four he kept and which three
hundred and ninety-six he set aside. I do not think he would have told me, and I did not need to
know. I only wanted to sit with the sorting."*

I have written already of an evening spent watching Lauris lose two games of stones to a man who did
not want company and permitted it anyway. I want to set down a second account of him here, in a
register considerably less restful than the first, because I think a reader deserves to see what
Anansi actually does with the hours he does not spend resting -- and because I believe Lauris chose,
this time, to sit with him in the work itself rather than offer him an escape from it.

The Ghost-Lattice network does not run itself. I have written elsewhere, in accounts closer to the
war, of its origin in seized Trust communications equipment and its slow growth into the crew's
largest single intelligence asset -- thirty-one locations raided at once, thirty-four thousand pages
saved from forty thousand in a single desperate night. What I had never set down before this evening
is the unglamorous truth beneath all of that: that a network built to catch the four reports that
matter requires someone to read, first, the several hundred that do not, and that Anansi has done this
himself, personally, every night, for longer than I believe anyone else in this crew fully appreciates.

Lauris found him at it in a room with too little light for the work and did not offer to fix the
light before she sat down, which I understood, watching her, as its own small courtesy -- she had
read, correctly, that he would not want the room brightened around him without being asked.

"How many tonight?" she asked.

"Four hundred and eleven." He did not look up from the report in front of him. "Most of it noise.
Rumor dressed as intelligence, grievance dressed as a threat report, a man's private complaint about
his neighbor filed by a courier who thought it might matter to someone."

"How many will matter?"

"I will not know until I have read all four hundred and eleven." He set one report aside -- the
smallest gesture, unremarked -- and picked up the next. "That is the whole of the discipline. You do
not get to skip the ones that look like nothing. The ones that look like nothing are precisely the
ones worth reading twice."

She did not offer to help sort, and I want to note that I believe this was deliberate rather than an
oversight -- she does not, by her own account to me on other occasions, offer competence where she has
none to offer, and she has none in the specific, trained pattern-reading his own work requires. What
she offered instead was simpler and, I have come to think, considerably rarer in his life: she stayed.
She read beside him, her own borrowed lamp angled to share his light rather than compete with it, and
for the better part of three hours the room held nothing but the small, steady sound of paper being
set down, one report at a time, in one of two piles.

"You do not have to stay for this," he said, some way into the second hour, in the low register I
have learned means he is closer to speaking plainly than his usual public bearing permits.

"I know. I am staying because I want to, not because the work requires it. I have found, across
enough of these evenings, that I do not always need to be useful to a thing to want to be present for
it."

He considered this without pausing the work itself, which I have come to understand is, for him, its
own form of full attention rather than divided attention. "Most people find this tedious to watch."

"I do not find watching discipline tedious. I have spent six thousand years watching people do harder
things worse than you are doing this. I find this restful, in its own way -- watching someone do a
slow, unglamorous thing correctly, with no one requiring them to perform the doing of it for anyone's
benefit but the work's own sake."

He set aside a fourth report -- the last of the evening's actual finds, as it turned out, though I did
not know that at the time -- and looked at the considerably larger pile of the discarded three hundred
and ninety-six for a long moment before he spoke again. "This is most of what I carry that no one
sees. Not the anger. Everyone sees the anger; it is, I am told, rather difficult to miss. This. The
part where I read four hundred reports of nothing to find four that matter, alone, every night, and
tell no one the shape of the labor because the labor itself is not the part anyone wants from me."

"I see it now," Lauris said. "I would not have known to ask for it. I am glad you let me sit for it
instead."

He did not answer that directly, and I do not believe he needed to. I want to record, closing this
account, that I did not watch his rage ease that evening the way I watched it ease, briefly, over a
game of stones. I do not think it was meant to ease. I watched something else instead -- a man who
spends his carried anger lighting other people's resolve finding, for three unglamorous hours, that
the lighting itself did not have to be done in a room with no one else awake in it. Four hundred and
seven reports set aside as nothing. Four kept. I do not know what the four were, and I did not ask,
and I do not believe this account is worse for the not-knowing.
