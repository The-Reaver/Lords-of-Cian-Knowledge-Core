*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Four of them in one room tonight, by
accident rather than design. I do not think it will happen again soon. I intend to remember this one
carefully, in case it does not."*

I do not know that I will have occasion to write another account quite like this one, and I want to
say plainly, before I begin it, why that is. Ozmund, Valen, Anansi, and Orlok do not often occupy the
same room. Their lives run at different speeds and toward different obligations, and I do not
believe any single one of them would have arranged this evening on purpose, had the arranging been
left to any of them alone. It happened instead the way most good things in this account seem to
happen around Lauris specifically — without planning, and without anyone quite noticing it was
happening until it already was.

Ozmund arrived first, restless in the way I have already recorded him being restless, and found her
already seated with a pot of something warm going untouched at her elbow, precisely as she had been
the evening I wrote of the rampart. Valen arrived some while after, drawn, I suspect, by nothing
more deliberate than the knowledge that she was in and the evening was otherwise unclaimed. Anansi
came later still, later than either of them, and later than I think he had originally intended,
judging by the faint reluctance with which he took the seat she had left open for him without
comment. Orlok's presence was the true accident of the evening — a visit that had been arranged for
an entirely separate, unremarkable purpose, extended by an hour when he found the room already full
rather than empty.

I do not believe any of the four came expecting to find the others there. I watched each of them, in
turn, register the room's actual occupancy with a flicker of something I can only call recalculation
— Ozmund reassessing his own restlessness in company he had not planned for, Valen taking the
measure of a room considerably less quiet than he usually prefers, Anansi weighing, visibly, whether
to stay or withdraw, Orlok settling with the unhurried patience of a man who has had seventy-six
thousand years to grow comfortable with rooms rearranging themselves around him. None of them left.
I think that is the entire substance of what I have to report, and I think it is enough.

They did not speak much as a group, which will not surprise a reader of the accounts I have already
set down of each of them separately. Ozmund and Orlok exchanged perhaps a dozen sentences on a
matter I did not consider mine to transcribe in detail, low and unhurried. Valen said very little to
anyone, and seemed, by every measure I could take of him, entirely at ease with saying it. Anansi
spoke least of all, though I noted, watching him across the evening, that he had chosen the seat
nearest Lauris rather than the one nearest the door, which I have come to read, in him, as a kind of
statement in itself.

What I want to record, more than any particular exchange, is the shape of the whole evening as I
watched it from my own place at its edge. I have written, elsewhere in this account, of the burdens
this crew's leads each carry — Kanja's grief, Ozmund's doubt, Ezio's deception, Anansi's rage,
Valeria's perception, Sephtis's time. I watched, this evening, three of those burdens sit in one
room together, unresolved and untouched by anything Lauris said or did to address them directly, and
I watched all three grow, by degrees I could not have measured in advance but could plainly see by
the evening's end, quieter for the sharing of the room. She did not counsel any of them. She did not
try to lighten what each one carried, or offer a word suited to the particular shape of it. She
simply sat among them, unqualified and competent in her own pleasure at their company, and let
whatever she carries instead of a burden do the only work it has ever needed to do around people she
trusts: nothing deliberate at all, and everything, by the evening's quiet end, regardless.

Orlok left first, as he always does, without ceremony. Anansi left soon after, quieter leaving than
he had been arriving. Valen and Ozmund stayed longest, neither speaking, until even they rose and
went their own ways, leaving Lauris alone with a pot long since gone cold and, I suspect, entirely
untroubled by that fact.

"Four of them," I said to her, once the room had emptied, unable to help myself. "I do not believe I
have seen that before."

"Nor have I," she said. "I do not think it will happen again for some while. I am glad it happened
once." She considered the cold pot for a moment, then set it aside without drinking from it, the
small gesture of a woman closing an account she had never intended to keep score of in the first
place. "I carry joy. I have said this to you before, and I imagine I will say it again before either
of us is finished with this record. I do not think joy is meant to fix anything the others carry. I
think it is only meant to be in the room with it, so that what they carry does not have to be
carried entirely alone. Tonight, for an hour, it did not have to be. I would call that a good use of
an evening, whatever else it was or was not."

I have not improved on that sentence since, and I do not intend to try. I include it here, at the
close of this run of accounts, because I believe it is the truest thing anyone in this chronicle —
myself included — has yet said about what she actually is to the people around her, and because I
do not think a colder record than her own words would serve the evening half so well.
