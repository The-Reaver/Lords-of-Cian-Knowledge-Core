# Daba Chronicle VIII: The Number They Chose

They had gone six weeks without a name, and it had never once seemed to matter, until the night Farro
asked what they should call themselves if someone needed to be told where to find them.

Farro had joined a month after Corvin -- a quiet man who had run supply routes before the fire and kept
running them after, mostly for the same people, mostly along the same roads, because the roads had not
changed even though everything alongside them had. He was the newest of the five core members and the
one least given to speeches, which was why the question landed with more weight than it might have
coming from anyone else. He was not asking out of restlessness. He was asking because he had, that
afternoon, needed to describe them to a frightened woman deciding whether to trust them with a wagon
of grain, and had found he had no word for what they were.

"I told her we were friends," he said. "She didn't believe me. Nobody believes friends carry what we
carry."

They were in the cellar again, the same five corners, though by now the habit had stopped feeling like
a rule and started feeling like simply how a room was used. Nessa sat closest to the stair, as she
always did -- she had come to them the same week as Sael, another of 1804's own survivors, one of the
handful of young caregivers who had lived through that day only to spend the following weeks setting
bones and packing wounds for everyone else who had. She rarely spoke first in these conversations. She
listened the way a person listens who has already decided that most talk is a rehearsal for the thing
that actually needs doing.

"We need something," Corvin agreed. "Not a banner. Just a word. Something that tells the right people
what we are without telling the wrong people anything useful."

Several names were offered and set down again almost as quickly. Something drawn from the old tenement
row's own name -- rejected, because it pointed a finger straight at where they'd all come from, and
Daba would not build a thing that could be traced to its own grief that easily. Something invented
whole, a word with no history behind it at all -- rejected by Sael, who said flatly that a name with no
weight in it wouldn't hold anyone to anything once the work got hard.

It was Nessa, finally, who said the thing none of the rest of them had been willing to say first.

"There's already a number," she said. "Nobody's given it a use yet. It just sits where it is, in the
Trust's own report, calling itself a fire of undetermined origin. I think about that number more than
I think about almost anything else I own. I think the rest of you do too. So why are we hunting for a
word to carry, when we're already carrying one, and setting it down every time we open our mouths to
avoid saying it?"

The cellar went quiet in the particular way it went quiet when someone had said the true thing under an
hour of circling.

Daba sat with it a long moment before he answered, because it was not a small thing to agree to, and he
did not want his agreement to look easy. "It isn't a comfortable word," he said. "Every time it's said
aloud, it says what happened. It doesn't let anyone forget, including us."

"That's the point," Nessa said. "We're not trying to be comfortable. We're trying to be remembered
correctly, by the people who need to remember us correctly, and forgettable to everyone else. The
number does both at once. Anyone who knows what it means will never need it explained. Anyone who
doesn't will hear it and think it's nothing -- a date, a tally, some clerk's error in an old ledger.
Let them think that. Let it hide in plain sight the same way we do."

Sael was nodding slowly before Nessa had finished. Corvin looked, for a moment, like he wanted to argue
for something grander, something that announced itself -- and then, visibly, set that instinct down,
the same instinct he had set down a month earlier about the size of the thing they were building.
Farro simply said, "It answers my question. Now I have something to tell her."

Daba looked around the cellar at the four of them, at the shape the room made now with five people in
five corners instead of one man alone counting doorframes and rooflines because counting was the only
thing that kept his hands still.

"Then that's what we are," he said. "Not a name we chose to be feared. A number we choose to carry so
it's never carried by anyone alone again."

Nobody raised a cup to it. Nobody made a speech. Sael reached over and wrote it, small, on the cellar
wall in a stub of chalk -- four digits, nothing else -- and that was the whole of the ceremony, and it
was enough.
