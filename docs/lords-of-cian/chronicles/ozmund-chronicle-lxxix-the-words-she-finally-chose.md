# The Words She Finally Chose

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1808`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXXIX — closing entry of the Val Mirel strand's third wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not
present in this entry. Ozmund is roughly twenty-eight, shortly after Chronicle LXXVIII. Closes the
wave on Val Mirel's first fully spoken (rather than gestural or disciplinary) statement of pride in
who her son has become, deliberately contrasting with Chronicle XXXV's wordless promise. Narrated by
Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years

---

This is not the last story the General ever told me about his mother — that honor, as I've already set
down, belongs to the one about the hand against his chest, the wordless promise she made him after the
year she didn't come. But it is, I've come to believe, the last one worth setting beside it, because
between the two of them they say something about her that neither could say fully alone. That one was
her answer to a question he had never dared ask aloud. This one, he told me, was hers to give without
being asked at all.

She came not long after the landslide, sooner than her usual visits allowed, and he told me he
understood, watching her cross the yard, that she had come specifically because of his letter — not
summoned by any House business, not timed to any Seventh Wing posting he ever learned of, simply come,
the way a person comes when something has moved in them that they haven't yet found the shape to say.

They held the Stone Count together that first morning, as they always did, and he told me nothing in it
felt different from a hundred mornings before it — the same rhythm, the same stillness, the same
comfortable silence neither of them had ever needed words to fill. It was only afterward, walking back
toward the house together in the gray early light, that she stopped him with a hand on his arm and told
him, plainly, that she wanted to say something to him she had never said before, and that she wanted
him to understand she had chosen every word of it deliberately, the way she chose everything.

He told me she said this: that she had spent twenty-eight years teaching him disciplines she had built
across centuries of loss, hoping, without ever quite letting herself believe it certain, that some of
them might actually take root in him the way they had taken root in her. That she had watched him hold
the Stone Count as a boy and wondered whether it would ever be more to him than a game his mother
played at dawn. That she had given him the Narrow Door not knowing whether a House-bred boy would ever
be asked to use it, or whether he'd have the will to use it rightly if he was. That she had handed him
the Seventh Cord uncertain whether he would understand, holding it, what it actually asked of the
person who eventually wore it.

She told him that his letter about the landslide had answered every one of those uncertainties at once,
and more completely than she had ever quite dared hope any single letter could. Not because the
decision itself had been difficult for her to imagine him making correctly — she told him, plainly, that
she had trusted his judgment on that count for years before he'd needed to prove it to her in writing.
What the letter had told her, she said, was something else: that everything she had ever given him had
become, somewhere in the years she hadn't been there to watch it happen, genuinely his — not a
performance of what he thought she wanted, not a son trying to please a mother he saw too rarely to
disappoint safely, but a man who reached for the discipline because it was simply who he had become, in
a moment when no one was there to see whether he used it or not.

"I am proud of you," she told him — and he gave me these words exactly, unchanged in every one of the
few times he ever repeated this story to me, because he said he had turned them over so often in his
own memory that he could no longer imagine them any other way. "Not for what you can do. I have never
once doubted what you could do. I am proud of who you have chosen, again and again, when there was no
one left to choose it for you but yourself."

He told me he had no answer ready for that — twenty-eight years of careful stillness, and the one thing
neither the Stone Count nor the Hollow Stand had ever quite prepared him for was simply hearing it said
aloud, plainly, without a lesson wrapped around it. What he did instead, he told me, was the only thing
that felt true in the moment: he told her, in return, that everything he had ever built himself into had
her held somewhere inside it, whether she was standing beside him to see it or not, and that he
understood now, finally, that this had always been the actual shape of what she'd been trying to give
him — not disciplines to survive her absence, but a version of herself he could carry so completely that
her absence stopped mattering nearly as much as it once had.

She did not answer that with words. She never fully did, in all the years after — some habits, he told
me, a lifetime doesn't entirely unlearn. But she placed her palm against his chest the way she always
did, and held it there a moment longer than usual, and he understood, standing in that yard with his
mother's hand over his own steady pulse, that between the words she had finally chosen to speak and the
gesture she had never stopped making, she had, between the two of them, told him absolutely everything
there was left to tell.
