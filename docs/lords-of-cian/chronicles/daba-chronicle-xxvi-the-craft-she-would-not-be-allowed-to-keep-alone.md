Rhyne Cadec was, by Sael's own account, the best set of eyes 1804 had trained since Sael herself, and she said so to Daba without a trace of the reluctance most instructors carried when a student outgrew the lesson. That was exactly the trouble. Rhyne knew it too, and had built, in eighteen months of watching roads that never once caught her watching them, a competence sharp enough to feel like an identity rather than a skill. When Daba told her she'd spend the coming season learning to set a charge under Corvin's eye, she looked at him the way a person looks at an instruction that seems to have been given to someone else by mistake.

"I'm not slow with tools," she said. "I'm good at this. You're pulling me off the one thing I'm actually good at."

"You're good at one thing," Daba said. "That's the problem, not the achievement."

He had built the argument years ago, in the cellar, long before he had the language to make it land on anyone but himself, and by now he could lay it out the way a man lays out a familiar tool. A cell with one set of eyes and one set of hands for demolitions was not two capabilities. It was one capability wearing two faces, and the day either face went missing -- caught, hurt, simply exhausted past use -- the whole cell lost a function it could not replace from inside itself. He had watched that exact failure happen to other resistances, factions with real numbers and real courage who nonetheless collapsed a season's worth of work because the one man who knew how to read a garrison's rotation took an arrow at the wrong moment and no one behind him had ever been made to learn it.

"I'm not asking you to be worse at watching roads," he told her. "I'm asking you to be dangerous in a second way nobody's counting on, so that the day someone finally does catch you watching, the thing you carry doesn't die with you."

Corvin took her on with the same flat patience he gave everyone, which Rhyne found, in the first weeks, more insulting than outright doubt would have been. He corrected her grip on the crimping tool eleven times in one afternoon and never once raised his voice about it, and she came away from those sessions with the particular exhaustion of someone who had spent a full day being competent at nothing. She missed the roads. She said so, more than once, to anyone who'd listen, and Sael -- who had gone through the same season herself, years before, learning to carry a stretcher under Nessa's patient correction -- told her only that the missing was the whole point.

"If it doesn't cost you something to learn it," Sael said, "you haven't actually learned a second thing. You've just gotten worse at the first one for a while and called it training."

It was Corvin, in the end, who gave her the moment that made the lesson stick, and he did it by accident, the way the truest lessons in 1804 tended to arrive. A charge meant to open a supply wall went wrong under his own hands -- a fuse cut a half-second short, nothing dangerous, nothing that cost more than a delay and a second attempt -- and in the silence after, waiting for the second fuse to catch, Rhyne found herself doing without thinking what eighteen months of watching roads had built into her without her permission: counting the guards' rotation past the wall's far corner, timing it against the delay, already knowing exactly how much room the mistake had bought them before it became a problem instead of an inconvenience. She said the numbers aloud before she realized she'd done it. Corvin looked at her for a long moment, and did not correct her grip that day.

"That's the whole reason," he told her afterward. "Not so you can do my job. So that the day my hands are the ones that fail, yours already know how to keep the two of us alive while they do."

She still preferred the roads. She never stopped saying so. But the season passed, and by its end she could set a charge clean on the first attempt, and Daba, watching from a distance the way he watched most things that mattered to him, thought that this -- not any single operation, not any single victory -- was the actual shape of what he was building: not people who could do one thing well, but a network where every pair of hands had learned, at real cost to the thing they loved doing most, to be a second set of hands for someone else's craft as well.
