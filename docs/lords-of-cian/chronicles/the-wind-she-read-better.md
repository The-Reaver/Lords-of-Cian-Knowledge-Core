# The Wind She Read Better

*Locked canon, Batch 199, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-778`). Sovereign Ghost of the Great Sea Alias Chronicle XXIII, wave 8 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The Trust courier ship was faster than *The Audit* by any honest measure of her lines — lighter build,
newer hull, a rig cut for speed rather than the endurance Dol Maren had spent two years optimizing
into her own vessel — and her captain knew it, running for the open sea the moment recognition flags
confirmed which ship had just crested the horizon behind him.

"We can't out-build her," Dol Maren said, watching the gap hold steady rather than close. "Not on
speed alone. She'll out-run us in a straight line every time."

"Then we don't sail a straight line." Kanja had already turned to her rather than to the weapons
below deck — no anchor-chain device would reach this distance, no boarding crew could cross open
water this wide. "What does she have that we don't?"

"Speed. What we have that she doesn't is you willing to let me actually sail this thing instead of
fight with it."

What followed was two hours of pure seamanship, no gear deployed, no powers invoked — Dol Maren
reading the wind shifts a full quarter-hour before they arrived, cutting angles that cost distance in
the moment but gained it back threefold once the shift she'd predicted actually came through, tacking
in patterns the courier's own less experienced helmsman couldn't anticipate and therefore couldn't
counter.

"She's faster," Dol Maren said, eyes never leaving the sails, hands constantly correcting by fractions
most sailors would never notice needed correcting. "But fast in a straight line only matters if the
wind cooperates with a straight line. I've spent two years learning what this exact hull does in
water like this. She hasn't had the ship long enough to know what hers does."

The gap closed by degrees too small to notice minute to minute and too large to deny by the two-hour
mark, until the courier's captain — reading his own instruments and understanding, finally, that raw
speed had lost to something he couldn't out-build his way past — struck his colors rather than force
a confrontation his ship's actual advantage had somehow failed to prevent.

"How," he asked, boarding party arriving without a single weapon drawn, "does a slower ship catch a
faster one?"

"The same way a slower man wins a fight he shouldn't." Dol Maren finally looked up from the sails, and
for the first time all afternoon, allowed herself something like satisfaction. "He learns the ground
better than the faster man ever bothered to."
