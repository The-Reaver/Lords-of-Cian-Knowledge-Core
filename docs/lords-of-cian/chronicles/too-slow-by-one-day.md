# Too Slow By One Day

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1164`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXXX, wave 27. A genuine failure of the method's own pace: a worker the crew was documenting a case for dies of exposure one day before the finding closes, prompting a new standing practice for emergency interim relief.*

---

The case itself wasn't complicated. A dockyard laborer, already ill with a lung sickness that had worsened across a hard winter, was owed three months of wages an administrator had been stalling on with the same tactics Ezio had learned to cut through in days rather than weeks. It should have been, by the method's own established pace, an unremarkable finding — testimony taken, production logs checked, the administrator's stalling exposed for what it was, a settlement within four days at most.

It took five. Not through any failure Ezio could point to directly — the administrator's records were unusually scattered across three different offices, a genuine complication rather than deliberate obstruction, and Ezio had insisted, as the method always insisted, on cross-referencing every figure properly before posting a finding he couldn't fully stand behind. The fifth day was, by any measure the crew had used on a hundred prior cases, reasonable diligence and nothing more.

The laborer died on the night between the fourth day and the fifth, in a company barracks with no heat the administrator had also been withholding, his wages still one unfinished cross-reference away from being confirmed. The finding closed the next morning exactly as strong as it would have been regardless — the shortfall real, the administrator's stalling exposed, the back wages paid in full to his surviving mother. None of it reached him alive.

"Four days was the standard," Ezio said, standing over a settlement he'd built as carefully and as honestly as any he'd ever built. "I followed the standard."

"You followed it right. That's the part that's hard to sit with." Kanja didn't tell him the death was anyone's fault in the ledger's own accounting — the diligence had been real, the delay explainable, nothing rushed or careless in how the case had been handled. What he made plain was that the method's own discipline, built to be unhurried enough to be trustworthy, had a cost it had never openly named: patience that served the truth could still, in a case urgent enough, arrive a day later than a life could wait for.

What came out of it, worked through with Ezio over the following week, was the crew's first standing emergency-relief practice: any testifier presenting a documented medical urgency — illness, injury, exposure — received an immediate, provisional partial payment from the crew's own operating funds the same day testimony was taken, advanced against whatever the final settlement produced, corrected afterward if the fuller figure came in lower. It wasn't a fix for every case the method might still arrive too late to help. It was, Ezio said, writing the new practice into the crew's own standing procedures for the first time, the only honest answer available to a method that could be right, careful, and still too slow.
