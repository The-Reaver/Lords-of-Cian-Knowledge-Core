# Anirak Chronicle II: The Second Harness

Tideglass at night. A free port. The Scourge's mark painted small on the harbor-master's door, and the
Trust's tax-house shuttered three streets back for longer than most of the dockhands had been alive.

Rain on slate. Tar smell off the boat sheds. Kelp rotting on the slip. Lamps in the tavern windows
along the front, and the water black between the piers.

She lay awake in the loft over the net-maker's shop. She could not have said what woke her.

Then she could.

A hull in the harbor. Out past the second pier, low in the water, no lamp. She felt it the way she
felt weather. A weight sitting in the swell where no weight had been at dusk. Oars, muffled, dipping
slow. Then stopping.

She was down the ladder with the chains in her hands before the oars stopped.

• • •

The woman was called Ardith. Twenty, or near it. She had come into Tideglass nine days before on a
salt barge, under a tarp, with a number cut into the skin behind her ear and no other thing she would
talk about. The harbor council had put her in the rooms over the chandler's. The crew had told the
council the rooms were watched.

They were. Edda had the door. Hamund had the lane. Odile had the roof across the way.

Anirak came round the corner of the chandler's at a run and saw it all in one look.

Twelve of them. Grey oilcloth over dull plate. No marks. Six already at the chandler's door, two of
those on the stair, and the door open. Edda down on one knee on the step, a hand to her head, blood in
her hair. Six more in the lane mouth facing Hamund, close-packed, in no hurry.

Two of the six in the lane carried a long bundle between them. Canvas over something that clinked.

Odile dropped off the roof across the way into the lane behind them. Hamund stepped up beside her.

On the stair, a voice. Ardith's. One word, and then a hand over it.

Twelve in a narrow lane. Rain on the stones. One door, one stair, a boat out in the dark.

The six on the stair: taking her now.

The six in the lane: waiting. For something. For her.

The stair first. The waiting ones are the trap.

She went for the stair.

• • •

The six in the lane let her through.

Too easy. She saw it as she passed them, and stopping would cost her. She went past them toward the door with every face in the lane turned on her, and none of the
six moved a hand.

The first man on the step had a short club in one hand and a coil of leather strap in the other. A Fang
went out on its chain and took the club arm behind the wrist. She pulled. The arm came across his body.
The Star came off her back and its talons took the club. Gone. He sat down on the step.

The weight of it came back up her arms. The chains began to warm.

The second man on the step. A strap swung at her head. She ducked under it and fed the turn into the
second Fang. The hook took his belt and she threw him off the step into the gutter.

The stair. Four more on it, and the top of it, and the door to the rooms.

Behind her, the canvas came off the bundle.

• • •

She heard it before she felt it. Iron weights clinking on cords. A whirring. Then the thing came down
over her shoulders from behind.

A net. Heavy. Thick bands of quilted leather stitched in a lattice, every band packed tight with lead
shot. It dropped over her head and shoulders and arms and the chains, and the six from the lane were
already round her on the stair-foot, each with a cord in his fists, and they leaned back on the cords
all at once.

The net closed.

She drove an elbow back. It sank into a band. The shot inside shifted and swallowed the blow. Nothing
came back up her arm. Nothing.

She drove the Star's haft into the lattice. Same. The blow went into the lead and died there. A dead
blow. No ring. No weight returned.

She tried to turn. The cords held. Six men leaning their whole weight on six cords from six sides. She
could move a hand's breadth any way. No more.

The heat began to go out of the chains.

She felt it go. Down her arms. Out of her shoulders. Out of her chest. A cracked jar draining in the
rain. Every blow she had banked since the step leaking away, and nothing she could do with her body
would put any of it back.

A hood came down over her head. Coarse cloth. Wet. It cut off the lamps.

She stood in the dark, held still, in a harness made for her.

They harnessed me. So the blows wouldn't stack.

• • •

Outside the hood, a calm voice. Close, from behind the cords.

"Hold tension. She will try the hands first, then the hips. Do not answer either."

Another voice, rougher. "She's quiet."

"She is draining. Look at the chain."

She knew that voice's kind. No heat in it. Every word chosen. A man reading a gauge.

"Stair team?"

"Subject secured. Moving to the water."

"Then we hold her four minutes and go."

On the stair above her, boots, and something dragged. A muffled sound through a hand. Going down the
other side. Going toward the boat.

The calm voice spoke again, nearer. To her, this time.

"Anirak." He said it the way a clerk reads a name off a docket. "Do not exert yourself. Exertion inside
the rig only drains you faster. No one here is authorized to injure you. In four minutes we will be
gone."

She stood still. She had to. Every breath of stillness bled her more.

Four minutes was long enough for a boat to clear the harbor mouth.

• • •

Her hands were caught. Her hips were caught. Her eyes were covered.

Her throat was not.

They had built the rig against the blows. Against the eyes. A Directorate gauge-man reads what a file
tells him to read, and her file said Kinetic-Stack.

She let her voice go.

Low. Under hearing. Into the gorget at her throat, and the crystal nodes took it up even through the wet
cloth of the hood, and laid it out ahead of her.

Forward. Where she faced. Where two of the six stood on the cords.

She felt it land in them, down the cords. The tension on the forward lines wavered. One
cord went slack in a jerk, then tight again, then slack. A man went to his knees on wet stone. She heard
him retch.

Behind her, the calm voice. "Forward lines. Hold. Hold."

The forward line went slack again.

A hand's breadth became an arm's length.

She turned into the slack. Hard. All her weight. The net came round with her, the lead bands swinging
out on their stitching. The cords on the far side snapped taut with her turn, and the men on them came
off their feet.

Movement. Only that. Her own weight, swung.

It came back up her arms.

Small. A single coin of heat in the chains. Then she turned the other way, and the cords on that side
came taut, and the men holding them came stumbling in, and that was two. Three.

The net ate every blow. So she pulled. The men on the
cords were holding her by the cords. The cords held them to her. Every turn she made dragged one of them
across the stones and every drag came back up her arms.

The chains were warm again under the bands.

Somewhere outside the hood, Hamund's voice. "Left cord!"

A blow. A man's grunt. The left cord went dead. Then Odile's. "Right!" The right one too.

Her three, on the cord-men. They did not reach for the net. They knew better. They held the line around
her and took the cords off one by one, so she never had to stop.

She ripped one arm up through the slack lattice. Got a hand to the hood. Tore it off.

Lamps. Rain. The lane.

Every face in the lane on her.

The calm voice was a thin man in grey oilcloth standing three paces back from it all, out of reach of
any cord. He had a slate in one hand and a stylus in the other. He was not holding a weapon. His face
had turned to her with all the rest. He looked at her as if she were the only thing in the world. He
looked down at his slate. Then back.

She went through the last of the lattice. The lead bands fell round her feet on the stones.

The men who had held the cords were down, or backing off, hands open. Not one of them came at her.

The thin man did not run. He did not come at her either. He wrote something.

• • •

She did not stop for him.

The lane. The slip. The end of the second pier, at a dead run, the chains warm and singing.

Out past the harbor mouth, a hull under sail.

She felt it before she saw it. A long keel, bigger than the rowboat. A cutter running before the land
breeze, heeled, every sail drawing. The rowboat bumping empty at its stern on a towline.

At the end of the pier she paced, three strides each way. Rain on the
planks. The weight in her arms and nowhere to put it.

The sea told her the rest. The cutter's hull went out and out across the swell. Faster than anything
that swims. Farther every breath.

She could have gone into the water. She would have caught nothing.

The keel went over the edge of what she could feel. Then there was only the swell.

• • •

When she came back up the lane, the detachment was gone. Over the seawall and down to a second boat she
never heard. Twelve men. Every one of them on his feet or carried. No one dead.

The net lay in the lane where it had fallen. Edda was sitting on the chandler's step with a rag to her
head. Odile was holding the rag. Hamund stood over the net.

He kicked it. It clinked.

"They made that for you."

"Yes."

"How long, to make a thing like that?"

She looked down at it. The bands. The stitching. Twelve men drilled on six cords until they could close
it on a moving body in one breath. A hood. A count of minutes.

"Long."

"Who?"

She thought of the thin man with the slate. A man reading a gauge. He had stood three paces off the
whole time and never once been in reach. He had watched the rig work. He had watched it fail. He had
written all of it down.

"The one who wrote," she said.

Odile looked up. "Gone too."

"He'll build another."

None of them answered that.

Anirak picked up the net. It was heavy, the lead dragging in every band. She carried it to the end of
the slip and threw it in the harbor. It went down fast.

It cost them nothing. It was on the slate.

Upstairs in the chandler's rooms, the bed was still made. A cup of water on the sill. A rain-wet
footprint on the boards by the door, too small for any of the twelve.

She stood in the doorway and looked at the cup.

Then she went down, and told the harbor council, and asked them for paper, and wrote the name down
herself. Ardith. Twenty, or near it. A number behind the ear. Taken.
