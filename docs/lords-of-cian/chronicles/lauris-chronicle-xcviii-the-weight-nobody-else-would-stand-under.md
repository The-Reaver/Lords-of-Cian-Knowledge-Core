# Lauris Chronicle XCVIII: The Weight Nobody Else Would Stand Under

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1715`). Ninety-eighth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, Strand W. A stakes-free entry pairing Lauris with Abyss (Ren Oshaal, `CC-101`),
the crew's youngest member -- she becomes his preferred practice partner for controlling his
Negative-Density Variant field's active-range output, since her own biology tolerates standing
inside it without the discomfort most of the crew experience. Puts `CC-134`'s combat-joy trait on
the page as the pleasure of testing a genuine physical limit safely, alongside her established
witness psychology's patience with someone isolated by their own capability. No new named
characters.*

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Ren asked, the first time, whether it
hurt. It did not. I told him so, and he did not believe me, so I stood there again until he did."*

Ren Oshaal is, by a margin of some hundred and thirty-odd years, the youngest member of this crew,
and I have observed that this fact sits on him less as a distinction than as a mild, chronic
inconvenience, the way a smaller man might resent being handed the lightest end of every load out of
someone else's misplaced consideration. His field is his arsenal and, by his own admission to me on
more than one occasion, also the reason most of the crew keep a careful, unspoken distance from him
even at rest -- a passive fifteen-percent increase in gravitational load is a mild discomfort to
most; his active range, which he must practice controlling if he is ever to trust it in an
engagement, is considerably less mild, and volunteers for that practice are, understandably, scarce.

Lauris volunteered on the second week she noticed no one else had.

"It will not hurt me," she told him, the first time, with the specific flatness she reserves for
facts she does not expect to be believed on the strength of her word alone. He did not believe her.
She stood inside the active radius anyway, for the better part of an hour, while he brought the field
up in increments and watched her for any sign of strain that did not come, and by the end of that
hour I am told he had stopped watching her for signs of strain and started, instead, simply watching
the field itself, the way an artisan watches a tool finally behaving the way the diagrams always
promised it would.

I have since sat in on perhaps a dozen of these sessions, and I want to record what I have observed
of them honestly, because I think a reader unfamiliar with either of them might assume this is
charity on her part, extended downward to the crew's most isolated member out of some general
largesse. It is not charity. She has told me, plainly, that she enjoys it -- the specific, rare
pleasure of a body built without compromise being asked to actually meet something that tests it,
rather than simply outlasting whatever is put in front of it. Most threats this crew faces, she has
said, she survives through capacity so far in excess of the demand that there is no honest contest in
it at all. Ren's field, at its higher settings, is close enough to her own tolerances that standing
inside it is, for her, something closer to genuine exertion than most of what she does in a given
year, and she has told me she finds that scarcity worth seeking out on its own account, entirely
apart from what it does for him.

What it does for him, though I do not think either of them would frame it this way to the other, is
considerable. He was sent to the surface at sixteen because his own settlement could no longer bear
the structural cost of standing near him, and I have come to understand, across the years I have
watched him, that a great deal of what makes him careful, and quiet, and slower than most of this
crew to accept an offered kindness, traces back to that particular exile -- to having been, for the
first sixteen years of his life, a danger his own people loved him despite rather than because of.
Lauris did not ask him about any of that directly, the evening I am recording here, some months into
their sessions. She simply asked him to bring the field up one increment further than he had tried
before, and stood inside it without flinching while he did, and afterward, catching his breath before
she had needed to catch her own, he said, "You are not afraid of it."

"No," she said. "I have found very little in six thousand years that frightens me in the way I think
you mean. I have found that this makes me useful to people who need something to stand against
without breaking, more than it makes me remarkable."

He considered that for a while, in the particular stillness I have learned is his version of careful
arithmetic, and then said, "It is strange, having something to push against that pushes back the
right amount."

"I know the feeling," she told him. "I have gone most of my life without it, myself."

I do not think either of them meant that exchange as more than the plain trade of two facts. I have
come, nonetheless, to think it is the truest thing either has said to the other across a dozen
sessions of steadily increasing weight -- that a crew built from people the world found too much to
hold has, in this small unremarkable corner of it, produced exactly the kind of company each of them
had been missing without knowing to ask for it.
