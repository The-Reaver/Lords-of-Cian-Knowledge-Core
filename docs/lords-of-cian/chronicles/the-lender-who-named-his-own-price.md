# The Lender Who Named His Own Price

*Locked canon, Batch 266, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1395`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle XCII, wave 31.
Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. A new institutional register: predatory lending answered with
honest infrastructure rather than confrontation.*

---

The tally method was honest, and honest work took time. A wage-theft claim had to be verified
against Garren Hask's cross-witnessed figures, presented to an owner, and settled — sometimes in
days, sometimes in weeks, if an owner was stubborn or a district was backed up with other
corrections waiting their turn. It was fair. It was also, for a family that needed coin for a sick
child or a collapsed roof tonight, no help at all.

The lender understood that gap better than anyone. He worked the districts where the method hadn't
yet reached, and increasingly the districts where it had, offering coin against a worker's own
future verified back-pay at terms that started reasonable and compounded past reasonable within a
season. Nobody forced anyone to borrow from him. He never needed to. Desperation did that work for
him, every time, without his hand ever showing on the ledger page.

Garren Hask brought the pattern to Kanja with four separate borrowers' figures laid out side by
side — the same original debt, the same lender, four wildly different amounts now owed, the
compounding terms buried in language none of the four men could read well enough to have caught.
"We can't touch him through the method," Hask said. "He's not stealing wages. He's just charging
what the market will bear from people with no other choice."

They didn't touch him through the method. They built something beside it instead — a standing
emergency fund, seeded from the crew's own resources and a small, voluntary portion of every
settled verification, held by Hask and lent at no interest at all to anyone waiting on a claim
already confirmed and only pending an owner's payment. Not charity. A bridge, repaid the moment the
verified back-pay actually arrived.

The fund ran dry twice in its first two months, more borrowers finding it than anyone had
budgeted for, and both times Hask made the same hard call without asking Kanja to make it for him:
the oldest, most urgent claims first, the rest told plainly to wait. Nobody starved waiting. Nobody
loved the wait, either.

The lender's business didn't collapse. It simply thinned, borrower by borrower, as fewer and fewer
people found themselves with no better option than his terms. He never lost a single loan to
violence or threat — nobody broke his knees, nobody burned his ledgers. He lost them to a fund that
made his own math, for the first time, genuinely have to compete.
