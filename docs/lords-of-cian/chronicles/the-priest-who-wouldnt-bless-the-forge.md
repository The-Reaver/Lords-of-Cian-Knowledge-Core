# The Priest Who Wouldn't Bless the Forge

*Locked canon, Batch 262, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1331`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LXXXV, first
entry in the twenty-ninth wave. Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241). A
spiritual/religious-friction entry, the alias's first, resolved through respect rather than
persuasion. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The local shrine-keeper met the campaign at the settlement's edge before anyone else did, and made
his objection plain before a single forge had even been unloaded from the Anvil's deck: the
district's own tradition held that iron worked too fast, without the proper rites observed between
each stage of the tempering, carried a kind of imbalance forward into whatever it became — a belief
older than the Trust, older than the war, something the campaign's speed and scale threatened
without meaning to.

"I'm not here to argue your tradition is wrong," Kanja told him, and meant it plainly enough that
the shrine-keeper, expecting an argument, seemed briefly unsure what to do with the absence of one.
"I've never once claimed the method is the only right way to work iron. I've claimed it's a way
that gets tools into hands that need them faster than the alternative. Those aren't the same claim."

"They feel like the same claim, when the fast way is the only one anyone offers," the shrine-keeper
said. "My grandfather's grandfather kept the old rites. There won't be anyone left who remembers
them at all if every settlement learns to want speed instead."

Kanja didn't press the point, and didn't offer the campaign's forges to the district over the
shrine-keeper's continued objection, which surprised the man more than any counterargument would
have. Instead the crew worked a smaller, slower circuit at the district's own edge, teaching
alongside — not instead of — the traditional rites for anyone who wanted both, letting individual
smiths choose which pace and which observances mattered to them rather than treating the open
method as a wholesale replacement for what had come before it.

It took the shrine-keeper most of the campaign's stay to soften, and what finally moved him wasn't
an argument at all — it was watching one of his own novices choose, entirely on her own, to keep
the old rites even after learning the faster method sat right alongside them, unforced and
unthreatened by the option.

"I thought you'd come to replace what we have," the shrine-keeper admitted, at the campaign's
departure. "I didn't expect you'd just let it sit next to what you brought and trust people to
choose for themselves."

"That was always the actual method," Kanja said. "Not the iron. The choosing."
