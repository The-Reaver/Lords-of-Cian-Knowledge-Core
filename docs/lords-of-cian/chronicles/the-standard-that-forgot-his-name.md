# The Standard That Forgot His Name

*Locked canon, Batch 262, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1336`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle XC, wave 30,
closing the wave. Set generations after the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241), during the Long
Mask era. A distant-future institutional-legacy entry, distinct from every prior personal-encounter
legacy beat. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The examination hall held forty candidates the morning the journeyman certification came due, and
not one of them, reciting the Open-Forge Standard's own founding principle back to the examining
board, could have named who had first taught it or when. It had simply always existed, for as long
as any of their own teachers could remember — a craft tradition as old and as unremarkable to them
as the tools it governed, its origin folded somewhere into a past too far back to feel like history
at all rather than simply the shape the trade had always had.

The examiner, a woman three generations removed from the successor who'd first carved the
Standard's own name into guild law, didn't correct them. It had never been part of the
certification to know the founding story — only the method itself, taught hand to hand exactly the
way the unstealable verification once demanded, mouth to hand, generation to generation, long after
any stamped die or written record could have carried it forward alone.

A disguised, unremarkable man near the back of the hall — one more observer among the families
come to watch their own candidates certify, gray at the temples the way three centuries did to
anyone who let it show — watched a girl no older than sixteen complete the hand-taught verification
flawlessly, correcting an examiner's own minor slip in the sequence without any hesitation at all,
and said nothing to anyone about who he might once have been to any of it.

"You did that perfectly," the examiner told the girl, once the sequence was done. "Where did you
learn the correction on the final step? That's not in the standard teaching text."

"My teacher's teacher's teacher taught it that way," the girl said, uncertain whether she'd done
something wrong. "She said it was closer to how it was first done, before people started
simplifying it. I don't know where she learned it."

"Nobody does anymore," the examiner said, and there was no loss in how she said it, only the plain
fact of it. "That's what happens when a method actually works. It stops needing anyone to remember
where it came from."

The disguised man left before the certifications finished, unremarked and unrecognized, the
Standard's own long, patient drift into simple, unglamorous permanence exactly the outcome its
original author had asked for the day she refused to let it carry her own name.
