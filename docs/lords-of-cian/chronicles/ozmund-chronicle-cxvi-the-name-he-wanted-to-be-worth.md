# The Name He Wanted to Be Worth

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1845`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CXVI, third entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave, set strictly pre-
Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. A purely internal turning point — Ozmund
overhears how the household has already begun speaking of him in the third person, as a coming legend
rather than a living boy, and privately arrives at a distinction between the reputation the world is
already assembling for him and the far smaller, quieter thing he actually wants to be remembered for.
Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. No new named characters.*

---

There is a particular discomfort, the General told me, in overhearing yourself discussed as though you
were already history, while you are still standing close enough to correct the record and choose, in
that instant, not to. He gave me this one carefully, the way he gave me anything that touched on how he
wanted to be understood rather than merely on what had happened to him, and told me it was among the
quietest turning points of his whole youth, and among the ones he trusted least to explain well.

He was perhaps sixteen or seventeen by his own reckoning, passing along an outer corridor of the House
seat on some errand he no longer remembered, when he came within earshot of two of the household guard
speaking together in the low, unguarded way people speak when they believe no one who matters is close
enough to hear. They were not speaking unkindly. That was the part he wanted me to understand first —
there was no cruelty in it, only the ordinary, admiring shorthand people reach for when a young man has
already, without quite meaning to, become a figure larger than the boy walking the same halls as them.
One of the two called him "the young lord," which cost him nothing to hear. The other called him
something else — a phrase the General would not repeat to me exactly, only that it named a virtue he
had not yet earned in his own estimation, spoken with the easy confidence of men already certain of a
story that had not finished happening.

He told me he stood in the shadow of that corridor longer than the errand required, and that what struck
him was not pride, though he admitted a boy his age might reasonably have felt some, and not
embarrassment either, though there was some of that too. What struck him, he said, was a kind of quiet
vertigo — the sense that a version of himself was already being built out there, in other people's
mouths, assembled from fragments of things he'd actually done and a good deal more that he suspected he
hadn't, and that this version was going to go on being built whether or not he ever said a single word
to correct or confirm any part of it. He understood, standing in that corridor, that he had no control
at all over the shape the world would eventually decide to remember him in. That decision, he realized,
did not belong to him. It never would.

What he did have, he told me, was something smaller and entirely his own: the choice of what he was
actually trying to be worth, on the days no one was composing anything about him at all. He told me he
thought, in that corridor, of Osric's thirty years of unclaimed grief, and of Cobb's laughter in the
paddock grass, and of a boy named Joren he had let go rather than risk, and understood that none of
those moments would ever make it into whatever story the guardsmen were assembling in the corridors
behind him — that the story the household was building ran on victories and virtues large enough to be
worth repeating, while the actual shape of who he was trying to become lived almost entirely in rooms too
small and too private for anyone to ever tell of them at all.

He told me he made a kind of private decision that day, though he was careful to say it did not arrive
all at once and fully formed, the way the story wants to compress it now in the telling. It was this:
that he would let the world build whatever legend it needed to build, because he had already understood,
even that young, that he had no power to stop it — but that he would not measure himself against that
legend, ever, not even privately, not even on the days it might have been comforting to. He would measure
himself instead against the smaller, harder, mostly invisible question of whether he had actually been
worth something to the particular people in front of him that day. Not worth remembering. Worth trusting.
The distinction, he told me, sounded almost too fine to matter when he first tried to hold it in his own
mind. He told me it was, in fact, the only distinction that ever fully mattered to him again.
