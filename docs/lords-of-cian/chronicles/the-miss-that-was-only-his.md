# The Miss That Was Only His

*Locked canon, Batch 285, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1505`). Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle XCIV, wave 32, first entry in the wave. Months after the fourth-generation apprentice was ceded full forecasting authority (`MCD-1419`), he makes his first genuine independent miscalculation while holding it alone -- an honest miss, not sabotage or a factual trap, testing what the authority actually costs now that no one stands behind him to check it. Nothing about the reading had felt wrong. That was the part he kept returning to, days after: not one gut-catch, not one hesitation he'd talked himself past. He had read the pressure the way he'd been taught to read it, cross-checked it against the horizon color the way he'd been taught, and called a six-hour window of building weather that would close the strait to any prudent captain.

The weather never came. The system broke apart against a ridge of high pressure nobody's tradition had a name for, and the forty-vessel convoy he'd held at anchor for a day and a half sailed two full days late into a market that had already cleared its urgent orders to someone else's hulls.

No one died. No ship was lost. It was, by any measure that mattered to the ledger, a small miss -- smaller than a storm that had once simply failed to come, smaller by a wide margin than an old loss that still stung worse. But it was the first one that belonged entirely to him, with no student standing at the chart's edge ready to take it back, no successor's steadying hand on a call that had already been made and only needed confirming. Just his name, alone, against a window that hadn't opened.

He wrote it into the ledger himself, in full, the way the tradition demanded -- the pressure figures, the horizon reading, the ridge he hadn't accounted for, the two days it had cost forty captains who'd trusted his word over their own judgment. He did not soften the language. He did not, as he later admitted to the student, feel the honesty as generous the way he'd always assumed it would feel from this side of it.

The student read it standing beside him, said nothing for a long moment, and then turned to the next blank line in the same volume rather than to him.

"You're not going to check it," the apprentice said. Not a question this time either, though it landed harder than the same words had landed from the other side of the table, many seasons ago.

"There's nothing to check," the student said. "You read what was there. The ridge wasn't in anyone's book yet. It's in this one now." She tapped the fresh entry. "That's the whole of what changes when it's yours -- not that you stop being wrong. That you're the one who has to write it down."

Kanja, who had said little through the whole exchange, was the one who finally spoke to the part neither of them had. "Sephtis missed by two hours once," he said, "on a call with a fleet's whole night riding on it. He didn't stop reading the sky the next morning. He read it better." He looked at the young man who'd held the coast's whole weather in his hands for six months now, wrong for the first time on his own. "You'll read this strait again in a month. You'll read it right. That's not forgiveness. That's just what happens next."

He nodded, closed the ledger on his own handwriting, and did exactly that -- three weeks later, on a system with a ridge he recognized this time before it had fully formed, and said nothing about it to anyone but the page.
