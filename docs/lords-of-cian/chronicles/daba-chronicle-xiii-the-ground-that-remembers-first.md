# Daba Chronicle XIII: The Ground That Remembers First

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1583`). Entry 13 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes the first
real guerrilla-doctrine lesson Daba teaches the young Kanja -- terrain-as-weapon and small-unit
thinking taught as deliberate doctrine rather than expressed as raw instinct -- the specific scene
`MCD-1568` names as the shared root of Daba's own tactics and Kanja's later terrain-physics doctrine
at the Dredge-Line Ambush and Iron Shallows. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as

Daba took him to the dry wash on the far side of the ridge, a mile and a half from anything with a
name, because the first lesson had to happen somewhere failure cost nothing but a bruised ego and a
long walk home.

"Tell me what you see," Daba said, standing at the wash's lip, looking down into a channel of
sun-cracked clay maybe forty paces long, narrowing at its center to a gap two men could barely pass
side by side.

The boy looked for a while. Daba had learned, in the two weeks since the grain theft, that he did
this -- looked for a while, longer than most people were comfortable looking, before he said anything
at all. "A place where you'd fight if you were outnumbered," he said finally. "The walls come in at
the middle. Whoever's chasing has to bunch up to get through."

"That's what your body already knows," Daba said. "It's not wrong. It's incomplete. Tell me what
happens after they bunch up."

The boy considered it. "You'd hit them there. While they're narrow."

"With what."

"Whatever I had."

Daba shook his head, not unkindly. "That's the part nobody taught you, because nobody's ever had to.
You've spent your whole life alone in a fight, reading one collapsing structure at a time, one
runner's stride at a time. I'm not teaching you to read ground faster. You already do that better
than any of my own people, and most of them have been doing this for years. I'm teaching you to stop
fighting alone in your head, even when you are alone in the fight."

He walked the boy down into the wash and had him stand at the narrow point, and then had him walk the
full forty paces from the wide mouth to the choke, twice, three times, until the boy could describe
without looking exactly how far a running man covered that ground in the time it took Daba to count
to six.

"A cell of three could hold this against fifteen," Daba said. "Not because three fighters beat five
apiece. Because the ground only lets two of the fifteen reach you at once, no matter how many are
behind them. The other thirteen are just weight, pressing forward, doing nothing but making it harder
for the two in front to retreat. You don't fight fifteen men here. You fight two men, fifteen times,
and the ground does the arithmetic for you."

"That's what I did with the scaffold," the boy said slowly. "I wasn't fighting three enforcers. I was
just making sure only one of them was ever actually in the fight."

"Yes. Except you did it once, by instinct, against three men who didn't know they were being managed.
I'm going to teach you to do it on purpose, against people who might." Daba crouched, drew a rough
plan of the wash in the clay with a stick -- entry, choke, two fallback lines, a third mark he
explained was never a place to stand but a place to be seen standing, to pull a pursuit's attention
exactly where the ground wanted it pulled. "Small-unit thinking isn't about being strong enough to
win a fight. It's about being disciplined enough to never let the fight be the size the enemy thinks
it is."

They ran it as a drill for the better part of the afternoon -- Daba playing the pursuit, forcing the
boy to hold the choke, retreat along the first fallback line, hold again, until the pattern stopped
being a thing the boy performed and started being a thing his feet did without consulting him first.
Daba corrected him twice, both times for the same fault: an instinct to improvise a new read of the
ground mid-drill rather than trust the plan they'd built together before the drill began.

"Your gift will always tell you a better answer exists," Daba said, the second time, not as a
rebuke. "Sometimes it will even be right. But a plan a unit can trust is worth more than a better
answer only one man can see. That's the discipline. Not reading ground. Trusting the read enough to
share it before you need it."

The boy was quiet on the walk back, turning that over the way Daba had already learned he turned
everything over -- slowly, completely, without performing the effort of it for anyone watching.

"I've never had anyone to share a read with," he said eventually, not quite a confession, more an
observation offered to someone who might understand its weight.

"Now you do," Daba said. "For as long as you want one."
