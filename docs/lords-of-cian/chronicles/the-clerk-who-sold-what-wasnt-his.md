# The Clerk Who Sold What Wasn't His

*Locked canon, Batch 286, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1520`). Captain Alias Chronicle C, wave 34. The sub-series' first purely voluntary, uncoerced betrayal for personal gain -- and the charter's first real test on a question it never addressed: what happens when someone must be removed, not merely chooses to leave.*

---

Nobody's family was being held anywhere this time. Nobody's sister was leverage in a Directorate handler's hands. The relief clerk who'd been quietly skimming a fifth of every grain shipment through Pier Nine's own registry for four months had done it for the plainest reason there was: the black-market price was good, and he'd calculated, correctly for a while, that nobody was checking his figures closely enough to notice.

Danne Sok's daughter noticed. It was her own careful counting, learned the ordinary slow way from Garren Hask's own methods, that caught the shortfall's pattern where a less patient audit would have missed it — a fifth, consistently, never enough at once to be obvious, always covered by rounding that looked innocent until four months of it sat next to each other on one page.

The dispute council convened, and for the first time in its history, found the charter simply had nothing to say. Membership was free to leave, per Maret Vos's own precedent (`MCD-593`). The founding generation's mortality was accounted for. Nothing anywhere addressed what the crew did when someone had to be made to go — not chosen to, made to — for reasons that weren't coercion, weren't a mistake honestly owned, weren't anything but plain theft from people who'd trusted him with their own hunger.

"We built this whole thing assuming everyone who ever left us would leave with clean hands," Corren Halst said. "We never once planned for someone who wouldn't."

They debated it for two full sessions. Efa Gol argued for restitution and expulsion both, with no path back offered. Callum Breck, newly seated, argued that permanent banishment sat wrong against everything the crew claimed to believe about people being more than their worst four months — but that saying so out loud to the clerk's own face, in front of everyone he'd stolen from, mattered more than any punishment they could hand down. Kanja, one vote among the rest, said the least of anyone in the room.

The ruling that emerged wasn't clean, and nobody pretended it was: full restitution, calculated to the same fifth he'd skimmed; formal removal from the crew, effective immediately, the charter's free-membership clause amended that same session with its first involuntary counterpart; and an explicit, uncomfortable door left open — not forgiveness offered in advance, just the plain statement that if he ever genuinely earned trust back, the crew wouldn't pretend that was impossible on principle.

The clerk left the session in silence. Garren Hask's successor closed the ledger's page on him the same way every other page had ever been closed — the number entered honestly, nothing rounded in anyone's favor, including his.
