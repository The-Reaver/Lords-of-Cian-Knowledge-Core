# The Mother Who Asked Him to Say the Name

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Captain Alias Chronicle LII, wave 18. Rebellion-adjacent era. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters — reuses already-locked Garren Hask; one unnamed bereaved mother.*

---

She came alone, on foot, and told the watch at the gangplank only that she wasn't there to fight and
wasn't there to beg — that she'd walked most of a week to ask one question of the man the crew called
Captain, and would leave the moment she had her answer. Garren Hask, who handled every such approach
personally since the casualty lists had become his responsibility as much as Kanja's, brought her
aboard rather than turning her away.

"My son served the Trust," she said, once she was seated across from him. "Conscripted, not
volunteered — he'd have told you that himself, if he still could. He died at the causeway your people
took two winters back. I've read every account of that day I could find. None of them use his name.
They use a number, or a unit, or nothing at all. I want to know if you remember it."

Kanja was quiet for a moment. "I don't. I'm not going to pretend to you that I do — you'd know, and
it would cost you more to be lied to than to hear the truth."

"Then why should I have come at all."

"You shouldn't have had to. That's the honest answer, not an excuse." He nodded to Hask, who was
already reaching for the ledger he kept of every engagement — not the Directorate's tallies, but the
crew's own, cross-checked afterward against whatever records could be recovered from both sides,
because he'd long ago decided a war fought honestly required knowing the cost on every side of it,
not only the one that called itself his own. "If he's recorded anywhere, it's in there. Tell us his
name, and if it takes all night, we'll find him."

It took most of two hours. When Hask finally found the entry — a supply-line casualty, not a combat
death, crushed under a collapsed cart during the retreat — he read it aloud without softening any of
it, and Kanja repeated the name back to her exactly as it was written, once, plainly, without
performance.

She sat with that for a long moment before she spoke again. "That's not an apology."

"No. I don't think I'm owed the chance to give you one, and I don't think you came for it. You came
to know he wasn't nothing to anyone who fought against him. He isn't. He wasn't. I can't undo what
happened to him. I can make sure his name exists somewhere outside your own memory of him, so it
doesn't end the day yours does."

She left before dawn, taking nothing but a copied page from Hask's ledger, and never gave her own
name in return. Hask asked, once she was gone, whether that had been enough.

"I don't know," Kanja said. "I don't think 'enough' is a thing that exists for what she's carrying.
I think all we did was make sure she wasn't carrying it entirely alone anymore."
