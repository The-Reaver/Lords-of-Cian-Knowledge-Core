# Lauris Chronicle CVII: The Weight They Moved for No Reason at All

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1724`). One hundred seventh entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of the second 50-Chronicle wave (Chronicles LX-CIX), the twelfth entry of the
"Strand W" (Witness / present-day, quiet-register) thread within this wave. A second Kanja entry,
deliberately inverting `MCD-1671`'s register -- where that entry showed exquisite, minimal-force
control on a fragile object, this one shows raw shared capacity spent on something enormous and
consequence-free, extending the "operational deference and equipment-maintenance authority" clause
of `MCD-215` into a purely playful register between the two heaviest hitters in the crew. No new named
characters. Written under Abad's blanket authorization: "go."*

---

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Kanja asked me to help him move a boulder
that did not need moving, to a place it did not need to be, for no reason either of us could state
aloud without laughing. It took us four minutes. We could have done it in one. Neither of us was in
any hurry to end it sooner."*

I have written one account already of Kanja seeking Lauris out for the opposite of what most of this
crew's story assumes either of them is for -- not raw capacity, but the exquisite discipline of
spending almost none of it on a clock small enough to fit in two hands. I want to set down a second
account of him here that inverts the first entirely, because I believe a reader who has watched both
of them break sieges and hold cities together deserves, once, to see what the two of them do with
their own combined capacity when nothing whatsoever requires them to spend it carefully.

The boulder in question sat at the edge of a construction site that did not, strictly, need it moved
-- a stubborn, half-buried mass of old rock that had simply outlasted whatever project once required
its removal, left where it was for lack of any urgent reason to shift it. Kanja had walked past it, by
his own account, perhaps forty times without giving it a second thought. I do not know precisely what
changed on the forty-first.

"I want to move that," he said, apropos of nothing, when Lauris found him standing in front of it with
an expression I have rarely seen on him outside of combat -- not grim resolve, which is his more usual
register, but something closer to plain, unguarded curiosity, the specific look of a man wondering
what he is actually capable of when nothing requires him to find out.

"Where would you like it moved to?"

"I don't know yet. Somewhere it isn't now."

She considered the boulder for a long moment with the same serious attention I have watched her give
to far smaller, far more delicate objects, and I understood, watching her, that she recognized the
offer for exactly what it was: not a task, but an invitation to spend, for once, without counting the
cost of the spending.

"I could move it myself," he said. "I know I could. I would rather not, this time."

"Then we'll move it together."

What followed was, by any ordinary measure, an entirely unremarkable four minutes -- two of the most
dangerous individuals on this world setting their combined weight and strength against a piece of rock
that offered them, by comparison to what either of them has broken elsewhere in this account, no
resistance whatsoever. I want to be precise about what I watched in it regardless, because I think
the precision is the whole of what makes the account worth keeping. They did not rush it. They did not
treat the boulder's total lack of challenge as a reason to finish quickly and move on to something that
mattered more. Kanja set his shoulder against one face of it and Lauris against the other, and for the
better part of those four minutes the two of them simply pushed, unhurried, matching each other's pace
by something that looked, from where I stood, less like coordination and more like two people who
have each spent centuries being the largest capacity in most rooms, finally allowed to be exactly that
in the same room, at the same time, against something that could not possibly object.

"We could have done that in a fraction of the time," Lauris said, once the boulder sat in its new,
entirely arbitrary resting place, some thirty paces from where it had lain for longer than either of
them could guess.

"I know."

"I do not think either of us wanted to."

"No," he agreed. "I don't think I did either."

He sat down on the boulder itself once it had settled, which struck me as its own small, unspoken
tribute to the exercise -- a man who has spent centuries carrying the weight of a war and, before that,
a childhood he rarely discusses, resting, briefly, on a rock that meant nothing to anyone, moved for a
reason neither of them could fully name. Lauris sat beside him, and for a while neither of them said
anything further, the particular comfortable silence I have come to recognize as characteristic of
her closest companionships settling over the two of them without either one troubling to fill it.

"I do not often get to do something simply because I want to," Kanja said eventually, in the plain
register I have learned, across enough chapters of his own story, means he is saying something he
rarely permits himself to say aloud. "Most of what I move, I move because something requires it of me."

"I know that better than most," Lauris said. "I am glad you wanted this one."

I have written a great deal, across this account, about the burdens this crew's leads each carry, and
I do not intend to repeat the catalogue here. I want to close this entry with something smaller
instead: that the two heaviest hitters in this crew's entire story spent four unhurried minutes moving
an enormous, meaningless weight to a place it did not need to be, for no better reason than that they
could, and that neither of them, by any measure I could take of them afterward, looked more at ease
than they did sitting on it once the moving was done.
