# What the Old Practice Cost

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1815`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXVI, House politics strand wave 3, seventh entry.*

---

The General told me this one plainly, without much of the wry humor he brought to most of these, and
I understood, hearing it, that it sat closer to something he was genuinely proud of than most of the
stories he offered me from those years.

House Verehimu had kept, since long before Aethelgard's own tenure, a formal custom for its most
serious debt defaults — not the ordinary shortfalls that passed through the clerks' office or the
Seneschal's minor courts, but the rare, larger cases of tenants who fell so far behind on their
obligations to the House that the debt itself threatened to end their tenancy outright. The custom,
called simply the Reckoning Walk within the household, required a defaulting tenant to appear before
the House's assembled staff and any other tenants present on rent day, publicly acknowledge the debt
by amount, and receive whatever terms the presiding Verehimu chose to set — an accounting meant, in
theory, to be transparent and evenhanded, and understood, in practice, the General said, by everyone
who'd ever witnessed one, as a public humiliation dressed in the language of fairness.

He was twenty-six when he presided over his first Reckoning Walk without his father present, called
to account a tenant farmer for a debt the General never specified to me in detail, beyond
saying it had arisen from two consecutive poor harvests rather than any mismanagement on the man's
part. He said what struck him, standing at the front of that gathering, wasn't the specifics of the
case, which was straightforward enough to resolve fairly on its own terms, but the man's face while he
waited to be called forward — not shame at the debt itself, which the General thought any reasonable
person would carry with some discomfort regardless of the setting, but a deeper, more corrosive
humiliation at being made to carry it in front of every person he worked alongside, every week, for
the rest of however long his tenancy lasted afterward.

The General said he set fair terms that day, terms he believed genuinely served the man's interests
and the House's both. But he told me he rode back to the manor afterward turning over a single
question he couldn't set down: whether the custom's public nature actually served any purpose the
House's own private ledgers couldn't serve just as well, or whether it survived, sixty years on, for
no better reason than that it had always been done that way, and no one senior enough to end it had
ever found the discomfort of a defaulting tenant's face reason enough to bother.

He raised it with Aethelgard directly, some weeks later, expecting resistance — the custom was old,
and the General had learned by then that age alone tended to earn a practice a certain presumption of
wisdom it hadn't always deserved. What he found instead was a father more willing to be persuaded than
he'd anticipated. Aethelgard told him, when pressed, that he'd inherited the custom the same way he'd
inherited most things, without examining it closely, and that he'd be glad to hear a genuine argument
for changing it rather than simply be told it was cruel, which he considered too easy an objection on
its own to act on.

The argument Ozmund made was practical rather than purely moral, though he told me the moral case sat
underneath it the whole time: that public reckonings did nothing the private accounting already
achieved in terms of actually recovering what was owed or setting fair terms going forward, and that
what they did achieve — a tenant's ongoing, compounding humiliation among the very people he'd have to
keep working beside — actively worked against the House's own interest in keeping its tenants
productive and loyal rather than resentful and diminished. He proposed the private accounting remain
identical in every substantive term, presided over with the same seriousness, recorded with the same
formality — but conducted in a closed room, with only the presiding Verehimu, the Seneschal, and the
tenant himself present.

Aethelgard approved the change within the month, remarking only, as he often did when he'd been
genuinely moved by an argument rather than merely persuaded by convenience, that he wished he'd
thought to question the custom himself years earlier. The General told me the Reckoning Walk, in its
public form, was never held again in his lifetime at that House, and that of everything he'd changed
or built in those years, it was the one he thought of most rarely and, when he did think of it,
with the least ambivalence of any of them — a small mercy, quietly withdrawn from a system that had
never needed to include it in the first place.
