# The Distance Station Made

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1822`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCIII, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2. Set strictly pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's mid
adolescence (roughly age fifteen), years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard
alive, Ozmund still the House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from an account Ozmund himself gave him, in the same rare, self-volunteered register as
Chronicle XLIV. The first entry in the strand to directly test Cobb's friendship with the General
against the fact of their different stations, rather than showing the friendship operating in a space
where station doesn't yet apply. No new named characters.*

---

This is another the General gave me himself, and he told it more slowly than the first one, the way a
man tells a story he's turned over enough times to know exactly which part still catches. It concerned
a visiting delegation — minor nobility from a neighboring holding, there for two nights on business he
said he never fully understood at fifteen and had no particular interest in understanding now — and a
single formal dinner that required, for the first time in either of their lives, that Cobb attend him
directly, in the hall, in front of people who mattered to House Verehimu's standing.

He said it hadn't occurred to him, until the steward laid it out plainly that morning, that there was
anything to think about. Cobb had been in and out of his company for years by then, in the stable yard,
in the low places of the House nobody supervised closely, and the General told me he'd simply assumed
that easiness traveled with them wherever they went. He said he learned otherwise the moment Cobb walked
into that hall in a steward's borrowed coat that didn't fit him properly, stood exactly where he'd been
told to stand, and addressed him — not by the name he'd used in the stable yard for eight years, not
even by any name at all, but by the formal address every servant in that hall used for every member of
the family they served.

He said it landed harder than he expected, and worse, that he understood immediately it wasn't Cobb's
choice. Cobb had no more freedom to drop the formal address in that hall than the General had freedom to
walk out of it early. What unsettled him, he told me, wasn't the address itself. It was watching Cobb's
face while he gave it — carefully blank, correctly deferential, doing the job he'd been told to do
exactly as well as it needed doing — and understanding, for the first time with any real weight, that
the ease between them in the stable yard had never once been a fact of the world. It had been a mercy
the world happened to allow them, in one particular corner of the House, that could be withdrawn the
moment anyone with real authority decided it mattered where it was withdrawn.

He said he got through the dinner without incident and found Cobb afterward, near the stable's side
door, still in the ill-fitting coat, and asked him — badly, he admitted, fifteen and not yet skilled at
saying difficult things plainly — whether it had bothered him. Cobb's answer, the General told me,
word for word as he remembered it: that it hadn't bothered him to say the words. It had bothered him to
watch the General's face while he said them, because he could see exactly what it was costing him to
hear it, and he hadn't known, until that dinner, that it would cost him anything at all.

They agreed, standing at that door, on something neither of them ever fully put into words again after
that night, by the General's account — that the hall could have whatever it needed from both of them,
the correct forms, the correct distance, the correct silence about a friendship the House had no
category for, and that none of it would touch what happened in the stable yard when no one with real
authority was watching. He said it was, in its own way, the first time he understood that dignity and
distance weren't opposites — that a man could hold a formal title in one room and a real friendship in
another, and that the trick wasn't choosing between them but refusing to let either one lie about what
the other actually was.

He told me he never again assumed, after that dinner, that ease between two people from different
stations was simply a fact that traveled with them everywhere. He said he'd carried that lesson into
every real friendship he built afterward, across every kind of distance a title could put between two
men, and that he owed the shape of it entirely to a boy in a coat that didn't fit him, standing exactly
where he'd been told to stand, and still finding a way, afterward, at a door nobody official was
watching, to tell him the truth.
