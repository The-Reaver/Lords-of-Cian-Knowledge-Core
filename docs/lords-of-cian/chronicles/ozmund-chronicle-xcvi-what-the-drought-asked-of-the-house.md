# What the Drought Asked of the House

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1825`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCVI, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2. Set strictly pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's later
adolescence (roughly age seventeen), years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard
alive, Ozmund still the House heir, the young heir by now a regular, informal presence at household
governance meetings (consistent with Chronicle XLV's own note that his involvement in daily
governance had visibly begun by this period). Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per
`VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a single meeting all four of the strand's established
figures — Armsmaster Berrin Hollis, falconer Annis Fairweather, Master Alric Fenmoor, and Sergeant
Oswin Kade — separately confirmed to him as having happened, though no single account of it survives
in full; Red Beard notes openly that he has assembled the scene from four partial memories rather than
one complete one. The strand's first full ensemble entry, all four figures in the same room together.
No new named characters.*

---

I want to be honest about this one, because it's the first account in this strand I've had to build
rather than simply transcribe. No single person I spoke with — not Hollis, not Fairweather, not Fenmoor,
not Kade — remembered that meeting whole. Each of them remembered a piece of it clearly enough to swear
to, and each piece fit the others closely enough, in sequence and in substance, that I've chosen to set
them down together as one account rather than four fragments, trusting the reader to understand that
this is reconstruction, not verbatim record, in a way most of what I've given the General is not.

The drought that summer had gone on long enough to threaten the well levels that fed both the stable
troughs and the mews's own washing water, and word had reached the steward's table that the guard's own
training rotation — which ran men and horses hard through the same water supply — would need to be cut
back if nothing changed. It was, by every account, meant to be a small administrative meeting: the
steward, a clerk, and whichever household heads had a stake in how the remaining water got divided. It
became, somewhere in the telling, the four of them arguing in one room, and the young heir sitting quietly
in the corner where he'd apparently taken to sitting at such meetings by then, saying nothing until he
had something worth saying.

Hollis wanted water prioritized for the horses used in active guard and training rotation, on the
straightforward logic that an untrained man or an unconditioned horse was a slower liability in any real
emergency the drought itself might eventually cause. Fairweather wanted the mews protected first, on the
equally straightforward logic that her birds could not simply be rationed and recovered the way horses
could, and that eleven working hawks represented years of training that water alone, once lost, could
never buy back. Kade, closer to Hollis's position than not but arriving at it from a different angle,
wanted the guard's own drinking water guaranteed above all else, arguing that a House that let its own
sworn men go thirsty while horses drank their fill had already broken the arrangement he'd once described
to me at length in his own account (Chronicle XLV) — protection given, obligation honored in return.

Fenmoor, by every account, said almost nothing for the first half of the argument, which Kade
remembered finding uncharacteristic and slightly unsettling in a man normally so quick to correct
imprecise thinking. What he was doing instead, it turned out, was working the actual figures — how much
water the wells could reliably yield at current levels, how much each competing need genuinely required
rather than what each speaker instinctively felt it required, and where, if the true numbers were laid
out plainly rather than argued from position, there might be more slack in the system than anyone
arguing from their own corner had allowed themselves to see.

What he produced, once he finally spoke, wasn't a compromise in the usual sense — no one got less than
they'd asked for, and no one got everything either. It was a genuine finding: that the wells, properly
measured rather than estimated, could support the guard's drinking water, the mews, and a reduced but
still functional training rotation for the horses, provided the household's own ornamental gardens,
which no one at that table had thought to mention because no one considered them a real stake in the
argument, gave up their water allocation entirely for the season.

All four accounts agreed on what happened next: a silence, and then the General's own voice from the
corner, the first thing he'd said in the whole meeting, asking only whether anyone objected. No one did.
Fenmoor's figures were adopted within the hour, and the gardens, by every account, looked considerably
worse for it by autumn and considerably better again the following spring. Kade told me he'd walked away
from that meeting with a clearer sense than he'd ever had before of what made the House worth serving —
not that it always agreed with itself, which it plainly didn't, but that when it disagreed, it disagreed
in the same room, out loud, until someone found the number that let everyone keep what actually mattered.
