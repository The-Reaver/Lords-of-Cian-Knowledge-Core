# The Blame That Wasn't His to Clear

*Locked canon, Batch 257, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1176`). Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LXV, wave 22. A reputation-defense entry -- Directorate propaganda blames the Blue-Collar Titan for a collapse he didn't cause -- testing the alias's honesty principle turned inward.*

---

The pamphlet reached the tunnels before Kanja did, passed hand to hand by workers who didn't know what to believe. A market undercroft two districts over had come down under its own weight three days earlier, killing six, and the Directorate's printed account named the Blue-Collar Titan's "reckless tunneling" as the cause -- his tunnels ran nowhere near it, but the name alone was enough to make the claim land with people who wanted someone to blame.

"They didn't even check the maps," Garren Hask said, holding the pamphlet like it might catch fire. "You could disprove this in an afternoon."

"I could," Kanja said. "That's not the same as it mattering that I did."

The easy path was obvious and useless -- march in, declare himself innocent, let the reputation do the rest of the work the way it had done for him a dozen times before. He'd spent this entire alias building the opposite habit: never fabricate, never take the shortcut a name could buy, let the structure itself make the argument. Clearing his own name the lazy way would have meant becoming exactly the kind of self-serving legend he'd refused to let the fire-rescue or the mine-rescue turn him into.

So he went to the actual collapse site instead, walked it with two independent surveyors from a guild that owed him nothing, and produced a forensic account naming the real cause -- a Directorate requisition order that had stripped the undercroft's supporting timber for a war project eight months earlier, documented in the district's own procurement records, which the surveyors copied and distributed themselves rather than him. He never denied the pamphlet's claim in his own voice at all. He let a competing, better-sourced truth outcompete it.

It took eleven days instead of an afternoon. By the end of it the pamphlet was still circulating in places that wanted it true regardless of evidence, and Kanja had made his peace with that before the surveyors ever finished their report.

"Some of them will believe the lie forever," Hask said, reading the surveyors' final account beside him. "No amount of true paper fixes that."

"Then it wasn't the paper's job to fix that part," Kanja said. "Its job was to make sure the true account existed at all, whether or not everyone reads it."
