# What One of the Twenty Carried Away

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Crow King Alias Chronicle LII, wave 18. Rebellion
era, roughly a year after the six-week curriculum ("The School That Had No Name," Chronicle XLVIII).
Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

Word reached the apprentice before it reached Kanja: a raiding company, unaffiliated with the
Directorate and unaffiliated with the Rebellion, had used something close enough to the craft's own
method to empty a granary ahead of a rightful harvest, leaving a village with a winter it hadn't
budgeted for. The pattern was crude, imprecise in ways the twenty scouts had all been taught to
avoid — but it was unmistakably built from pieces of what they'd learned.

It took less than a day to trace it to one of them. Captured by the raiding company months earlier,
starved and given a plain choice between teaching what she knew and not surviving the week, she'd
taught it.

"I need you to understand I'm not asking whether to punish her," the apprentice said, when she
brought it to Kanja rather than deciding alone, the same way she'd once brought him the third
generation's refusal. "I already know I'm not going to. I need you to understand what this actually
costs, because I don't think either of us priced it honestly when we agreed to teach twenty strangers
in a drill yard."

"You knew this was possible."

"I knew it was possible in the abstract. I didn't know what it would feel like when it was a woman
I'd personally taught, choosing between her own life and a village she'd never meet. I still don't
think she was wrong to choose the way she did."

Kanja didn't argue that she was. "Then the answer isn't to stop teaching it, and it isn't to punish
her for surviving. The answer is that the craft needs something it's never needed before — a way for
the people who actually trained under us to prove it, and a way for everyone else to tell the
difference between us and whoever else has since learned to fake the shape of it."

What they built over the following weeks was smaller than any prior evolution of the craft and, the
apprentice suspected, more important than most of them: a specific phrase, never written down,
carried only person to person, woven quietly into any pattern that genuinely came from Kanja's own
lineage — nothing a captured practitioner under duress would think to volunteer, because nothing
about it looked deliberate to anyone who hadn't been told what to listen for.

"It won't stop this from happening again," Kanja said, once it was done. "It'll let the next village
tell the difference between us and whoever's copying us badly enough to leave people hungry."

The woman was never found. The apprentice thought of her often enough that Kanja eventually stopped
asking whether she wanted to talk about it, and simply waited until she did.
