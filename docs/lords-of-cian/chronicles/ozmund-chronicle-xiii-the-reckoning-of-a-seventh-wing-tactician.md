# The Reckoning of a Seventh Wing Tactician

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1742`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XIII — second entry in the Val Mirel strand. Set strictly pre-Book-1, years before
the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not present in
this entry. Ozmund is roughly fifteen. Dramatizes a specific Kareth War-Order tactical principle
grounded in Val Mirel's Seventh Wing role (`MCD-101`/`CC-004`), set explicitly against a House
Verehimu steward's more political, persuasion-based method for the same problem. Narrated by Red
Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years
later. No new named characters.*

---

This is one of the few stories the General ever told me twice, years apart, and the two tellings
never disagreed on a single fact between them — which told me more than the story itself did, because
by then I'd learned that the things he told me the same way twice were the things that had actually
shaped him, and the things he only ever told once were the things still costing him something to say.

A raiding band had been working the grain road out past the eastern mills, small and patient the way
the worst ones are — never taking enough to justify the cost of chasing them, never showing themselves
long enough to be counted properly. They'd struck twice by the time word reached the House, both
times against minor convoys, both times gone before the House Guard's outriders arrived. A third
convoy was due within the week, larger than the first two combined, and Aethelgard was three days'
ride south settling a border dispute of his own, which left the matter, formally, in the hands of his
seneschal — and, because Val Mirel happened to be in residence that month, informally in the hands of
two people who had never once agreed on how a problem ought to be solved.

The seneschal's plan came first, and the General told me it was not a foolish one. Find whoever led
the band, offer him a standing toll — a fraction of every convoy's worth, paid quietly, on the
condition the raids stopped entirely — and buy six months of certainty for less than one more
successful raid would cost outright. It was, the seneschal argued, the Verehimu way: a problem solved
with coin and correctly-aimed words was a problem that never had to be solved twice, because
everyone involved walked away with something to protect.

Val Mirel listened to the whole of it without interrupting, which the General said was itself
unusual for her, and then asked the seneschal a single question: how many men did he believe were in
the band. The seneschal answered with the number the outriders had counted at the second raid — six,
maybe seven. Val Mirel said that was the number the band had wanted counted, and that a band patient
enough to strike twice and vanish twice was patient enough to show a stranger exactly as much of
itself as a toll negotiation required to look reasonable. Pay them, she said, and the House would be
buying peace from six or seven men while some larger number it had never actually seen kept the real
count to itself.

She did not argue the seneschal out of his plan. She asked to be given the week before it was acted
on, and Aethelgard's authority in his absence being what it was, she got it. She took Ozmund with her
— not to fight, she told him plainly, but to watch, because a Seventh Wing tactician's first and last
discipline was never how a force struck. It was how long she was willing to watch it before deciding
it had shown her everything it had.

They spent four days on a ridge above the grain road doing nothing the General could describe as more
than sitting. She had him mark every hour: what moved, what didn't, which trees held longer shadows
than the light alone explained, which stretch of road the outriders' patrol pattern left uncovered
longest and for what reason. On the fourth evening she pointed, without ceremony, to a dry wash a
half-mile off the road the outriders had never once ridden — too narrow, too inconvenient, exactly the
kind of ground a rider dismisses and a man on foot uses as a home. Eleven, she told him. Not six.
Eleven, provisioned for a stay measured in weeks, not days, which meant the two prior raids had never
been the whole of their intent — they were testing the road's patience the same way she was testing
theirs.

"Your father's man wanted to buy six months from six men," she told him, the second telling adding
this line the first hadn't, though the General swore the meaning never changed. "I wanted to know what
eleven men patient enough to hide a camp from mounted outriders actually needed badly enough to risk
it. Coin answers the count you're shown. Only watching answers the count you aren't."

What happened to the band itself the General never told me in any detail worth repeating here — only
that it ended with far fewer House Guard committed than the seneschal's own contingency had budgeted
for, and that his mother never once told the seneschal he'd been wrong, only that his plan and hers
had both been aimed at the same outcome by two different clocks, his bought in coin and hers paid in
patience. It was the first time, he told me, that he understood the two halves of him weren't
opposed at all. They were simply spending different currencies against the same debt.
