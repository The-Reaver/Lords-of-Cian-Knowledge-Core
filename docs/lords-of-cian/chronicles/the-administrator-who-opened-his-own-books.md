# The Administrator Who Opened His Own Books

*Locked canon, Batch 119, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-406`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle VI, closing the
second wave. Rebellion era, unarmed throughout. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material.
Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The district administrator had spent two days refusing every request for access to his own wage
records, certain that stalling would outlast whatever patience a rebel commander could be expected
to bring to a paperwork dispute. He had not accounted for a method built entirely around patience as
its actual weapon.

"You can keep the books locked as long as you'd like," Kanja told him, on the third morning, having
spent the previous two collecting testimony from every worker willing to speak without the
administrator's own records to cross-check against. "I've got four hundred sworn statements already,
independently corroborated against each other. Your locked books aren't protecting you from
documentation. They're just removing your own side of the story from a record that's getting written
whether you participate or not."

The administrator held out one more day before the arithmetic became undeniable even to him: four
hundred consistent, cross-corroborated worker statements, compiled with or without his cooperation,
would read to any capital auditor as more credible than a district administrator's continued silence.
He opened his books on the fourth day, not from any change of conscience, but from the plain,
unsentimental calculation that participating in an account already being written gave him more
control over its final shape than refusing ever would.

"You understood something most men in my position never do," he admitted, once the reconciled ledger
— his own records and the workers' testimony, cross-checked and largely, uncomfortably, agreeing —
was complete. "I thought silence was a defense. You proved it was just a delay, and a delay only
matters if the thing being delayed eventually goes away. Yours never was going to."

"That's the whole method." Kanja closed the final ledger page, the district's true wage debt now
documented in both directions. "I don't need you to agree with me. I don't even need you to like the
outcome. I just need the account to be accurate, and accurate accounts have a way of becoming true
whether or not the people they're written about wanted them to be."

The administrator's own testimony, added last and unprompted, became one of the method's most-cited
examples in the districts that followed — proof, crew members would say afterward, that even a
hostile witness eventually preferred participating in an honest record over being written about by
one he'd refused to help build.
