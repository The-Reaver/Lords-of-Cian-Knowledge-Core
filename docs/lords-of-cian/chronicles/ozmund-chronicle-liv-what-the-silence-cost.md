# What the Silence Cost

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1783`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series, Chronicle LIV, Draconis strand wave 3.*

---

The General told me most of these stories more than once, over years, refining them the way a man refines a thing he returns to often. He told me this one exactly once, in full, and never again, and I understood without being told that he didn't trust himself to tell it a second time without the telling costing him something he wasn't prepared to keep paying.

Brenner was grown by then — the boy from the muster yard, the one Draconis had pulled off the cut list over a groundskeeper's daughter, now some years into the House Guard's ranks and posted, that season, to an escort detail running grain assessments along a stretch of the eastern holdings that had given the household trouble before. It should have been ordinary work. It had been ordinary work for most of the season, the General told me, right up until a band of raiders working the same stretch — displaced men, hungry men, the kind the eastern rate disputes had been quietly producing for years without anyone in the household connecting the two — mistook an assessor's modest column for something worth the risk of attacking in daylight.

The General was riding with that column himself, a rare enough thing by then that he could no longer fully remember, telling it to me, why he'd chosen to that particular week. What he remembered with total clarity, he said, was the moment the raiders broke from the tree line and the Spike came fully awake the way it always did, instant and total and entirely his to use, and the moment directly after it, in which he made the same choice he had made on the road as a boy and at the reception as a young man and had never, in all the years since, stopped making.

He fought as a man fights. That was how he put it to me — not as an excuse, he was careful to say, but as the plainest description he had for what happened next. He drew a blade like any other officer in that column would have drawn one, and matched his strength to what a strong young man's strength ought to look like, and took the fight the slow, contested, uncertain way a fight is supposed to go when no one in it can simply end it in a single motion. He told me he had done this so many times by then that the discipline of it had become close to automatic — and that the automatic nature of it was exactly what he blamed himself for afterward, because a fight fought the honest way takes honest time, and honest time, that particular afternoon, was the one thing Brenner didn't have enough of.

Brenner went down covering the column's flank, where a raider the General's own restrained fighting hadn't yet reached in time put a blade in low under his guard, and the wound that followed — deep across the hip and into the leg beneath it — was the kind the household physicians told the General plainly, afterward, that a man could survive and never fully recover from. Brenner lived. He kept the leg, though it never again carried him at a run the way it had carried him across that muster yard to reach a stranger's daughter years before, and he limped for the whole of the rest of his service, visibly and permanently, every day a small accounting of the seconds a real end to that fight would not have cost him.

The General told me he visited him twice a week through the whole of the recovery, the way he visited any wounded man in the household's service, and told no one, least of all Brenner himself, the actual shape of the debt he believed he owed him. He arranged, quietly and through channels no one could trace back to a single request, for a physician's continued care long past what the household's ordinary provision covered, and for a modest standing allowance that let Brenner's family want for nothing while the leg mended as far as it was ever going to mend — the same instrument, the General told me, he had used for Halyn's flooded bank house years before, though this time, he admitted to me, it felt less like a mercy freely given than like a payment made against a bill he could never fully settle.

He never told Draconis what he privately believed had caused it. Draconis, hearing the account of the ambush secondhand, praised Brenner's courage and the General's own steady conduct under attack, and neither man ever learned that the officer they were praising had held a power in reserve that whole afternoon capable of ending the raid before the first blade cleared a sheath. The General told me that was the one time in all these years he came closest to hating the discipline he'd built his whole life around — not because he doubted the choice was right, he said, but because for the first time the cost of it had a face he saw every week for the rest of that man's service, walking a little slower than he used to, and never once knowing why.
