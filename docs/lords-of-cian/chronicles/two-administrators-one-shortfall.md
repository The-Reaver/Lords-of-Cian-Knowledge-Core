# Two Administrators, One Shortfall

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1155`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXXI, wave 24. The method's first triangulated-fraud case: two rival mill owners sharing a single wage shortfall, each pointing at the other, neither permitted to escape by naming the second man guilty.*

---

Both owners arrived at the tent within an hour of each other, neither having planned it, both furious for what turned out to be the same reason. Their mills sat on opposite sides of a shared millrace, drew water and labor from the same seasonal pool of workers, and had — until three weeks earlier, when a worker's honest complaint reached the crew — each quietly assumed the other's books, not his own, were the ones with the shortfall in them.

Ezio's first pass at the combined production and payroll logs showed why neither man had noticed his own hand in it. The shortfall wasn't hidden in either mill's individual records — each mill's own book, read alone, looked close enough to clean that a cursory audit would have passed it. It only showed up when the two mills' shared worker roster was cross-referenced against both payrolls at once: workers who split shifts between the two mills in the same week were being paid a full day's wage by each employer for what amounted, once the actual hours were added together across both books, to nine and a half hours of combined work paid as if it were sixteen — a shortfall that existed only in the gap between the two ledgers, invisible to either owner looking solely at his own.

"It's not my shortfall," the first owner said, the moment Ezio laid it out. "My book's clean on its own terms."

"So is mine," said the second, arriving at the same defense independently.

"They're both clean on their own terms," Ezio said. "That's exactly the problem. Neither of you ever checked what the other was paying the same men for the same week." He wouldn't let either man narrow the finding to the other's fault, because the arithmetic itself refused to apportion it that way — the shortfall existed in the coordination gap, not in either mill's individual dishonesty, and both owners had benefited from workers quietly absorbing hours neither book accounted for.

Kanja split the remedy the same way the fraud had split itself: each mill paid half the combined shortfall, calculated jointly rather than separately, with a new standing requirement Ezio wrote into both books before the crew left — a shared weekly reconciliation between the two mills' payrolls for any worker splitting shifts between them, so the gap the two owners had each assumed belonged to the other could never again go unwatched by both at once.

Neither owner left satisfied. Both had come hoping to prove the other the guilty one, and both left having paid for a fraud that belonged, in the ledger's own accounting, to the space between them rather than to either man alone. It was, Ezio said afterward, the first finding he'd ever written where the fairest number wasn't the one either party wanted, and both had to accept it for exactly the same reason.
