# The Year He Stopped Asking Why

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1777`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XLVIII, third entry of the closing coming-of-age strand, set strictly pre-
Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. A purely internal turning point —
Ozmund's own gradual shift from resenting or questioning the inheritance he never chose (the Density
Spike and the Crown-Scar, `MCD-024`/`138`) to consciously, deliberately owning it — grounded in the
already-locked genealogical-copying custom implied by the Drakmund-to-Aethelgard lineage (`MCD-138`)
and in Val Mirel Kareth's Stone Count discipline (`MCD-1741`). Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari)
per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. No new named characters.*

---

Of everything the General ever told me, this is the story with the least in it that anyone watching
from outside would have called an event. No blade cleared a sheath. No water rose in the night. He
gave it to me almost apologetically, the way a man gives you the true answer to a question he suspects
you were hoping had a better one, and told me that if I wanted to understand him — really understand
him, past the campaigns and the Legion and everything that came after — this was closer to the center
of it than anything else he ever told me.

Every heir of House Verehimu copies the line by hand once, he said, some year of their youth that no
one fixes in advance — the whole of it, back past his father, past his father's father, back to
Drakmund, in the old script, on paper that will outlast the ink twice over. It is not taught as a
punishment and it is not taught as an honor either, particularly; it is simply understood to be a thing
an heir does, alone, at a desk, for as many evenings as it takes, and no one had ever explained to him
what it was supposed to accomplish beyond the plain fact of the record existing in his own hand as well
as everyone else's before him.

He told me he began that year already tired of a question he had been asking, in one form or another,
for longer than he could clearly remember asking it: why him. Not the self-pity of it — he was careful
to correct me when I first heard it that way — but the genuine, structural puzzlement of a boy who had
never had a single day of his life without the Spike sitting inside him, awake, waiting, asked for
nothing and given anyway, the way another boy might be given a name he didn't choose or a face he
didn't design. He had asked his father. He had asked his mother, once, badly, in a tone he told me he
still winced to remember. Neither of them had an answer that satisfied him, because there wasn't one to
give — the Spike was not a decision anyone had made about him, it was simply the shape the blood had
always taken, going back to a man five thousand years dead whose own reasons, if he'd ever had any,
were unrecoverable now by anyone.

What changed, he said, was not an answer arriving. It was the copying itself, some evening deep enough
into the work that his hand had stopped thinking about the letters and started simply making them,
when he came to a line in the old record — not Drakmund's founding, not anything the House made a
ceremony of, just a small, dry, administrative notation about a coin, a debt settled generations back
in a manner the scribe had thought worth a single sentence and nothing more — and understood, in the
plain unglamorous way understanding sometimes arrives, that every one of the names above his own had
also never chosen the thing they were given, and had spent their lives doing something with it anyway,
without waiting for the question of *why* to be settled first. The record did not care why. It only
recorded what was done.

He told me it was around then that he had begun sitting with the Stone Count in the evenings — the
discipline his mother had given him, counting each breath against a fixed stillness until the mind
either quieted or admitted, honestly, that it wasn't going to — and that he started to notice something
about the exercise he hadn't noticed while he was still treating it only as training. The Stone Count
never once answered why he had to sit there doing it. That had never been its purpose. Its purpose was
to let him hold an unanswered question without needing it resolved before he could act, and he realized,
somewhere in that stretch of evenings with the pen in one hand and the breath in the other, that this
was precisely what he had never let himself do with the larger question underneath all the smaller
ones. He had been waiting for permission to stop asking. No one was ever going to grant it. He would
have to simply stop.

He was careful to tell me it did not happen in a single evening, however cleanly the story wants to be
told that way now. It was a year, by his own reckoning — not a date he could point to, but a gradual
turning, the question arriving less often, mattering less each time it did, until one evening late in
that same year he realized he had gone some weeks without asking it at all, and had not particularly
missed asking it. What replaced it, he said, was smaller and far more useful: not *why was I given
this*, but *what does the work in front of me actually require*. He told me the second question had an
answer nearly every time he asked it. The first one never had, not once, in all the years he'd spent
demanding one.

He finished the copying that same year, his own name added at the bottom of the line in the same hand
that had struggled through the coin notation months before, steadier now, and told me he had felt, closing
the cover on it, not triumph and not peace exactly, but something quieter that had outlasted both —
the simple, settled sense of a man who has stopped waiting for his inheritance to explain itself and
started deciding, instead, what he was going to do with it.
