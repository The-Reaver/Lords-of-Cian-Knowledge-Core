# The Quiet Between Assignments

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1754`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XXV, closing the second Draconis-strand wave (Chronicles XXI-XXV). Set some time
after Chronicle XXIV, still strictly pre-ceremony — Aethelgard Verehimu alive, the Fulfillment
Ceremony (`MCD-025`) still in the future. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per
`VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. A deliberately still, unguarded entry — a rare full day off shared by Ozmund
and Colonel Viktor Draconis (`CC-085`) with no threat, no test, and no near-discovery, closing the
wave on ordinary companionship rather than another incident. No new named characters.*

Not every story the General told me about Draconis had a shape to it worth calling a story. This one,
he told me, was his favorite anyway, and he told it to me more than once, always the same way, always
ending it the same place: nowhere in particular, which was rather the point.

It happened on a day neither of them had asked for and neither of them, by the General's account, had
expected to get. A muster that should have run three days ran two instead, a formal visit from a
neighboring House's steward was postponed by weather no one had predicted, and Draconis found himself,
for the first time in longer than either of them could easily remember, with a full day standing empty
in front of him and no roster, no watch, and no one above him inclined to fill it. He didn't spend it
resting, the General told me, not exactly. He came to find the General instead, not as an officer
reporting to a duty but the way one man seeks out another he'd simply rather spend an idle day beside,
and asked him, with none of the careful formality he usually kept even in private, whether he had any
particular use for the hours either.

They rode out to the estate's lower lake before midday, the General said, with nothing more purposeful
in mind than the fishing lines Draconis kept, half-forgotten, in a chest he hadn't opened in years. He
told the General, settling in against the bank with the kind of unhurried patience the General said he
rarely saw the man allow himself anywhere else, that his father had taught him to fish on a river three
weeks south of anywhere that mattered, and that he'd never quite lost the habit even after decades of a
life that had no particular room left in it for rivers. He showed the General how to set a line the way
his father had shown him — badly at first, the General admitted, laughing about it even years later,
tangled twice before he managed a cast that didn't immediately foul itself on the reeds — and neither
of them caught anything worth naming, and neither of them, by the General's telling, minded that in the
slightest.

They talked about nothing that mattered, which the General said was its own kind of gift, coming from
a man who otherwise spoke to him almost exclusively about matters that did. Draconis told him about
Duskmere again, not the letter and the flood this time but the ordinary childhood underneath it — a
brother who'd once fallen through river ice and come up laughing before anyone had finished being
frightened for him, a mother who could gut a fish faster than any of her five children ever managed to
learn, a whole small unremarkable life lived long before any of it had anything to do with House
Verehimu at all. The General told me he found himself, listening, unexpectedly moved by how little of
it concerned him — that for one entire afternoon he was allowed to simply be a young man beside an
older one, hearing about a river and a mother and a brother who laughed too easily, with nothing in the
telling asking anything of him at all.

They ate whatever the kitchens had sent along without much ceremony, dozed a while in the afternoon
sun the way men do who've earned an idle hour and mean to spend the whole of it, and rode back only
once the light had properly started to go, neither of them in any particular hurry to be anywhere else.
The General told me that was the whole of the day, start to finish — no ambush, no order given or
refused, no moment either of them would ever have cause to recount to anyone else in the household as
anything worth recording. He said that was exactly why he'd kept it, all those years, more carefully
than half the stories that actually had something happen in them. It was the one day in all the years
he knew the man that asked nothing of either of them — not loyalty proven, not danger weathered, not
even the quiet unspoken weight the General carried into every other hour he ever spent near anyone. Just
a lake, a badly tied line, and a man who for one whole afternoon let himself be nothing more
complicated than someone glad of the company.

The General told me, closing the story the last time he told it to me, that he thought of that day more
often than any of the others, in the years that came after it mattered less and less to have days like
it at all. He said a man remembers the tests he passed and the dangers he survived because the world
insists he remember them. He remembers a lake and a bad cast and an idle afternoon because no one ever
asked him to.
