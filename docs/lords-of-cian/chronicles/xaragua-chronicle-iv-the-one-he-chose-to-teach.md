# Xaragua Chronicle IV: The One He Chose to Teach

*Locked canon, Batch 99, 2026-09-10 (`MCD-362`). Fourth Xaragua Chronicle, set after Xaragua
Chronicle II (`MCD-337`) in the "modern"
Arturo era -- chronologically the most recent of the four Xaragua Chronicles. Protagonist Arturo
Salvatierra Duho (`PH2-061`), with Naya, his protegee, dramatized directly for the first time --
`PH2-061` names her only in passing ("flagged for future payoff, not yet dramatized"). Introduces

Kanja appears only briefly and at the margins, granted no command, intervention, or resolution

Arturo found her the way he found most things worth keeping — by accident, in the wreckage of
something that should never have happened, six years old and sitting very still in the ash of a
tenement fire that had taken the rest of her family in the time it took the water carts to arrive.
He had not gone looking for a child that night. He had gone to see what could be salvaged for the
families left standing, the way he always did after a fire, and had found, instead, a girl who had
not cried once in the two hours since, because some part of her had already understood that crying
would not bring anyone back and had decided, with a clarity no six-year-old should have needed,
to save whatever she had left for something more useful.

He recognized that particular stillness. He had worn it himself, once, in a cellar a very long time
ago.

Sixteen years later, Naya sat at the far end of his table on the night two families' captains
arrived at his door at the same hour, both certain the other had crossed a line that could not be
uncrossed over a stretch of dock neither had held clean title to in three generations.

"I'm not hearing this one," Arturo said, before either man had finished sitting down. "Naya will
hear it. Whatever she decides holds the same weight as if I'd decided it myself."

The younger of the two captains looked at her the way men who had grown up on stories about the
head of this table often did — assessing, doubtful, already composing the version of this evening
he'd tell his own people afterward. Naya did not seem to notice, or did not care to show that she
had.

She let them both speak in full, twice each, without once interrupting either — the same patience
Arturo had spent sixteen years teaching her was worth more than any threat she could make, because a
man who has been fully heard has one less reason to lie about what he actually wants. She asked
questions that were not about the dock at all — who had first raised a hand, who had lost sleep over
it, who genuinely could not afford to lose the piece of it he was demanding — and by the end of an
hour had a shape of the dispute neither captain had bothered to articulate for himself.

"Neither of you actually wants that dock," she said, finally. "You want the other man to admit,
publicly, that he was wrong to raise a hand first. That's a thing I can give you both, and it costs
neither of you the dock." She laid out terms — an even split, a formal apology delivered at the next
full gathering of the Five, witnessed, on record — and watched both men's shoulders come down half an
inch, the specific relief of two men who had wanted a way out and had not known how to ask for one.

The stranger, who had been near the door for most of the evening the way he was for most evenings
now, watched the two captains leave satisfied with a dispute that had walked in ready to spill blood
over a stretch of dock worth less than the trouble it had already cost both families.

"You didn't need me tonight," Arturo said, once the room had emptied, not quite a question.

"I don't think I did. I think I needed you to trust that I didn't, which isn't the same thing." Naya
looked at the empty chairs, still faintly holding the shape of two men who had arrived certain of
violence and left with a handshake instead. "You could have heard that in twenty minutes. You let it
take an hour because you wanted to see what I'd ask, not just what I'd decide."

"I did." Arturo studied her for a long moment, the particular look of a man doing arithmetic only he
could see the terms of. "I found you the night you'd already decided, at six years old, that grief
was a thing to spend later rather than all at once. I've spent sixteen years since trying to teach
you the rest of what I learned the hard way, so you'd never have to learn it exactly the way I did."

"Is that why it took you so long to let me hear one of these on my own."

"It's exactly why. I wasn't testing whether you could hear a dispute fairly. I've known you could do
that for years." He looked at the door the two captains had gone through. "I was testing whether I
could stand in a room and let someone else's judgment hold the same weight as mine, for once, without
needing to be the one holding it. That part I wasn't sure of until tonight."

Naya said nothing to that for a while, the particular quiet of a person weighing a thing they had
not expected to be handed. "Yaisa's the only one who gets to banter with you unguarded. Everyone
says so."

"Yaisa remembers who I was before any of this. That's not a thing I can teach anyone, and it's not a
thing you've earned wrong by not having it — it's simply not yours to have, the same way it isn't
mine to give." Arturo almost, almost smiled, an expression that reached nowhere near as far as it
once might have. "But tonight's the first night I've trusted your judgment the way I trust my own.
That's not nothing either. That's the whole of what a table like this is actually for."
