# The Engineer Who Changed Sides

*Locked canon, Batch 197, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-716`). The Iron Bastard Alias Chronicle XXI, wave 7 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

He came alone, unarmed, under a strip of undyed cloth tied to a length of scrap pipe, and identified
himself only as one of the engineering corps that had spent three years designing countermeasures
against the doctrine and losing, in sequence, every one of them.

"I'm not here to surrender information for mercy," he said, before Kanja could ask. "I'm here because
I've spent three years studying how you defeat every structure my corps builds, and somewhere in the
second year I stopped being able to tell myself the work was worth the cost of the men who die testing
each new design in the field first."

"What do you want, if not mercy?"

"To keep building. Just not for them anymore." He set down a folded set of schematics, four alloy
variants and two insulation approaches, everything his corps had tried and everything they were still
developing. "You'll find countermeasures against yourself in here. Real ones. I'd rather you have them
early and adapt than watch more of my own people die finding out the hard way which ones almost
worked."

Kanja studied the schematics without touching them yet. "Your corps will call this treason."

"My corps already calls what they're doing 'refinement.' I call it three years of watching young
engineers get reassigned to the front lines to field-test theories, one broken design at a time, while
the men who approve the designs stay well behind them. I've made my decision about which side of that
arrangement I want to keep being part of."

The schematics proved useful — not decisive, but useful, confirming two directions the corps hadn't
yet fully committed to and buying weeks the doctrine might otherwise have spent relearning them under
fire. More than the schematics, though, was what the engineer's arrival itself represented: the first
crack, however small, in an institution that had spent three years treating the doctrine as a problem
to be solved rather than a man to be reckoned with.

"You understand you can't go back," Kanja said.

"I understood that the day I decided to come here. I'd rather build something I can live with than
keep perfecting something I can't."
