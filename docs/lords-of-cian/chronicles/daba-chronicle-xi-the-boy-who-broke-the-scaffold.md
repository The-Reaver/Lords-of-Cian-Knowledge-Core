# Daba Chronicle XI: The Boy Who Broke the Scaffold

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1581`). Entry 11 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,

contact between Daba and a young, not-yet-Rebellion-era Kanja, during the "otherwise-unrecorded
formative years" `MCD-1568` establishes -- a scene of raw, uninstructed instinct (`MCD-311`) seen

Riasa Vorn found him first, and it was three days before Daba believed a word of what she told him.

The Corrow cut was not worth a cell's attention on its own -- a Trust ore-works two ridges past the
nearest garrison town, forty-some laborers breaking a low seam of red iron under a quota schedule
nobody expected them to meet. Riasa had gone in to case the garrison's resupply route, nothing more,
carrying a laborer's papers and a laborer's silence. She was good at both. In four years with 1804
she had never once been the reason a watch got made.

What she reported, crouched in the scrub above the cut with her voice flattened the way Daba had
taught every one of them to flatten it, was this: the overseers had decided to make an example of a
gang that missed quota, the way overseers on every Trust concession eventually decided to make an
example of somebody, and a boy in that gang -- not a man, not yet, though he stood like the
difference wouldn't matter much longer -- had put three enforcers on the ground in under a minute
without touching a single one of them with his own hands.

"He read the scaffold," Riasa said, and Daba understood before she finished the sentence that this
was the part that had frightened her. Not the violence. The reading.

The ore-basket rig above the cut ran on green timber and borrowed rope, the kind of structure a Trust
concession threw up fast and never inspected twice. The boy had put his shoulder against a single
load-bearing post -- not hard, Riasa insisted, almost gently -- at the exact instant the lead enforcer
stepped beneath the swing arm to deliver the first blow. The whole rig had come down where he needed
it to come down and nowhere else. One enforcer buried to the chest in timber and ore-sacking. A
second caught a falling crossbeam across the shins that Riasa swore had been aimed, though nothing
about the collapse looked aimed to anyone who hadn't watched a Rexmar work a structure before. The
third had simply run. The boy hadn't chased him.

Daba went himself the following night.

He did not go to introduce himself. He went the way he approached every unclaimed variable that could
get a cell burned: from the dark, unannounced, prepared to leave the way he'd come if what he found
didn't warrant contact. He found the boy alone at the ruin of the scaffold, working a length of the
broken crossbeam over his knee for no reason Daba could immediately name -- not salvage, not
sabotage, something closer to a man running a hand along a wound to learn its shape.

"You could have killed them," Daba said, from the treeline, not moving closer.

The boy didn't startle. That, more than the scaffold, was the thing Daba filed away first. "I could
have," he agreed. "I didn't want to."

"Why not?"

"Because then it's a killing, and everyone who comes after has to decide what to do about a killing.
This way it's just an accident nobody can prove." He set the crossbeam down. "You've been watching
since before I finished."

It wasn't a question, and Daba didn't treat it as one. He stayed at the treeline. Two men who have
each just confirmed the other is dangerous rarely close the distance on a first meeting, and Daba had
survived twenty years of exactly this instinct by never being the one who closed it first.

"Who taught you that," Daba asked instead, meaning the scaffold, meaning the read, meaning the
particular economy of a boy who could have ended three lives and chose instead to end three careers.

"Nobody," the boy said, and something in his voice made clear he'd been asked some version of this
question before, by people who hadn't believed the answer either. "It's just how the thing looks to
me. Weight goes where it wants to go. I just help it get there early."

Daba said nothing for a long moment. He had run cells through burned villages and watched grown
fighters freeze at half of what this boy had done without apparent effort, without apparent
training, without, as far as Daba could see, any awareness that what he'd just done was worth being
afraid of.

"I don't know what you are," Daba said finally. "I intend to find out before I decide whether that's
a danger to people I answer for."

"Fair," the boy said, and something that might, in a few more years, have become a smile crossed his
face and didn't stay. "I don't know what you are either."

Daba left him there, with the broken scaffold and the piece of crossbeam he'd gone back to turning
over in his hands, and did not sleep well that night, running the numbers on a variable he could not
yet decide whether to fear or recruit.
