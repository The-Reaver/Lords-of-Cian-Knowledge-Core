# The Night the Feed Barn Burned

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1820`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCI, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2 — first entry of the second wave, bringing the
strand from five entries to twenty. Set strictly pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's early-to-mid adolescence
(roughly age thirteen), years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund
still the House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed
from three separately-taken accounts cross-checked against each other: Armsmaster Berrin Hollis
(Chronicle XLI), Sergeant Oswin Kade (Chronicle XLV), and a fragment Ozmund himself once volunteered
about the stable boy Cobb (Chronicle XLIV). The household's first genuine emergency in the strand — a
real fire, real risk, real competence under pressure — rather than training-yard or household
instruction. No new named characters.*

---

The fire started in the lower feed barn a little past midnight, in high summer, in the dry weeks
before the first cutting, and Hollis told me afterward that he'd never in his life been more grateful
for a hip that woke him with pain most nights anyway, because it meant he smelled the smoke before the
bell did.

By the time the bell did ring, the barn was already lost — that much every account agreed on. What
mattered, Kade told me, wasn't the barn. It was the stable block adjoining it, built close enough that
a strong wind that night would have carried the fire straight into stalls holding better than thirty
horses, several of them young and not yet steady enough to be led calmly out of a burning building by
strangers shouting in the dark.

Hollis had the bucket lines organized before the House Guard proper had finished turning out — old
instinct, he said, the same instinct that ran a drill yard applied without thinking to a crisis, because
a line of men passing water in rhythm wasn't so different from a line of men passing a blade drill down
the ranks. Kade arrived to find him already shouting positions and simply folded his own guardsmen into
Hollis's structure rather than build a competing one, which Kade said was the single best decision either
of them made that night, though neither man, he was careful to tell me, ever much cared to admit the
other had been right about anything.

The General was thirteen that summer, old enough to be woken by the bell same as anyone and quick
enough to be at the stable block before most of the adult household had finished dressing. Hollis said
he arrived to find the boy already inside the threatened stalls, not fighting the fire — the fire wasn't
his to fight, and everyone standing close enough to see his face that night agreed later that he understood
that without being told — but working the horses out one at a time with a calm that unsettled Hollis more
than panic would have, because panic he knew how to manage in a boy that age, and this wasn't panic.

What none of them knew, and what I only understood myself once I'd heard the account whole, was that
the boy had spent the first several minutes of that fire consciously and deliberately doing nothing at
all with the one thing that might have ended it outright — a Density Spike could have collapsed the
threatened section of roof cleanly away from the stalls in less time than it took Hollis to organize a
second bucket line, and the General told me, decades later, that the temptation had been the worst of
his young life precisely because it would have looked, to everyone watching, like nothing more than a
lucky beam giving way at the right moment. He said he'd stood in that stable yard doing arithmetic no
thirteen-year-old should have had to do — how many horses might be saved against how many questions
might never again go unasked — and chosen the slower, harder, entirely honest way of pulling them out
by hand, one at a time, the way anyone else's son would have had to.

It was Cobb, Hollis said, who was already in the worst stall when the General reached it — the same
gelding, grown older now and long past the shaking hands of its breaking, but panicked enough by smoke
to be dangerous to anyone it didn't already trust. Cobb had it haltered and half out the door before
anyone else arrived, hands shaking exactly the way the General had once told me they always shook, going
in anyway because the horse needed getting out and there was no one else positioned to do it. The two of
them worked the last three stalls together without a word passed between them that either could later
recall — Cobb reading the animals, the General reading Cobb, neither one needing instruction from the
other.

The barn itself was a total loss by dawn. Not one horse was. Hollis said that was the number that mattered,
the only number that mattered, and that he'd spent thirty years training men for exactly one night like
that one without ever being entirely sure any of them would rise to it until they actually did. Kade told
me the House rebuilt the barn within the season and never once, in his hearing, made a ceremony of what
had been saved instead of what had burned — which he said was itself the correct instinct, the same
instinct that ran everything else about how that House treated the people who served it. I asked Hollis,
near the end, whether he'd ever wondered how the boy had moved so fast, so calmly, through a burning
building at thirteen. He said he'd wondered exactly once, that night, and then decided it was none of his
business, because a boy who could do that and never once made anyone feel unsafe standing near him wasn't
a mystery he needed solved. He was just, as far as Hollis was concerned, the best hand in that stable
block that night, and there'd been no shortage of grown men to compare him against.
