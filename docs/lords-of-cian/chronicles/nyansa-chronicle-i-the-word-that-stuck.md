# Nyansa Chronicle I: The Word That Stuck

*Locked canon, Batch 91, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-354`). First entry in Nyansa's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Adisa (`PH2-057`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`) through Hekalu Chronicle I (`MCD-353`). Narration is
close-third on Adisa throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Continues Detroit's own run of territory
Chronicles. Slots into no existing mainline battle — original homage-era material set in Nyansa
itself.*

---

Adisa had sat in enough movement meetings by then to know the particular silence that fell over a
room when someone said a true thing everyone in it had already half-known and none of them had
wanted spoken aloud. He'd learned to recognize the silence's shape before he ever understood what
his own voice did once it broke that shape open.

The meeting that evening had been arguing, for the third week running, over whether the district's
organizing committee should keep excluding the plant's night-shift workers from its votes — a
practical decision, everyone insisted, made for reasons of scheduling and quorum, nothing more than
that, said the same way every time the question came up and was set aside again unresolved.

"You've built a committee that claims to speak for every worker in this district," Adisa said,
"and you've built its own meeting schedule around the one shift that guarantees the workers with the
least power in this district never get to vote on anything that governs them. You've been calling
that a scheduling problem for three weeks. I don't think it's a scheduling problem. I think it's
who this committee was actually built to serve, and I think most of you already knew that before I
said it, which is exactly why nobody's fixed it yet."

The room did the silence he'd learned to recognize — not offended exactly, worse than offended,
caught. The stranger, sitting near the back where he'd sat for most of the meeting without drawing
particular notice, watched three separate faces in that silence do the same thing at slightly
different speeds: the specific, visible work of a thought arriving that couldn't be unarrived once
it had landed.

They didn't fix it that night. Adisa hadn't expected them to; a true thing spoken aloud didn't
rearrange a meeting's schedule by itself. But the argument that had run unresolved for three weeks
never once returned to its old shape after that evening — every subsequent version of it started
from the fact Adisa had named, rather than circling around it the way it always had before, and
within the month the night shift had its vote.

"That's a real thing you did," the stranger said afterward, once the room had emptied. "I've watched
you do it twice now. Say the thing everyone already half-knew, and it stays said."

"It only stays said because it was already there before I opened my mouth." Adisa gathered his
papers, unhurried. "I didn't teach that room anything it didn't already carry. I only made it
impossible to keep carrying it quietly."

He tried it again three days later, in a very different room — a closed meeting with a rival
faction's leader, a man whose whole political program Adisa considered built on a contradiction he
was certain the man himself had never once examined. He said it plainly, the same way he'd said it
to his own committee: named the gap between the man's stated principles and the alliance he'd
quietly cut with people those principles should have made impossible to sit across from.

Nothing happened. The man heard him out, disagreed on every point, and walked out of the room
exactly as certain of his own program as he'd walked in.

"It didn't land," the stranger observed, once the rival had gone.

"It didn't land because there was nothing underneath it to land on." Adisa said it without
frustration, more the tone of a man confirming something he'd already half-suspected and had simply
never had cause to test directly before. "My own committee already half-knew what I told them. They
just hadn't let themselves finish knowing it. That man doesn't half-know what I told him. He's built
his whole program on not knowing it, all the way down, and I can't plant a belief in ground that was
never already carrying it. I can only say out loud what's already there, waiting."

"So the gift only works on your own side."

"The gift only works on people already partway to the truth, whoever they happen to stand with. That's
not the same thing as only working on my own side, though I understand why it looks that way from the
outside." Adisa looked out at the empty room, still faintly holding the shape of the silence from
three nights before. "It's a narrower tool than people assume when they first hear what it does. I've
made my peace with the narrowness. A tool that could implant any belief in anyone wouldn't be
correction. It'd just be another kind of control, dressed up as truth."

The stranger was gone before the next meeting convened, the way he always seemed to be. He left
nothing carved, no phrase repeated hand to hand — only, the following week, one more chair added to
the back row, where the night-shift workers now sat for the first time, casting the first votes
anyone in that room could remember them ever being allowed to cast.
