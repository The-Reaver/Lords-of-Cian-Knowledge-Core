# The Order He Refused

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1752`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XXIII. Set in the same general pre-ceremony span as Chronicles XXI-XXII, still
strictly before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu alive. Narrated by Red
Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. Dramatizes Colonel Viktor Draconis (`CC-085`)
refusing a dishonorable order from a superior within the House Guard's own chain of command — not

to shield him from the fallout without ever revealing his own hand, extending the "help at human

Of the stories the General told me about Draconis, this is the one he told me with the most obvious
satisfaction, and the only one, I think, where he let himself feel something close to pride in his
own part of it rather than simply guilt at the part he could never disclose.

It began with grain, the way so much trouble on Verehimu lands eventually did — a season's Scrip
rate raised on the eastern holdings without warning, steep enough that a dozen tenant families
petitioned the household in the ordinary way, through the ordinary channels, and were told, in the
ordinary way, to wait for a review that hadn't yet been scheduled. The petition might have ended
there, the General said, the way most of them did, if a second season's rate hadn't come down on top
of the first before the first had even been answered, and if the families hadn't finally done what
frightened, patient people do when waiting stops looking like patience and starts looking like
neglect: they gathered, peaceably, at the edge of the eastern grain-yard, and refused to move until
someone above a steward's clerk came to hear them.

It reached Guard-Marshal Fenwick before it ever reached Aethelgard. Fenwick held the House Guard's
outer command by then — levies, gate posts, the ordinary policing of Verehimu lands, a real and
legitimate authority that Draconis's own inner escort sat formally beneath even by that point in his
career — and Fenwick's answer to a dozen tenant families standing quietly in a grain-yard was to order
a full company under Draconis's own command out to disperse them before nightfall, by whatever force
the crowd's stubbornness required. He framed it, the General told me, as a matter of the House's
authority needing to be seen and not questioned, and he framed it, further, as an order given in
Aethelgard's name, though he had not in fact sought Aethelgard's word on it at all.

Draconis didn't march. The General told me he heard the shape of the argument secondhand, the way he
heard most of what happened between officers, but he'd never forgotten Draconis's own account of it,
given plainly, years later: that he had stood in front of Fenwick and told him he would not send armed
men against families who had broken no law and drawn no weapon, whatever authority the order claimed
to carry, and that if the order was truly Aethelgard's own, Aethelgard could give it to him directly
and he would obey it as he had obeyed every order that man had ever given him. Fenwick had no answer
for that beyond his own rank, which was real, and which he used — Draconis was relieved of the eastern
posting that same night, and told, in language that left no honest doubt of the intent behind it, that
a formal review of his conduct would follow within the week, the kind of review that ended careers
more often than it repaired them.

The General learned of it the next morning, from a steward too worried to keep the matter fully to
himself, and told me he understood immediately what was actually at stake — not the grain-yard, which
Fenwick's own men, less patient than Draconis, dispersed roughly enough two days later to leave three
tenants with broken bones and no rate review any closer than before, but Draconis's whole career,
built across decades on exactly the kind of loyalty that had just refused an order for the first time
anyone in that household could remember.

He didn't confront Fenwick. He didn't go to his father and ask him, directly, to intervene on a
colonel's behalf, which would have named a favor Aethelgard might one day have cause to remember and
call in against him. What he did instead, the General told me, was mention to his father, over some
unrelated matter entirely, that he'd heard troubling things about how the eastern petition had
actually been handled — the raised rate stacked on an unanswered rate, the review that was never
scheduled, the order given in Aethelgard's name without Aethelgard's word — and let his father's own
sense of what was owed his tenants do the rest. Aethelgard opened his own inquiry within the day, and
it found, as the General had known it would find, that Fenwick had exceeded an authority he did not
have and invoked a name he had no right to invoke. The formal review of Draconis's conduct never
happened. Fenwick's own did.

Draconis was never told whose word had started that inquiry, and never asked, the General said,
because by the time it concluded he had other things to occupy him and no particular reason to
wonder. The General told me he'd wanted, more than once in the years after, to let him know — not for
thanks, which Draconis would have refused as thoroughly as he'd refused the order itself, but simply
so the man would understand he hadn't stood alone in that grain-yard the way it must have felt he had.
He never did tell him. He said the victory Draconis had actually won that day belonged entirely to
Draconis, earned with nothing but his own refusal to march, and that adding his own quiet hand to the
account of it would have taken something from a man who'd never once asked to have anything given to
him that he hadn't earned outright.
