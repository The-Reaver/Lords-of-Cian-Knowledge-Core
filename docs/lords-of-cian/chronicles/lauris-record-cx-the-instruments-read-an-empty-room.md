*Archive fragment, present-day: "Cairnholt. Thirty-eight held. Thirty-seven walked out. One ran from
me as well, and I let her. One man is dead. His instruments recorded the whole of it. They recorded
no one."*

I have the Cairnholt Intake's instrument rolls on the table in front of me as I write. Lauris carried
them out under one arm, and she has not asked for them back. I will set down what they say first,
because they are the most exact witnesses to that night, and because what they say is the reason
the night is now spoken of in rooms neither of us will ever enter.

The Cairnholt Intake is a holding operation of the smaller kind: a quarry-cut stone block on a
hillside, three levels below the gate, one corridor to each level, forty cells on the lowest. It
was split off from a Directorate regional containment office some generations ago and has run since
then on the protocols it inherited, in the manner of such places. Its walls carry density
instruments built to the old pattern, set every thirty paces along each corridor, each one scribing
a continuous line onto a paper roll. They were tuned, by men long dead, to register one signature in
particular. Hers.

Its transfer manifest carried two columns. The first listed subjects the receiving facility had
accepted. The second was headed *non-viable*. Beside the second column, in the warden's own hand,
was the inherited instruction for subjects that could not be moved: seal the lowest level at the
lock, open the cistern sluice, and record the cells as lost to water. Thirty-eight designations stood
in that column. CP-609 was the twelfth.

She arrived on the night the transfer wagons were due, four hours before them.

The gate roll records her first. At the outer yard, at the sixth hour past dusk, the needle climbs
hard and holds high: one Karesian signature, strong and rising. The yard guard was eleven men. She
did not kill any of them. Attia's Rite is a discipline built for many soft targets at once, and the
guard's own accounts describe what that is like from inside it: a woman who was never where the
strike went, a sequence of joints and balances failing in an order none of them could follow, and
then the cobbles. Seven minutes. Every man of the eleven lived. The roll for those seven minutes is a
dense, rising line. Saturation is entered through sustained effort, and she spent those seven
minutes entering it.

The corridor rolls begin at the inner gate.

First level, first instrument: the line jumps, stutters, and falls to a flat tremor. The clerk's
margin note reads *calibration fault*. Second instrument, thirty paces on: a faint ripple, then
nothing. *Calibration fault.* Third instrument: a single flat line. No note at all.

The guards on the first level saw her. Their statements, taken later by the operation that absorbed
what was left of Cairnholt, agree on her pace, which was a walk, and on her face, which none of them
described as angry. Two of them used the same word for it, independently. Pleased. One of them
raised a pike and set it down again without a strike, because, as he put it, the instrument behind
him said the corridor was empty and he could no longer tell which of the two of them was wrong.

Second level: four instruments, four flat lines. Third level: six instruments. Six flat lines.

I will be precise about this, because it is the whole of what the legend rests on. For eleven
minutes, a corridor system built and tuned by the Directorate's own methods to find exactly one kind
of body recorded that it held no body at all, while that body walked the full length of it, and the
men standing in it watched her pass.

The warden was not watching his instruments. He was warned by the noise from the upper levels, and
he went where the protocol told him to go. The lock at the foot of the third level carries an iron
sealing wheel, waist high, eight spokes, which closes the lower door and opens the cistern sluice
on a single shaft. Ilmar Hesketh was at that wheel when she reached the foot of the stair. He had
already turned it two spokes. The roll for the lowest corridor shows nothing. The cistern gauge
beside the wheel shows water entering the lowest cells at the rate of a hand's depth each minute.

She said one word to him. "Stop."

He turned the wheel a third spoke.

What follows, I have from her, from the gauge, and from the body, and I set it down in that order.

She closed six paces. The Aristocrat is a precision strike, the discipline Voreth Karth-Ven spent
seven hundred years teaching her: one blow placed at the exact point where a structure stops being a
structure. On a man, that point is not large. Her right hand entered below the left collarbone at a
downward angle. The first rib failed. The upper lobe of the left lung failed with it. The force
travelled on through the great vessels above the heart, which tore at their root. Hesketh's hands
came off the wheel in the same instant. He was dead before his knees reached the stone. The wheel
stopped at three spokes. She turned it back by hand, all three, and the water
stopped at the depth of a child's shin.

The surgeon who examined him for the absorbing operation wrote one line about the wound: *a single
blow, with no second contact anywhere on the body.*

She opened the forty cells. Thirty-eight were occupied. Thirty-seven of those held came up the stairs
in a line, past guards who did not raise anything, and out through the yard where the eleven were
still sitting on the cobbles.

CP-609 came out last. Lauris has given me her own account of this, and I give it as she gave it. The
subject was younger than CP-414 had been, by the old intake measure. She stood in the doorway of the
twelfth cell and looked at Lauris for some time, and Lauris let her look. Lauris told her that a
place existed where she would be kept safe. CP-609 asked whether she would be held there. Lauris told
her the truth, which is that she would be protected there, and that protection and holding are often
indistinguishable from the inside. CP-609 thanked her, walked past her up the stair, and ran.

Lauris did not follow. She told me she stood at the foot of the stair until the sound of the running
was gone, and that for the first time since Operation 29 the word CP-414 had given her had been used
the way it was meant.

She took two things from the warden's office before she left: the instrument rolls, and Hesketh's
transfer ledger. The ledger covers nineteen years. It records six earlier transfers out of Cairnholt,
each with a non-viable column, and each column closed with the same three words, *lost to water*. It
also records four receiving facilities by number, three of which do not appear in any registry Vael
Korr-Drennen has yet searched. CP-414 told her she would not be the last. The ledger agrees, and it
adds addresses.

The legend did not come from her. She has never spoken of Cairnholt outside this room. It came from
the eleven men in the yard and the guards in the corridors, who carried out one detail intact and
repeated it to anyone who would buy them a drink: the Intake's instruments, built to find her kind,
read an empty room for the whole of it while the warden died at his own wheel. Within a season the
phrase had reached the file of an institution that already classifies her Reclassified -- Hostile. It
sits there now, in a clerk's hand, under her designation. *The instruments read an empty room.*

I asked her, when she had finished, how the night had been for her. She considered the question for
longer than I expected.

"The walk was very good," she said. "The rest is in the ledger."
