# What He Practiced in the Dark

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1778`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XLIX, fourth entry of the closing coming-of-age strand, set strictly pre-
Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. The most interior, least-witnessed
entry in the series to date — a wholly private, self-directed discipline in which young Ozmund tests
not the ceiling of his Density Spike but its floor, alone, at night, at the already-locked flooded
quarry near Aldenmoor (`MCD-1747`), extending the "no threshold to cross" psychology (`CC-015`/
`017`/`MCD-024`) into the one register where he alone gets to set the terms. Narrated by Red Beard
(Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, explicitly flagged within the text as the thinnest account
he was ever given, reconstructed from a single, brief, unrepeated mention. No new named characters.*

---

There is a kind of story a man tells you exactly once, in passing, and never returns to no matter how
many times the conversation circles back near it afterward, and you learn, if you know him long enough,
not to ask twice. This is that kind of story. I have less of it, in the General's own words, than I have
of almost anything else in this account, and what I am giving you now is mostly my own understanding of
what such nights must have been, built around the handful of sentences he actually gave me.

What he told me was this: that there was an old flooded quarry on the low ground below Aldenmoor, long
since abandoned to still black water and a rim of broken stone, and that in certain years of his youth
he went there alone, at night, without telling anyone — not Osric, not Draconis once Draconis was in
his service, not even his father — and stayed for hours doing something he never called training and
never called anything else either, because he had never needed a name for it beyond the doing.

Everyone who ever writes or sings of what he is writes about the ceiling — the weight he can bring to
bear in a single motion, the density with no measured limit anyone has ever found, the thing that makes
grown soldiers go quiet when they finally understand what they're looking at. The General told me,
that one time, that the ceiling had never once been his to discover. It was there before he had words
for it, fixed by blood he didn't choose, terrifying in the exact proportion that it was inherited rather
than earned — and a man cannot practice his way into owning a thing that was simply always true of him.
What he could practice, he said, was the other end of it entirely: how little of it he could let through
and still call it engaged at all. The floor, not the ceiling. The one part of the whole inheritance that
was actually, genuinely his to define.

I believe — and this is mine now, not his, offered honestly as such — that he went to that quarry to
stand on the black ice at its edge in the coldest months and hold himself at the exact threshold where
the faintest instant of density would crack it and the faintest instant less would not, over and over,
learning the width of that threshold by feel until he could find it in the dark without looking down. I
believe he practiced holding something as small and as easily ruined as a moth, or an egg taken from
one of the kitchen stores and never missed, cupped in both hands at a density so close to none at all
that the difference existed only to him, until he trusted that hands capable of ending a man in a single
motion could also, with total reliability, do nothing at all to a thing that could not survive even a
mistake. I believe, because it is the only shape that fits everything else I know about him, that he
did this not for any battle he expected to fight and not for anyone who would ever see it and be
impressed, but because a boy who has never once, in his whole remembered life, crossed a threshold into
becoming what he is, needs some other way to know the shape of himself from the inside — and the only
tool he had for that was control itself, tested at its gentlest possible edge, where no one was ever
going to get hurt no matter how badly he judged it.

He told me — and this part I have exactly, word for word, because it was the sentence that ended the
subject and I have never forgotten it — that he never told his father where he went on those nights, and
that Aethelgard, to his knowledge, never asked. I have wondered since whether that silence was its own
kind of understanding between them, a father who suspected something and judged it wiser to let his son
have one door that opened onto nothing but himself, or whether it was simply a thing that passed
beneath Aethelgard's notice entirely, one small absence among a boy's many small absences. The General
did not offer which, and I did not ask. Some rooms, even in a story a man gives you freely, are meant
to stay exactly as private as he built them to be, and I have tried, in the telling, to leave this one
nearly as dark as I found it.
