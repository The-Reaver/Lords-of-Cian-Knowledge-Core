# The First Sheet Ever Printed

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle XXXIII, wave 11.
Rebellion era, age 18, before the Black Trench. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The crier who worked the Marrow Landing market square had been reading news aloud from hand-copied
sheets for longer than the Trench Monarch's reputation had existed, and it was her decision, made on
her own initiative and without asking anyone's permission, that turned the five-district agreement
into the first printed sheet the canal district had ever seen dedicated to a single living person.

She hadn't asked Kanja's permission because she hadn't thought to. The sheet went up the same way any
market notice went up — hand-lettered, copied a dozen times by two apprentices working through the
night, distributed to every crier within reach of the canal by morning.

*FIVE DISTRICTS, ONE STANDARD*, it read, in lettering large enough to draw a crowd on its own. *The
figures that ended a broken handshake, verified by independent hand. Read here what the man they call
a king does not deny and does not exaggerate: the numbers, and nothing more.*

Kanja found out about it the way most of the canal did — secondhand, from a dredger who'd heard it
read aloud that morning and repeated the gist of it half-remembered.

"You should see it," Callum Breck said, when Kanja finally tracked down one of the surviving copies.
"It doesn't even sound like a story. It just reprints the numbers, the same way you'd read them
aloud yourself."

It didn't sound like a story, and that was, Kanja realized reading it, exactly what unsettled him
about it — not the crier's choice to print it, which he understood and didn't resent, but the plain
fact that a piece of paper, copied a dozen times and carried by criers who'd never met him, now
existed independent of anything he did next. The name had traveled by rumor before. This was
different. This would still be readable, unaltered, long after any single conversation about him had
been forgotten or misremembered.

He went to find the crier himself, not to object, but because the sheet raised a question he hadn't
had to answer before.

"You didn't ask before printing this," he said.

"Should I have?" She didn't seem worried by the question. "The figures are public. Anyone could have
verified them the same way I did before I set the type."

"No — you shouldn't have needed to ask. That's not what I came to say." He considered the sheet again,
still slightly unsettled by its permanence. "I came to ask you to print the next correction the same
way, whenever there is one. If a wrong number of mine is ever going to survive on paper the way this
one has, I'd rather the correction survive just as plainly, printed by the same hand, so the two
travel together instead of one outliving the other."

She agreed. It was the beginning, though neither of them understood it fully that morning, of a
habit that would follow every alias Kanja ever carried — the printed record, and its correction, kept
in the same honest hand.
