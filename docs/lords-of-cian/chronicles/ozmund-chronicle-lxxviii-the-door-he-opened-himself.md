# The Door He Opened Himself

The General told me this one plainly, without much ornament, and I understood, listening to him, that
he wasn't giving it to me as a story about danger or triumph. He was giving it to me as a story about
arriving somewhere his mother had been standing all along, and finding, once he got there, that she'd
left the ground exactly as steady as she'd promised it would be.

A landslide had taken out a stretch of the eastern road that spring, following weeks of rain heavy
enough that the House had half expected some part of the hill country to give eventually. It came down
in the night, across a stretch where two separate work-camps — road menders on one side, a smaller
surveying party on the other — had been sheltering against the weather, and by the time word reached
the House and Ozmund himself reached the site at first light, both camps were cut off from rescue by
ground too unstable to cross safely with more than a handful of men at once.

He told me he stood at the top of that unstable ground for longer than anyone watching him understood
the reason for, and that what he was doing, the whole of that stillness, was the Narrow Door — not
performed, not recited to himself the way a man recites a lesson he's trying to remember correctly, but
simply present in him the way his mother had promised, seventeen years before on a flooded riverbank,
that a discipline properly learned eventually stopped being something you did and became something you
simply were.

The road menders' camp sat on firmer ground, reachable with reasonable risk. The surveyors' camp sat
directly beneath a slope that was, by every reading he could make of it, still settling — stable enough
to hold for now, unstable enough that a rescue party working it too long risked bringing the rest of it
down on both rescuers and rescued alike. He told me the House Guard captain on site that morning wanted
to split their limited rope and manpower between both camps at once, the same instinct the captain on
that riverbank had once had, the same fairness that felt, on its surface, like the kinder choice.

He did not split it. He sent every rope, every hand he had, to the surveyors' camp first — the harder
ground, the shorter window, the one that could not wait — and held the road menders, on firmer footing,
for the second crossing once the slope had either settled further or given entirely. He told me he
made the decision in less time than it took to describe it to the captain, and that some part of him,
making it, understood precisely what it would cost if he was wrong, and made it anyway, because his
mother had taught him, seventeen years before, that a door which opens halfway for everyone opens all
the way for no one.

He was not wrong. The surveyors came off that slope whole, every one of them, in the hour before the
rest of the hillside finally gave way behind them — ground that would have taken any rescuer still
working it when it went. The road menders, held back on their firmer footing, lost nothing but time.

What he told me afterward, once the site was cleared and the count of the living had come in whole on
both sides, was that he had felt, standing at the top of that unstable ground making the call, his
mother's absence more sharply than he had in years — not because he doubted the decision, but because
some part of him had wanted, badly, to tell her he'd made it the way she taught him, and there had been
no one there to tell. He wrote to her that week, one of the few letters he ever sent her that he told
me the content of directly, describing the slide, the choice, the outcome, ending it with a single line
he gave me exactly as he'd written it: *I opened the door the way you showed me. It held.*

Her answer, when it came, was shorter than his letter had been, and he kept it, he told me, for the
rest of his life. It said only that she had never once doubted it would, and that the discipline had
stopped being hers to be proud of the moment he stopped needing her standing beside him to use it.

"I understood, reading that," he told me, years later, "that some lessons are only finished the day you
teach them to no one watching but yourself, and find they were already yours all along."
