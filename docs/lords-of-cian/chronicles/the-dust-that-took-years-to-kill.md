# The Dust That Took Years to Kill

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LII, wave 18. Rebellion era, during the active Sewer War of Killane (MCD-234, age 20). Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

Nobody had died that week, which was why it took Kanja three days to notice the old dry-gallery crew
had a problem no single collapse or ambush would ever announce.

He noticed it in a cough — the same cough, in four different men who'd worked the same unventilated
stretch of dry excavation for going on two decades, long before this war or this siege had given any
of it a name worth reporting.

"How long's the gray-bearded one been coughing like that?" he asked the section foreman, watching the
oldest of the four work a full shift without once catching a full breath between swings.

"Since before I started, and I've been here eleven years." The foreman didn't look especially
concerned; this was, to him, simply what the trade eventually did to a man's chest. "Dry stone work.
Cuts fine, no water to settle it, so it hangs in the air the whole shift and you breathe what you cut.
Always has. Nobody's found a way around it."

Kanja spent the next two days watching the dust itself rather than the men working through it —
tracing where it hung thickest, where the air actually moved and where it only seemed to, testing
airflow with nothing more than a held breath and a cloth soaked in water at a dozen points along the
gallery.

"There's no single moment where this kills anyone," he told the foreman on the second evening,
laying out what he'd found. "That's why nobody's ever fixed it. A collapse, you can point at. A flood,
you can point at. This just takes twenty years off a man's chest one shift at a time until his lungs
give out on an otherwise ordinary morning, and by then nobody remembers to blame the dust for it."

"So what do we do? Stop cutting dry stone? Half this network doesn't have water anywhere near it."

"We move the air instead of moving the men." Kanja had already sketched it — a series of forced
draft channels, cut at specific points where his testing showed the current would actually clear the
working face rather than just stir the dust deeper into the gallery, paired with wet cloths worn over
the mouth during the heaviest cutting. "It won't undo what twenty years already did to a man like the
one coughing over there. I can't fix that. But it changes what the next twenty years does to whoever's
cutting this stone after him."

It took nine days to cut the draft channels through sections that had never needed structural work of
their own, time the campaign could barely spare, and Kanja spent four of those nine days doing the
cutting personally rather than pull hands off the active siege lines. The gray-bearded cutter worked
through every one of them, unconvinced any of it would matter to a chest already ruined, and said so
plainly when the channels were finished.

"Won't help me. Twenty years is twenty years, and no draft's going to give me these lungs back."

"No. It won't." Kanja didn't offer him a comfort that wasn't true. "It'll help the eighteen-year-old
who started dry-cutting this month, though, and he'll never know your chest was the reason his doesn't
sound like yours does at your age. I couldn't fix what already happened to you. I could at least make
sure it stopped happening to the next one."

The old cutter worked another six weeks before the campaign moved him to lighter duty, coughing no
less than before — but the eighteen-year-old kept working the draft-cleared gallery for the rest of
the siege, and never developed the cough at all.
