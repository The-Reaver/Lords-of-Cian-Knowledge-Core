# The Ones He's Teaching Now

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1784`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series, Chronicle LV, Draconis strand wave 3.*

---

The General told me that Draconis never once spoke of Brenner's wound as a tragedy, and it took him a long while, hearing him talk about it years afterward, to understand that this wasn't hardness. It was, he came to believe, the same instinct that had pulled Brenner's name off a cut list in the first place, turned now toward a different use.

Brenner didn't leave the House Guard's service, though the leg that never fully healed put an end to any hope of the field postings a younger version of him might have chased. What Draconis did instead, once the worst of the recovery had passed and it was plain the limp was permanent, was give him the autumn musters — the same muddy yard, the General told me, where Draconis had once stood judging farm boys and second sons on instinct rather than polish, handed now to the one man in the household who'd lived, in his own body, the exact lesson that yard existed to teach. Brenner took to it, by the General's account, the way a man takes to a thing he didn't know he'd been training for his whole career without meaning to.

The General watched one full muster season from the same distance he'd learned, over the years, to keep from Draconis's own private business — close enough to see, far enough not to be seen seeing it. He told me it was strange, at first, watching a scene that so plainly echoed one he'd heard about secondhand years before play out now in front of him with different men standing in the old positions. A recruit came late to the yard that season, ill-outfitted and short-tempered from the road, and got into exactly the kind of scuffle over a small slight that had nearly ended Brenner's own trial years earlier — and Brenner, standing where Draconis had once stood, with the same list of names in front of him that a sensible officer would have wanted trimmed by midday, left the boy's name where it was and said nothing further about it until the trials themselves were run, in words the General told me he recognized, nearly, from a story Draconis himself had told him once.

He asked Brenner about it directly, some days later, careful to frame the question as simple curiosity rather than anything closer to a test of his own. Brenner told him, without much ceremony, that he'd learned a long time ago from a man he trusted completely that any fool with enough drilling could learn to hold a line, and that what couldn't be taught into a recruit no matter how many seasons you had him was the instinct to break formation for something that mattered more than the formation did. He said it plainly, the General told me, with no sense at all that he was quoting anyone — the words had simply become his own somewhere between the yard he'd nearly been cut from and the yard he now ran, the way a lesson passes cleanly from one man into another until neither of them remembers whose it was to begin with.

The General told me he didn't tell Brenner where the words had actually come from, and didn't need to, and understood, watching him work that season, that he was looking at something closer to an unbroken line than he usually let himself notice in the ordinary run of the household's business — a standard Draconis had built out of instinct once, on a road and in a muster yard, now carried forward by a man who bore the physical cost of a choice he would never know had been made on his behalf, teaching it in turn to boys who would likewise never know its true origin. He told me it was the closest he ever came, in all those years, to believing that something good could grow directly out of a thing he privately still counted as a failure of his own — that the very restraint which had cost Brenner his stride had also, without either of them intending it, given him something to spend the rest of his service building instead.

Draconis, for his part, never spoke to the General about handing the musters to Brenner as anything more than the sensible use of a good judge of character who could no longer chase raiders across a field. The General let it stand at that, the way he let so much else stand unexamined between the two of them, and told me only, closing the story, that he thought some debts were better repaid by watching them multiply quietly into other men's lives than by ever trying to settle them directly.
