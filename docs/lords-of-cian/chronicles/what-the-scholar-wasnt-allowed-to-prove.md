# What the Scholar Wasn't Allowed to Prove

*Locked canon, Batch 199, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-798`). Sovereign Ghost of the Great Sea Alias Chronicle XLIII, wave 15 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The naval historian had spent three years compiling the most rigorous account of the ghost fleet's
activity that existed anywhere — cross-referenced sightings, tidal correlations, hull-configuration
analysis, everything short of a direct interview, assembled with the patient discipline of someone who
genuinely wanted to understand rather than merely fear what she was studying.

Her request, delivered by a neutral intermediary, was simple and almost apologetic: a single meeting,
off the record, to confirm or correct the manuscript before she submitted it for publication.

Kanja granted it, meeting her alone on a stretch of neutral coastline rather than aboard ship, curious
despite himself about what three years of honest study had actually produced.

"Most of it's right," he told her, paging through the manuscript's careful diagrams. "The tidal
correlation an analyst found independently, months ago — you've confirmed it from a different
direction. The hull-trim analysis is more precise than even our own shipwright's notes."

"Then it can be published as written?"

"It can be *true* as written. Whether it can be published is a different question entirely, and not
one I get to answer for you." He handed the manuscript back. "You've built the most accurate account of
this fleet that exists. You've also built a document detailed enough that the right reader could use
it to counter half of what makes us effective."

She was quiet for a long moment, the specific stillness of a scholar confronting the gap between
truth and consequence. "I didn't write this to help the Trust hunt you."

"I believe that. It doesn't change what the document could do once it's out of your hands." Kanja
watched her weigh a decision that wasn't really his to make for her. "I won't stop you from publishing
it. That's not a threat, and it's not my call to make. I'm telling you what I know, honestly, the way
you asked me to. What you do with that honesty is entirely yours."

She didn't publish it. Not out of fear, she told the intermediary weeks later, but because three years
of rigorous work had convinced her the fleet's actual value to people who needed it outweighed her own
professional credit in proving how thoroughly she'd understood it — and the manuscript stayed in a
locked drawer, complete and accurate and permanently unread by anyone who might have used it as a
weapon.

"Doesn't that bother you?" the intermediary asked her. "Three years, and nobody will ever see the
work."

"I'll know it's right," she said. "That's different from needing everyone else to know it too."
