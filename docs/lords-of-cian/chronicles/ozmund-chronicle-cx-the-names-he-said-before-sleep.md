# The Names He Said Before Sleep

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1839`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CX, fifth entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave (part A), set strictly
pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. A private, self-directed nightly
habit — silently naming, in order, everyone he spoke with or dealt with that day before allowing
himself to sleep — that young Ozmund never explained to anyone, and that quietly seeds the value later
shown in full at `MAW-084`/`CC-022` (why he trains Red Beard as a soldier rather than liberates him as
a victim). Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. No new named characters.*

---

Of all the small private habits I came to learn about the General across our years together, this is
the one that took me longest to understand the shape of, because he never once described it to me as a
discipline at all, the way he spoke of the Stone Count or the quarry or the copied line of the House
record. He described it, when he finally described it, almost sheepishly, as though it embarrassed him
a little to admit a grown man had once needed, and perhaps still needed, so small and so private a
thing to fall asleep by.

He told me it began sometime in the years before he was fully grown, and that he could not point to the
single evening it started, only that at some point he had noticed himself doing it and simply kept
doing it afterward without much examining why. Each night, lying in the dark before sleep took him, he
would go back over the day just finished and name, silently, in the order he'd encountered them, every
person he had actually dealt with — not the great business of the House, not his father's councils or
his mother's lessons, but the ordinary run of a day's small exchanges: the stableman who'd saddled his
horse, the woman at the kitchens who'd set aside a portion for him without being asked, the guard who'd
stood the gate through a long cold watch, Osric's morning report, a servant's passing greeting in a
corridor he might otherwise have walked through without truly seeing at all.

He told me it was never enough, in those early years, simply to remember that such people existed in
the general shape of *the household* — he wanted, each night, to be able to say something true and
specific about each one of them that a day's business alone would not have taught him: not just that
the stableman had saddled his horse, but that the stableman's hands had been stiff that morning from
some old injury he never complained of; not just that a guard had stood a watch, but which watch, and
whether the man had looked tired doing it, and whether anyone had thought to relieve him on time. He
told me he did not always manage it. Some nights the list came up thin, a name or two recalled only in
outline, and those were, he said, the nights that troubled him most, because they meant he had moved
through an entire day surrounded by people and had somehow managed to see almost none of them clearly.

He never told anyone he did this. Not Osric, who might have found it a strange thing for a young lord
to trouble himself with; not his father, who he suspected would have approved of it in principle and
made something formal of it in the telling, which was exactly the outcome he wanted to avoid. He told
me he understood, even as a boy, some instinct in himself that the habit would not survive being turned
into an instruction given to others, or a virtue displayed for anyone's benefit — that it only worked,
whatever it was actually doing to him, because it asked nothing of anyone but himself, in the dark,
where no one could see whether he kept to it or let it lapse.

I asked him once, much later, whether he thought it had made him a different kind of man than he might
otherwise have been, and he told me he honestly did not know how to answer that, because a habit kept
quietly for so many years stops feeling like a choice at all and starts feeling like simply who you
are. But he did tell me — and this is the part I have never forgotten, because it explained a great
deal about a man I had by then already chosen to follow — that he thought, looking back, it was where
he had first learned to see a person as a whole life rather than a role attached to a task: not
*the guard on the gate*, but a particular tired man who had stood a particular cold watch and deserved,
at the very least, to be remembered as more than the post he'd filled that day. He told me he did not
think it was an accident that the habit outlasted his boyhood entirely, or that it was still, in some
form, alive in him on the day he first looked at a column of Cestari and saw soldiers rather than
cargo. He said a man does not learn to see people all at once, on the day it finally matters. He learns
it in the dark, alone, naming names no one will ever ask him to account for, years before the world
gives him any reason to need to.
