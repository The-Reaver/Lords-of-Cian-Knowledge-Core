# What His Hands Remembered

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1797`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXVIII — the Aethelgard strand's third wave, ninth entry, set strictly pre-Book-1, in

reconstructing a story told to him long after Aethelgard's death, matching the strand's established
retrospective register. A quiet, ordinary-competence entry distinct from the strand's swordwork

Of everything the General ever told me his father taught him, this is the one he mentioned least often
and, I came to think, valued most — not because it carried some hidden weight the way the coin did, or
some argument he was still working out decades later, but because it never asked anything of him at all
beyond doing the thing correctly, with his own two hands, for no reason except that his father had
decided he ought to know how.

It started, the General said, with a leak in the hull of an old flat-bottomed skiff the House kept
mostly for ferrying supplies along the nearer channels — nothing grand, nothing a proper shipwright
couldn't have fixed in an afternoon if summoned. Aethelgard didn't summon one. He took the boy down to
the boathouse himself, laid out tools the General said his father clearly knew how to use with more
real competence than he'd ever shown at swordwork, and told him plainly that they were going to fix it
themselves, the two of them, and that it would likely take most of the day and go wrong at least once
before it went right.

It did go wrong, more than once. The General said the first attempt at the patch split clean along its
seam the moment they tested it, soaking them both to the knee, and his father laughed about it in the
same register he'd later laugh about the fishing boat, unbothered, already reaching for a different
piece of wood before the first was even out of the water. There was no lesson attached to any of it in
the moment — no pause for a coin, no question about whether the boy had chosen something or had it
happen to him, none of the usual shape their conversations took when Aethelgard was teaching him
something that mattered in the way most of what he was taught mattered. It was just work. Cut, fit,
seal, test, and when it failed, cut again.

The General asked him, partway through the second attempt, why they were doing it themselves at all,
when any shipwright in the district could have finished the job properly in an hour. His father
considered the question seriously, the way he considered most things the boy asked him, and told him
that he wanted his son to have at least one thing he could do with his own hands that had nothing to do
with the House, nothing to do with the Scar, nothing anyone would ever expect of him because of who his
family was or what he could do that no one else could. A skill like this one, Aethelgard said, belonged
to whoever actually learned it and to nobody else — no title made a man better at fitting a hull patch,
no inheritance did the work for him, and no amount of anything he'd been born with could substitute for
simply having done it wrong enough times to finally do it right.

It took most of the afternoon, exactly as promised, and the General said the third attempt held. Not
elegantly — his father inspected the finished seam with visible pride that the General suspected wasn't
entirely warranted by the quality of the work itself, and pronounced it seaworthy with the same total
conviction he'd later bring to declaring himself the victor over a fish that had, if anything, gotten
the better of him. They tested it properly the next morning, loaded and on the water, and it held for
years afterward, the General said, long enough that he checked on it himself, unprompted, every few
seasons for the rest of his boyhood, the way you check on something you made rather than something you
were simply given.

He told me he never became especially skilled at it — there was no need, once he was grown, for a man
capable of what he was capable of to spend his afternoons patching hulls. But he said he never forgot
how, either, and that some quiet part of him, all the years afterward, took a particular satisfaction in
knowing there was at least one thing he could do competently that had nothing whatsoever to do with the
Spike, the Scar, or the name he carried — a thing his father had given him not because it was owed to
him by blood, but simply because he thought a boy ought to have it.
