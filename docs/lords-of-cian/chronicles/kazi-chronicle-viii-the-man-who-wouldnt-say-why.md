# Kazi Chronicle VIII: The Man Who Wouldn't Say Why

*Locked canon, Batch 288, 2026-09-12 (`MCD-1527`). Eighth entry in Kazi's own Chronicles, its fifth
with Tunji (`PH2-065`) as protagonist. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but granted no command,
no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention. Narration is
close-third on Tunji throughout. An internal-factional-distrust entry, also dramatizing the friction
between Tunji's shop-floor register and Kunle's/Kalamu's legal-and-press registers for the first time.
Slots into no existing mainline battle — original homage-era material set in Kazi itself.*

---

Torvald had not spoken to Tunji since the gantry gate, and for three months Tunji had let the silence
sit unexamined, the way he let most things sit before deciding whether they were worth disturbing.

It stopped being something to leave alone the afternoon Torvald told two other riggers, loud enough to
be meant for more ears than theirs, that Tunji couldn't be trusted — that a man who spent half his week
talking to a lawyer and the other half feeding a newspaperman couldn't possibly still belong to the
floor the rest of the time.

"He's not wrong about what I do," Tunji said to Zola, later, without heat. "I do talk to Kunle. I do
talk to Kalamu. He's wrong about what it means."

"You want me to talk to him?"

"No. He didn't trust me because of what I do. Someone talking on my behalf won't fix a doubt that's
about me specifically." Tunji considered it a while longer. "I think I have to let him watch me do it,
the actual thing, rather than explain it to him after the fact. Explaining is what a man does when he's
already lost the argument."

The chance came a week later, when Kalamu wanted a name for the following morning's sheet — the plant
manager who'd docked three men's pay over his own scheduling error, the same manager from the gantry
season, still unpunished for it in any way that had reached four thousand readers. Kalamu wanted it
printed plainly, with the manager's title attached, timed to land the same week a new safety inspection
was due.

Torvald was in the room when Tunji said no.

"Not yet. Not this week." Tunji said it to Kalamu directly, the same plain, unhurried voice he used for
everything. "You print it now, tied to the inspection, and it reads like we're trying to force the
inspector's hand with a threat instead of a fact. Wait two weeks. Let the inspection stand on what it
actually finds. Then print it, and it reads like proof instead of pressure."

"Two weeks is a long time to sit on a name that's already true," Kalamu said, not arguing exactly, more
turning the timing over out loud.

"It's true either week. It only helps the men if it lands the week it can't be waved off as a threat."

Kalamu printed it two weeks later, exactly as Tunji had asked, and it landed the way Tunji had said it
would — as proof, not pressure, precisely because the inspection had already spoken first on its own
terms.

Torvald said nothing about any of it for another week, and then, finding Tunji alone at the water
station, said the only thing that mattered. "You told him no. He's the one with the press. You could've
had him print anything you wanted, any week you wanted, and you told him to wait."

"That's the whole of it, yes."

"I thought — " Torvald stopped, started again. "I thought a man who talked to a lawyer and a
newspaperman that easy must have decided the floor wasn't where his real loyalty sat anymore. Watching
you tell Kalamu no was the first time I understood it might be the other way around. That maybe you
talk to them so easy because you've never once let either of them decide anything for you."

"I don't hold their kind of power, and they don't hold mine," Tunji said. "Kunle can win a case I could
never argue. Kalamu can reach four thousand readers I could never gather in one room. I can read a
floor in a way neither of them ever will. None of that makes one of us more loyal than another. It just
means we're not the same tool, and a man who only trusts one tool is going to lose the fights that need
a different one."

A stranger who had been filling water jugs at the station the whole time said nothing, refilled
Torvald's own cup without being asked, and moved on to the next station before either man thought to
thank him for it.

Torvald was not, by the end of that week, a man Tunji would have called fully vetted — that kind of
trust still took the months it always took, on both sides now, not just Tunji's. But he was, for the
first time since the gantry gate, a man willing to watch a while longer before deciding what he'd seen.
