# The Order He Reversed in Front of Everyone

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Captain Alias Chronicle, wave 13. Rebellion era. Not
a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters beyond
already-locked crew.*

---

Kanja gave the order to hold position, certain the approaching vessel was a scout rather than a
threat worth engaging directly, and had already turned to address something else when one of the
younger deck hands spoke up in front of the full assembled crew, which nobody did lightly to a
direct order mid-operation.

"That's not a scout pattern," the sailor said. "Scouts hold their bearing steady. That ship's
adjusting course to match our drift, which means it's tracking us on purpose, and if we hold
position we're giving it exactly the time it needs to signal the rest of its fleet."

Kanja stopped, actually looked at the approaching vessel's movement again rather than dismissing the
correction out of reflexive authority, and recognized within seconds that the sailor was right.

"Break position," he said immediately, no hesitation, no attempt to save face by pretending the
original order had been anything but wrong. "Full evasive, now — she called it correctly, I didn't."

The crew moved on the new order instantly, the vessel's threat neutralized through the evasive break
before it could close the distance further, and no harm came of the near-miss at all. What stuck
with the crew afterward wasn't the danger avoided — it was how fast the reversal had come, with zero
defensiveness, in front of everyone rather than corrected quietly after the fact.

"You could have waited until we were alone to admit that," the sailor said afterward, still faintly
startled by it.

"I could have. It would have protected my pride and cost this crew nothing today, since it worked
out. But the next time you catch something I've missed, in front of everyone, I want you to already
know from experience that saying so out loud gets heard immediately, not filed away to be
acknowledged privately later where it's safer for me. That confidence is worth more to this crew
than my pride is worth to me."

Word of it spread through the ship faster than the near-miss itself had — not as a story about
danger, but as a story about what happened to a junior sailor's correction the moment it turned out
to be right.
