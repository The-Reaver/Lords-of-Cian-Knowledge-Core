# The Order He Wouldn't Carry Out

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LIII, wave 18. Rebellion era, during the active Sewer War of Killane (MCD-234, age 20). Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The order came down through the resistance's own command relay, clean and tactically sound on paper:
collapse the eastern junction to sever the garrison's fastest reinforcement route, before dawn,
before the Trust could finish the troop movement everyone knew was coming.

Kanja read it twice and refused it once, plainly, to the courier who'd carried it.

"Take this back and tell whoever wrote it that the eastern junction has forty-one people sheltering in
the side galleries above it — families who moved there three days ago because it was the safest
section left in the district. I'm not bringing that junction down with them still inside it."

"The order accounts for that. It says clear the shelter first, then collapse." The courier didn't
enjoy relaying the pushback any more than he'd enjoyed relaying the order. "Command's asking for it
before dawn. That's maybe four hours to move forty-one people and still get the demolition done in
time to matter."

"Then command's asking for something I can't deliver honestly, and I'd rather tell them that now than
pretend I can and fail forty-one people at four in the morning." Kanja was already walking toward the
shelter galleries as he spoke, the courier half-jogging to keep pace. "Moving forty-one people safely
— checking every one of them clear, not just counting heads and hoping — takes longer than four hours
even without a demolition crew working against the same clock. I'm not going to rush that count
because a deadline says I should."

The courier carried the refusal back up the relay, and Kanja spent the four hours command had given
him doing the count properly instead — waking families gently rather than urgently, checking the side
passages twice each for anyone too old or too young to move at the pace the others managed, confirming
the last child clear with his own hands before he'd let anyone call the shelter empty.

Command's reinforcement route argument held anyway; the garrison's troop movement was real, and every
hour the junction stood was an hour closer to a relief force the siege could not afford to absorb. A
second order arrived at the six-hour mark, sharper than the first, making clear that the delay itself
now had a cost measured in more than forty-one lives.

"You were right that I couldn't do it in four hours," Kanja told the crew chief who delivered it,
already setting the first charge now that the galleries above stood genuinely, verifiably empty. "You
were also right that every hour I spent proving it cost the district something on the other side of
that ledger. I don't think either of us was wrong. I think we were just weighing two true costs
against each other, and I chose the one I could look someone in the face about afterward."

The junction came down two hours past the original deadline, the reinforcement route severed eight
hours later than command had wanted and not one moment sooner than forty-one people's safety would
allow. The garrison's relief force reached the district regardless, delayed rather than stopped,
costing the campaign three more days of siege than a clean four-hour execution would have.

"Command's not happy about the three days," the crew chief said, watching the dust settle over the
collapsed junction.

"I understand why. I'm not happy about the three days either." Kanja looked back toward the now-empty
shelter galleries, forty-one people resettled elsewhere in the network by morning. "But I'd take three
hard days over one order I followed exactly as written and one family I couldn't explain it to
afterward. That's not insubordination. That's just refusing to let a deadline decide something a
deadline was never equipped to weigh."
