# The Shrine They Built Without Asking

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Scourge Alias Chronicle XLVI, wave 16. Long Mask era, age 172, V3 gear (Golden Terror period). Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

He came back to the fishing settlement eleven years after freeing it, not to be seen, and found the
low stone building at its center before he found anyone who might recognize him.

It wasn't a meeting hall. The coat hung inside it, or something built to resemble the coat — sailcloth
dyed black and stiffened with resin, draped over a driftwood frame — with two lanterns hung where a
man's eyes would sit, kept burning even at midday. Offerings lined a shelf beneath it: dried fish,
carved wooden figures, a child's first tooth.

He stood in the doorway a long while before the settlement's eldest, a woman who'd been among the
captives freed that night, found him there and didn't recognize the ordinary sailor's clothes he wore
without the coat.

"You've come to see him," she said, mistaking his silence for reverence. "Everyone does, eventually.
He watches the strait for us now. Keeps the slavers from coming back."

"Does he." He kept his voice even. "Or did you keep them from coming back — the harbor watch you
built, the warning-fire chain to the next three settlements, the trade compact that gave you something
worth defending together instead of separately."

She frowned, unused to being contradicted about something she'd built her faith around. "The watch
helps. But it was him who freed us. It's him who's kept us safe since."

"I'd guess the eleven years since did more of that keeping than one night did." He stepped further
in, looking at the lanterns, the driftwood shoulders, the offerings that could have fed someone
instead. "What happens here when the next threat isn't the kind a coat can stand against? A bad
harvest. A sickness. Does the shrine feed anyone, or does it just burn lantern-oil that could have?"

"That's not—" She stopped, genuinely turning it over rather than defending out of reflex, which was
the opening he'd hoped for rather than expected.

"I'm not asking you to tear it down," he said, gentler now. "Memory's worth keeping. But memory of a
man isn't what kept this harbor safe for eleven years. You did that. The watch chain did that. If
you ever stop believing that — if you start waiting for the coat to come back instead of trusting what
you built yourselves — the shrine will have cost you more than it ever gave you."

She studied him a moment longer than was comfortable, something uncertain crossing her face that
never quite resolved into recognition. "You talk like you know him."

"I've heard the stories," he said, and left before she could ask anything more specific, the lanterns
still burning behind him, the offering shelf undisturbed — not his to take down, only his to have
said something true beside.
