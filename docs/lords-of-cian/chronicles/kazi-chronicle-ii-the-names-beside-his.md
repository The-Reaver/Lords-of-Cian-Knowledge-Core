# Kazi Chronicle II: The Names Beside His

*DRAFT -- pending Abad's approval, not yet locked canon. Batch 100 candidate (`MCD-363`
candidate, `PH2-063`/`PH2-064` candidates). Second entry in Kazi's own Chronicles, its protagonist
Irin (`PH2-051`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but granted no
command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention. Narration
is close-third on Irin throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Names and details the two lieutenants
`PH2-051` already flags but leaves undetailed: "Two lieutenants drawn from the real DRUM/League
leadership circle (homage to John Watson, Mike Hamlin, and Ken Cockrel Sr.) stand as his founding
co-organizers, not yet individually named or detailed." Two new named characters, Kunle (homage to
Ken Cockrel Sr.) and Kalamu (a composite homage to John Watson and Mike Hamlin), both
collision-checked against the full live ledger (zero prior hits).*

---

They came for Bakari eleven days after the line stopped, the way Irin had known someone would —
not for calling the halt himself, since no single order could be traced back to a single voice
loud enough to punish, but for the smaller thing they could actually pin to a name: a torn gate, a
foreman's bruised pride, a charge dressed up in language heavy enough to put a man away for years
over a fight that had lasted less than a minute.

Irin went to the jail that night and found two people already there ahead of him, in the narrow
hallway outside the cells, arguing with a guard who had no intention of letting either of them
through before morning.

"I filed the paperwork three hours ago," Kunle was saying, in the particular flat, unhurried tone
of a man who had learned that raising his voice only ever gave people permission to stop listening
to the words in it. "You can read it again if it makes you feel better about ignoring it a second
time."

"He's not seeing anyone tonight."

"He's seeing his lawyer tonight, or I'm back here at dawn with three more filings and a magistrate
who already doesn't like being woken up on your account." Kunle did not move. He had the stillness
of a man who had said this exact sentence, or one close enough to it, more times than he cared to
count, and had learned it worked better delivered quiet than loud.

Kalamu stood a step behind him, already writing — not the argument itself, but the guard's own
words, precise, dated, the kind of detail that meant nothing to a jail hallway at midnight and
everything to four thousand readers by the following afternoon.

"You're writing this down," the guard said.

"I write most things down. It's the only part of this job that's ever done any good." Kalamu did
not look up from the page. "A man doesn't get charged the way Bakari's charged unless someone
upstream decided this strike needed an example made of somebody. I'd like the four thousand people
reading my sheet tomorrow to know exactly whose decision that was, in exactly the words used to
make it."

The guard let Kunle through twenty minutes later. Irin watched it happen the way he'd watched most
things since the day the line first stopped for him — not with surprise, exactly, but with the
specific recognition of watching two people do, without needing to be asked, exactly the two things
he had never once known how to do himself.

"You didn't send for either of them," Irin said, once Kunle came back out.

"We don't wait to be sent for anymore. Haven't since the day you first stopped that line." Kunle
leaned against the wall, the tiredness he'd been holding off finally showing through for a moment.
"You gave four thousand men a way to feel the floor beneath them shift at the same instant. That's
not a thing either of us can do. What we can do is make sure the floor doesn't get pulled out from
under one of them alone, in a room with no one watching, once the plant decides the easiest way to
break a line is to break the men holding it."

"Bakari's trial is in nine days," Kalamu said, closing the notebook. "By then every reader on my
route will know his name, know the charge, and know it was filed the same week four thousand men
found out they could move as one. A magistrate can convict a man for a torn gate. It's a great deal
harder to convict him for that, in a courtroom half the city's already decided to watch."

The stranger, who had been in the hallway the whole time without either man remarking on him,
helped carry a stack of Kalamu's printed sheets out to the waiting distributors before the sun came
up, saying nothing, credited with nothing, gone again before the first one reached a reader's hands.

Bakari's charge did not hold. It took nine days, a courtroom Kunle argued the way a man argues
something he has already decided he cannot afford to lose, and four thousand readers who knew the
shape of the story before the magistrate ever opened his mouth to rule on it — but it did not hold,
and every man on the line understood, by the time it was over, that the thing Irin had started did
not begin or end with him alone.

"I built the way the floor moves," Irin said, that evening, watching the two of them already deep
in an argument about the next thing worth watching for. "I never built the parts that catch a man
when the floor alone isn't enough to hold him. I don't think I could have. That's not a gap in what
I can do. It's just not the same gift, and I'd rather stand next to the two of you who have it than
pretend I don't need you."
