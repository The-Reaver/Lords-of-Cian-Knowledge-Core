# The Night the Gate Went Unwatched

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1833`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle CIV, Wider Verehimu Household/Guard strand, wave 2. Set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony
(`MCD-025`), in Ozmund's later adolescence — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund already taking a genuine hand in
the House's daily governance. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, a third
piece drawn from the same long conversation with Sergeant Oswin Kade. A genuine mistake, confessed
rather than discovered, and handled with grace — the origin, left implicit, of the honest-reporting
culture Kade later prizes in the successor he trains (Chronicle XCIX). No new named characters.*

---

Sergeant Oswin Kade did not offer this one easily. He told me, before he began it, that he had never
repeated it to anyone in the House while he still wore the colors, and that he was only telling it to
me now because he had decided, somewhere in his long retirement, that a story kept entirely to oneself
stops being useful to anyone, including the man carrying it.

He had been young then, not yet a sergeant, still years from any rank that mattered — a guardsman
assigned to a stretch of the outer wall on a night his own father lay dying two villages distant, word
of which had reached him only hours before his watch began. Kade said he had gone to his post anyway,
believing, in the stubborn way of young men convinced grief is a weakness to be outworked rather than
felt, that he could hold the watch and hold his own composure at the same time. He said he had been
wrong about that, more completely than he had ever admitted to another living soul.

He did not fall asleep, he told me — he was careful on that point, unwilling to let the story become
smaller or larger than it actually was. What happened, he said, was worse in its way for being so
plainly human: he had stood the watch with his body present and the whole of his attention two villages
away, and for the better part of an hour before his relief arrived, he could not have told anyone, if
asked, what had passed along that stretch of wall. Nothing came of it. No breach, no incident, nothing
that any report would ever have needed to mention. But Kade said he had known, standing there, that he
had failed the one plain obligation of the post — not through carelessness or cowardice, but through
grief he hadn't yet learned how to carry alongside a duty — and he had never told a soul, not his
sergeant then, not any commander since, carrying it quietly for the better part of forty years.

What finally moved him to speak of it, he told me, was watching the young guardsman he was training —
the one from the story I have already given the reader — come to him unprompted to report an error of
his own. Kade said the pride he'd felt in that moment had curdled, almost at once, into something closer
to shame, because he had spent years holding every recruit under him to a standard of honest reporting
he had never once met himself. He went, he told me, directly to the General that same week — by then
already a young man with a real hand in how the House was run — and told him the whole of it, decades
late, expecting nothing in particular beyond finally having said it aloud.

He said the General heard him out without interrupting, and when Kade finished, asked him only one
question: whether anything had actually happened that night, on that stretch of wall, that the House
had never learned of. Kade told him no — nothing had happened, only the absence of a man's full
attention for the length of an hour no one had ever needed. The General said, then, that a post held
imperfectly through grief wasn't the same thing as a post abandoned, and that he didn't see a fault
worth carrying forty years for a mistake that had cost nothing and taken four decades of silent guilt
to admit. What he asked instead, Kade told me, was whether the story might be worth telling to the
guardsmen coming up under him — not as confession, but as instruction, so that the next man standing a
watch through his own grief might know sooner than Kade had that admitting the difficulty wasn't the
same as failing at it.

Kade said that conversation was the actual beginning of the policy he'd since built his whole training
of a successor around — not a rule handed down from above, as most of the House's other reforms in
those years seemed to have been, but something he'd built himself, out of his own forty-year silence,
once he'd finally been given permission to stop keeping it.
