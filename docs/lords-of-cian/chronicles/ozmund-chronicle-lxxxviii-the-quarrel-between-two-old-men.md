# The Quarrel Between Two Old Men

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1817`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXVIII, House politics strand wave 3, ninth entry.*

---

The General allowed himself, telling me this one, more open amusement than he usually permitted
himself over these stories, and said he counted it, without much hesitation, among the more
genuinely funny episodes of his years at that House — though he was careful to add that it hadn't
felt funny in the moment, standing between two furious old men each entirely convinced the other was
trying to ruin him.

House Verehimu kept, as most Houses of its size did, a Master of Hounds and a Master of Stables, two
positions that had coexisted under the same roof for as long as anyone living could remember without
any formally settled understanding of which one held precedence when their duties overlapped — a
gap in the household's own protocols that had apparently caused no serious trouble in living memory,
mostly, the General suspected, because the two men who'd held the posts before the current pair had
simply gotten along well enough not to test it. Their successors did not get along nearly so well,
and the annual autumn hunt, which required both the kennels and the stables to coordinate closely for
the better part of a week, had become, by the third year of their shared tenure, an occasion both men
approached less like colleagues preparing a hunt and more like two generals preparing a campaign
against each other.

The dispute that finally forced Ozmund's involvement, he twenty-seven by then, concerned nothing
grander than which man's staff would be permitted to use the yard nearest the main house for their
morning preparations in the week before the hunt — a genuinely minor convenience, made significant
only by three years of accumulated resentment that had transformed it into, as the General put it, a
matter of honor neither man could any longer see clearly enough to recognize as absurd. Both had
appealed to him separately, each certain the other was deliberately obstructing his own work out of
spite, each citing some version of tradition that, on inspection, turned out to rest on nothing firmer
than each man's own confident memory of how things had "always" been done.

The General said his first temptation was simply to rule — the yard was, after all, House property,
and he held every authority needed to assign it to whichever man he judged had the stronger claim.
What stopped him, he told me, was recognizing that a ruling would settle the yard and do nothing at
all about three years of accumulated pride now standing behind it, which meant the underlying quarrel
would simply relocate itself to the next available grievance the following season.

So instead he did something he admitted, telling it to me, bordered on outright mischief. He called
both men to the same room at the same time, which neither had agreed to in advance and which visibly
unsettled them both, and rather than adjudicating the yard dispute directly, asked each, in front of
the other, to describe — honestly, and at length — precisely what made the other man good at his own
work. He said the silence that followed lasted long enough that he genuinely wondered whether he'd
misjudged the whole approach. But eventually, haltingly, and with considerable visible reluctance, the
Master of Hounds admitted that he'd never once seen a horse better cared for than under the Master of
Stables' hand, and the Master of Stables, not to be outdone even in grudging praise, allowed that he'd
never known a pack better disciplined than the one the Master of Hounds kept.

The General told me the yard dispute resolved itself within minutes after that — a simple alternating
schedule, the same solution either man could have proposed himself three years earlier, agreed to
almost as an afterthought once the actual argument in the room had stopped being about the yard at
all. What lingered, he said, wasn't the schedule but the visible, faintly embarrassed thaw between the
two men as they left the room together, still arguing, but arguing now about whose hound had actually
frightened whose horse during some incident years past, in the easy, needling tone of men who had
just remembered, almost despite themselves, that they respected each other.

He said he never took any particular credit for the reconciliation, which he considered, in the end,
something the two men had done themselves once given a reason to say it aloud rather than something
he'd engineered for them. What he did take from it, he told me, smiling slightly even in the retelling,
was a small, durable piece of practical wisdom he said he used more than once in the years that
followed: that a surprising number of quarrels that look, from a distance, like disputes over
principle are actually disputes between two people who have simply forgotten, under the accumulated
weight of old grievances, that they once admired each other.
