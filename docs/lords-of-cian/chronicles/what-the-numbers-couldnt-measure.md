# What the Numbers Couldn't Measure

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle III, wave 13,
closing the wave. Rebellion era (age 19-21), unarmed and non-combat throughout, matching the alias's
established ethos. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named
characters.*

---

The dockhand's figure, when Ezio asked for it, came out smaller than the man's years of overtime
should have produced, and Ezio said so gently, expecting the usual explanation — fear, modesty, a
number rounded down out of habit.

"The wages aren't what I'm owed," the dockhand said instead. "Not really. Write whatever the hours
say I'm owed in coin. That's the easy part. It's not the part that matters."

"Then tell me the part that matters."

"My mother was dying for the better part of two years. Every season I asked for time to sit with her,
the yard found a reason the overtime couldn't be covered without me, and I went back to the dock
instead, because we needed the coin and because I was told, every time, that it was only a few more
weeks. She died on a morning I was hauling crates three districts away. I got the word four days
later." He said it without performance, the plain delivery of something turned over so many times it
had worn smooth. "There's no wage figure for that. I don't want one. I want it written somewhere that
it happened, and why, so it's not just a private grief I carry alone — so it's a fact about what this
yard costs people, sitting next to all the coin figures, where anyone reading this book has to see it
too."

Ezio didn't try to fit it into the wage column. He opened a second kind of entry instead — unmonetized,
plainly stated, the dockhand's own words recorded as closely to verbatim as memory allowed, filed
alongside the financial tally but never mistaken for it.

"This won't get you anything," Ezio said, honestly, once it was written. "No settlement pays this
back. I want you to understand that before it goes in the book."

"I understand it better than you do." The dockhand watched him close the ledger over the entry. "I
didn't ask for it to be paid. I asked for it to be true, and written somewhere it can't be quietly
forgotten the way the yard forgot every time it told me a few more weeks wouldn't cost me anything."

The completed district ledger carried, alongside four hundred wage entries, six of this second kind
by the time the tally closed — losses no figure could settle, recorded not for remedy but for
acknowledgment, a column with no total at the bottom because none was ever meant to be added.
