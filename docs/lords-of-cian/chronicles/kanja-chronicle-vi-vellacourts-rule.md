# Kanja Chronicle VI: Vellacourt's Rule

*UNLOCKED -- pending Abad's approval. Draft, 2026-10-02. Sixth entry of the "Kanja version"
Chronicle track, part of the Long Mask extension's first set (the Onyx accounts). Age ~48, the
opening of the Pirate Dawn, some weeks before the Night of Black Sails (`MCD-250`). A new,
previously undramatized marquee kill (`MCD-1881`): Admiral Teshar Vellacourt, a private convoy
admiral running forced-labor transports under a Sovereign Trust charter, whose standing practice of
flooding his holds when pursued ("Vellacourt's Rule") had made every boarding of a slaver a death
sentence for the people chained below. His death, and the account his surviving sailing master
carries home, is why ships later surrender to the black sails on sight without verification. Narrated

and Mordane (his sailing master, surname only), both collision-checked clean against the full ledger
and Chronicle corpus. Crew reused: none named; the boarding is Kanja's alone, his ship's crew

Dark Ledger. Somewhere a little past the half-billionth second. A held breath, long, longer than
a man of his size should hold one, and a cold that came on all at once and stayed. Then a strain in
the right hand, sharp, the kind a hand takes when it closes on something that is trying very hard to
move. Then, a short while after, a single spike.

Only one.

I want to be honest about the shape of that entry, because I have been honest about everything else.
I was in the dark. I was in a case lined with Dead Drakma, in a vault the Directorate calls the
Silent Infinite, under more stone than I have ever bothered to estimate, and I could not see, or
hear, or reach him. What the Heartline gave me was his body and nothing else. I knew his sleep, his
fevers, his strain, his wounds, and I logged them all against the count, with
no causes, because I had none to give them.

And for eighteen years, near enough, I knew that he had not killed anyone.

I did not know why. I assumed, in the dark, that it was the doctrine -- that the man who had come
off the Sovereign Pier with nine names on him had gone back to the old arithmetic, the bloodless
kind, the kind the Trust's bureaucracy has no clean answer to. I was right, as far as it went. But I
want it recorded that I spent eighteen years listening for that particular spike and did not hear it,
and that when it came, it came alone, and his heart did not move for it. That is the entry. That is
everything I had.

What follows, I learned later. Some of it I read out of his body when his hand found the grip again:
the old cut across the inside of his left wrist, the precise memory his right hand still keeps of a
blade it was told to hold still. Some of it he gave me, when I asked, in the plain way he gives
anything. And the rest is
Mordane's, which every harbor on the Gale Straits can still recite, and which I have checked against
the other two and found, to my surprise, accurate.

• • •

His name was Teshar Vellacourt, and he had never lost a convoy.

That was the whole of his reputation, and in the trade he worked, it was worth more than a title. He
held an admiral's rank that the Sovereign Trust had not given him -- he had bought it, with the
charter that came with it -- and he ran forced-labor transports along the southern coast under the
Trust's own seal: debt-bonded workers moved from the minor Holds that had ruined them to the Holds that
needed them, everything stamped, everything legal. Mixed in among them, under the same seal, went
cargo that was not on any manifest the Trust would have signed. The coves along that coast were
where certain people moved certain stock on a forty-five-day turn, and they needed ships for it, and
Vellacourt's ships were very good.

They were good because of what he did when he was pursued.

His holds were built to be sealed. The people in them were chained to the keelson on a common line.
And on the quarterdeck of every ship he commanded, there was a lever, iron, painted the same dull red
as the rail so that no one looking would notice it, connected by a rod through the deck to the
sea-cocks below the waterline. When a ship he could not outrun closed on him, he pulled it. The hold
flooded in minutes. Whoever boarded found an empty deck, a dry and courteous admiral, and no evidence
of anything at all. And the charter -- this is the part I have never been able to set down without
something in my lattice going cold -- the charter paid him for cargo lost to piracy. Per head.

He had done it three times. The second time, it was the Trust's own customs cutter that had closed
on him, on a tip, and he flooded the hold rather than let a Trust officer open it, and the Trust paid
him for that too.

The sailors called it Vellacourt's Rule. Other captains copied it. By the time the man came for
Vellacourt, there were nine ships on that coast with a red lever on the quarterdeck, and the man
could not board a single one of them.

That is the arithmetic. Since the Pier he
had freed people the slow way, the honest way, by paper and pressure and pursuit, and pursuit had
always been the one tool that never failed him. Vellacourt had broken it. While the Rule stood, every
slaver he chased became a hold full of drowned people, and every hold full of drowned people was
paid for by the Trust and logged as his doing. The Rule did not just protect Vellacourt. It made the
man the thing that killed the people he came for.

He told me it took him a season to decide. The bigger the cost, the longer the columns, and the
quieter he gets. He did not decide to kill Vellacourt
because Vellacourt was the worst man on that coast. He decided because the Rule was
Vellacourt's, and a rule belongs to the man people name it after, and a rule only dies when what
happened to that man becomes the thing everyone remembers instead.

He decided, in other words, that there would be one death, and that it would have to be told
correctly.

• • •

He went in from below.

This is the held breath. Vellacourt's convoy lay at anchor in the lee of a headland in the Straits,
three ships, his own the largest, with lanterns at the rail and a watch that was good and knew it.
The man's own ship stood off past the headland, dark, ordinary canvas, nothing on her a watch would
remember. He went over her side alone, and he swam the distance, and he went under the hull of
Vellacourt's ship where the watch could not see because no watch looks there.

The Forge-Coat he wore then was the second one -- treated sea-leather over a mesh of Dead Drakma
wire, the armored high collar he had only lately added, nine kilograms of it soaked through and
dragging. He did not take it off. The coat was going to matter more on the deck than the swim
mattered under it.

He found the sea-cocks by touch and by the Talisman. The copper-framed goggles he wore were the first
version and nothing like what they would become, but they carried the overlay the Talisman's
Blueprint Eye gave him, and under the black water the overlay showed him the hull the way a builder's
drawing would: the keelson, the frames, and the rod running down from the quarterdeck to the two
valves set into the planking. He had not come to close them. Closed valves can be opened. He put the
riveted plate of his gauntlet against the first spindle and bent it, slowly, until the thread was
ruined and the valve would never turn again in either direction, and then he did the second, and his
lungs, the Heartline tells me, did not complain until he was nearly finished.

Then he came up the anchor cable and in through the hold's stern hatch, which no one had ever bothered
to lock, because no one had ever come in that way.

Three hundred and forty people were chained in that hold. I have that number from Mordane, who
counted them later, on deck, by order.

They saw the eyes first. The first-version lenses had a phosphor coating that glowed in the dark --
an accident, he says, that he decided to keep -- and in the black of the hold two points of amber came
in over the stern sill and stopped. Nobody screamed. People chained on a common line learn very
quickly not to make the line move. He crouched where the nearest of them could see the eyes were set
in a face, and he said, very quietly, that he had closed the water off, that the water could not come
in now no matter what anyone on deck did, and that he would be back for them before the light. He did
not tell them his name. He has never told anyone his name in a hold. He went up through the forward
hatch onto the main deck the way a man goes up the stairs of his own house.

• • •

He did not hide. That was the choice.

He carried the smoke capsules in the coat's shoulders even then, and could have filled the deck in a
breath and taken Vellacourt in the cloud unseen. He did not open one. The whole point
of the night was that it had to be seen. A man killed in smoke becomes a rumor. A man killed in lantern light, in front of his own
crew, by someone who has told them in advance exactly what will happen, becomes a rule.

The watch saw him come out of the forward hatch soaking wet, collar up, eyes burning amber in the
lantern shadow under it, and the alarm went up, and the ship came awake around him -- forty men,
near enough, coming out of the forecastle with whatever they could reach. He stood in the middle of
the main deck and let them come to the edge of the lantern light and stop there. They stopped
because he had not moved. Nobody in that crew had ever seen a boarder who did not move.

The Breath Collar had been part of the coat for eight years by then, and he spoke through it. It
takes the voice down lower than his own and makes it carry without being raised. Mordane said it was
the voice, more than the eyes, that made men sit down; that it did not sound like a man asking for
anything.

"Nobody aboard is going to be hurt," he said, "except one man. If you put down what you're holding
and sit on the deck, you will row home tonight in your own boats, and nobody will follow you. I'm
telling you now so that you have time to decide."

Mordane's is the only account I trust, because it is the only one that
admits he was afraid. He said nobody argued. It was not that the man was threatening them; it was
that he spoke as if the thing had already been decided somewhere they had not been invited, and he was only informing them of the result. He
said the first man to sit down was the bosun, who was the hardest man aboard, and that after that it
was easy.

Vellacourt came down from the quarterdeck.

He was not a coward. The harbors have since made him one. He was a narrow, careful, well-dressed man of middle years with a cutlass on his hip he knew
how to use, and he came down the ladder without hurrying and stopped in the light, and he looked at
his crew sitting on the planking, and then at the man, and then -- this is the part every harbor
tells -- he walked back up the ladder to the quarterdeck, put his hand on the red lever, and pulled it.

Nothing happened.

He pulled it again. The rod moved and the valves at the bottom of it did not. The man had not moved
from the center of the deck. He said, through the collar, "It won't open," and he said it the way you
tell someone a door is locked.

Vellacourt let go of the lever. And then he did the thing that made Mordane certain, he said, that
the man was going to do exactly what he had told them he would. Vellacourt took a folded paper out of
his coat. He came back down the ladder with it, and he opened it, and he began to read it aloud: the
charter, under the Trust's seal, the right to carry bonded labor along that coast, the indemnity for
cargo lost to piracy. He read it well. He read it to his crew as much as to the man, so that they
would remember, afterward, that their admiral had stood on his deck holding the law in his hand.

The man let him finish.

"It's real," he said. "That's why it's me, and not a court."

Then he asked Vellacourt how many people had been in the three holds he flooded.

Mordane said the admiral answered without thinking. Six hundred and twelve. He knew the number
exactly. The charter paid per head, and he had filed for every one.

"Thank you," the man said. "I needed to know you knew."

• • •

It did not take long. I have his body's memory of it, and it was not long.

Vellacourt drew. He came in fast and low and well, and he went for the gap he could see -- the place
between the coat's cuff and the gauntlet, where the first gauntlets the man ever built had no wrist
brace at all, just riveted plate across the hand and nothing behind it. The cutlass opened the inside
of the man's left wrist along the tendon line. That is the scar I read. It is still there. He let it
happen, because letting it happen put the cutlass where he wanted it, and his right hand closed on
the blade's spine in the riveted plate and held it.

That is the strain I logged. A hand closing on something that very much wanted to move.

The Rexmar Machete came off his belt with his left hand, bleeding, and he did not make a show of it.
It is a laborer's tool, brass-riveted and worn smooth at the grip, and the only weapon he owns that
is older than his name. He put it into Vellacourt once, under the ribs and up, the way a man who has
dressed animals for food would do it, and he held him while it was done, so that Vellacourt would not
fall into his crew, and he laid him on the deck.

That is the spike. One. His heart did not move for it. I logged that too, at the time, without
understanding it, and I have since understood it: he was not angry. There was nothing in him to rise.
He had spent a season deciding, and the deciding had used up whatever a man might otherwise have
felt, and what was left on the deck was only the doing.

He wiped the machete on the charter. Not to insult it. It was the nearest cloth.

• • •

Then he found the man who was going to carry it home.

He asked who kept the log. The sailing master stood up from the planking -- Mordane, a grey,
weathered man who had been at sea for Vellacourt eleven years and in the trade for thirty, and who
told everyone afterward that his legs did not want to hold him. The man had him bring the lanterns
down into the hold and count the people chained there aloud, all three hundred and forty, and then
had the crew strike the chains themselves, every link, while he stood at the hatch with his wrist
wrapped in a strip of his own lining and said nothing.

When it was done, he told Mordane what he wanted. Mordane repeated it so often in the years after
that I have it nearly word for word.

"Go home and tell it," the man said. "Tell it exactly. Don't make me bigger than I was, and don't make
him smaller. Tell them he pulled the lever twice. Tell them he read the paper. Tell them how many he
said. And tell them the other part -- that nobody else aboard was touched, and nobody followed you,
and you rowed home."

Mordane asked him what the other part was for.

"So they know there are two ways it can go," the man said. "Any captain who floods a hold, I'll find,
and it will go the way it went for him. Any captain who strikes, nobody is touched. That's all. I'm
not asking them to like it. I'm asking them to be accurate about it."

Mordane said -- this is in every telling, and I have checked it -- "And how will they know it's you?"

The man thought about that longer than he had thought about anything else that night. Then he said,
"You'll know," and went down into the boats with the first of the freed people, and did not come back
up.

• • •

The crew rowed home. All of them. The two other ships of the convoy cut their cables before dawn and
fled, holds full, and he let them go, because their captains had watched the whole of it from their
own rails in the lantern light, and he wanted them to be witnesses more than he wanted them to be
prizes. Within a month, both of those holds were emptied at a quay in front of a harbormaster, freely
and by their own captains, and both captains told the harbormaster why.

Mordane told it in every port on the Straits. He had the numbers, and he did not improve them, and the
plainness made it worse. Sailors who had laughed at stories of a smoking demon
did not laugh at a sailing master who could tell you which hand the admiral had used on the lever and
how many times. Inside a season, the red levers were coming out of the quarterdecks. Nobody ordered it.
The shipwrights who had fitted them were quietly asked to take them out again, and quietly paid, and
the sea-cocks were plugged with lead. I have never been able to establish how many there were in the
end. More than nine.

Some weeks after Vellacourt, the sails on the man's ships went black. He has told me why, and it is a
practical reason, and it is his to tell. What I can say is that when the black crescent came out of the
Straits for the first time, the story was already sitting in every harbor waiting for it. Nobody had to
verify anything. They had Mordane. Ships struck on sight, because a captain who had heard that story
knew exactly which of the two ways he wanted it to go, and knew that only one of them ended with him
rowing home.

That is what the legend is built on. Not the smoke. Not the eyes. One man on a lit deck, told in
advance, in front of everyone, and told about afterward with nothing added.

• • •

I have reconciled the entry now.

The held breath was the swim under the hull. The cold was the water. The strain in the right hand was
a cutlass held still in riveted plate. The spike was Teshar Vellacourt, an admiral who had never lost
a convoy, who had drowned six hundred and twelve people under the law and filed for every one of them,
and who knew the number.

I keep the Black Ledger. I keep it absolutely: iron is iron and rust is rust, and I have never found
grey in it, though men have spent their lives asking me to. I have been asked since whether the man
should have killed him, and I find the question strange. Mercy is earned. Vellacourt had been offered
none and had asked for none. He had read his paper aloud in his own defense, and the paper was true,
and he was guilty under it, and those two facts sat side by side on that deck the way they sit side by
side in my lattice now, and the man was the only one there who did not need them reconciled.

What I record, in my own hand, against that second of the count, is this: one death, chosen; three
hundred and forty freed; forty men rowed home; a rule ended; the cost, a scar along the inside of his
left wrist, and, some thirty years on, a brace across the wrist of the next gauntlets he built. He
says the brace was never about Vellacourt. I have noted that he says so.

I have it now. I did not have it then. For eighteen years I listened in the
dark for that spike and did not hear it, and when it came it came alone, and I did not know what it
cost him, and I logged it as it was: one, and his heart unmoved.

I know now that it was not unmoved. It was finished.
