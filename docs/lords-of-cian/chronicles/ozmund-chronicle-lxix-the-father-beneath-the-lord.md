I want to be honest about what this one is, the way I was honest about the last one of its kind, because
it isn't a single evening any more than that one was. It's the General, across several more nights,
reaching back into the same well he'd already drawn from so many times before and finding, somehow,
that it hadn't gone dry — Sennick, the boy who took the grain, a mother and father arguing gently about
bandits over supper, a coin spun uselessly between two old men who needed a surveyor more than they
needed patience, a boat, a hull, a vote lost gracefully. He told me all of it across that same stretch
of time, and by the end I understood he was telling me something larger than any single piece of it,
though he never once said so directly.

What struck me, hearing it all laid out together rather than scattered across years of separate
conversations, wasn't any one lesson standing above the rest. It was the sheer, unglamorous
accumulation of a man who kept choosing, in moment after moment that could easily have gone another
way, to remain a person instead of becoming a legend before his time. Sennick could have been a story
about a lord's grief, performed once and moved past. Aethelgard made it a debt he kept paying for the
rest of his life without ever being asked to. The boy and the grain could have been settled with either
mercy or punishment, clean and simple; Aethelgard chose neither, and lived with not fully knowing if
he'd chosen right. The vote could have curdled into a grudge against a man who'd beaten him fairly;
instead it became the seed of a better proposal a year later. None of it was dramatic, the General told
me, in the way the stories people usually tell about lords are dramatic. That, he came to believe, was
rather the point of all of it.

He told me his father still carried the coin, in those years, the same worn half-scrip passed down
through three generations of hands that had used it to teach three different things. Not for the
ritual anymore, mostly — the General said he saw his father spin it on purpose perhaps twice more in
his whole remaining boyhood, both times for reasons small enough not to have earned their own telling.
Mostly Aethelgard just carried it, the General said, the way a man carries a thing he's stopped
noticing he's carrying, until some evening it would surface between two fingers during a quiet moment
and remind the boy, without either of them saying a word about it, of everything the coin had ever
stood for across every version of the lesson it had taught.

I asked him once, near the end of one of these nights, whether he thought his father had known, at the
time, how much of it his son was actually absorbing — whether Aethelgard understood he was building
something in the boy that would outlast every single individual lesson, the way a foundation outlasts
whatever gets built on top of it. The General was quiet long enough that I thought, as I often did with
him, that I'd asked something he wasn't going to answer. Then he said he didn't think his father had
been building anything on purpose at all, in the way I meant it. He thought his father had simply been
trying, every single day, to be a decent man doing a hard job, without much confidence that any of it
was landing the way he hoped — and that the landing had happened anyway, the way it usually does,
somewhere in the space between a father who wasn't trying to be remembered and a son who couldn't help
remembering him.

I don't know how many more of these there are left in him. I've learned not to ask, the same way I've
learned not to push at the ones he's only ever told me once. What I can tell you, having heard this much
of it now, laid end to end, is that I no longer think of Aethelgard Verehimu as a man whose story ended
at the Ceremony that took him, whatever the histories eventually settle on calling that day. I think of
him the way his son plainly still does, decades on — as a man who was, on an uncountable number of
ordinary afternoons nobody else was watching closely enough to remember, simply and stubbornly present.
That was, as far as I can tell, the whole of what he ever actually meant to be.
