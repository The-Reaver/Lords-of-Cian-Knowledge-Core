# Orin Chronicle I: What Can't Be Sold

*Locked canon, Batch 87, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-350`). First entry in Orin's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Onilu (`PH2-029`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`) through Ijoko Chronicle I (`MCD-349`). Narration is
close-third on Onilu throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Completes a first Chronicle entry for all
five Los Angeles territories. Slots into no existing mainline battle — original homage-era material
set in Orin itself.*

---

Onilu had walked away from the kind of career most musicians in Orin would have given anything to
hold, and he had never once, in all the years since, explained that decision to anyone who hadn't
already sat in a room while he played and felt for themselves what none of that career had ever
given him. It wasn't an argument that survived being spoken out loud. It only survived being heard.

The room that night was small — a converted storefront, folding chairs, more people standing than
sitting — and Onilu played the way he always played, for the room and not past it, no eye on
anything beyond the walls. Something happened in that room that had happened in a hundred rooms
before it and would happen in a hundred after: everyone who was actually listening, not just
present, came away carrying something of everyone else who'd been listening beside them. Not a
memory exactly. Closer to a thread, thin and permanent, running between people who'd never spoken a
word to each other before the music started.

The stranger had been leaning against the back wall for most of the set, arrived without
announcement, the way he did everything, listening the way Onilu could always tell the difference
between listening and merely being present for.

A man came in during the last number — not one of the regulars, dressed for a different room
entirely, already scouting the space the way a man scouts a thing he intends to buy rather than a
thing he's come to experience. He waited until the set ended to make his offer: a studio date, real
money, the whole performance captured and pressed and sold to rooms Onilu would never see the inside
of.

"I could get you into every corner of the city with this," the man said, and meant it as generosity.
"People who'll never walk through that door could hear exactly what just happened in here."

"No they couldn't." Onilu said it without heat, the way he said most things. "They could hear the
notes. What happened in this room wasn't the notes."

"It's still music. Music's still music once it's on a record."

"It's still sound. I never said it was only about the sound." Onilu set his instrument down,
unhurried. "Ask anyone who was standing here twenty minutes ago what they're carrying home tonight.
Not one of them will tell you it's a melody. They'll tell you it's each other."

The man pressed once more, the way men with money in their hand generally do before they accept an
answer they didn't come prepared to hear, and Onilu, for the sake of proving a point he'd never had
occasion to prove out loud before, let him record the final number anyway — the whole room's
knowledge, no objection raised. The thread that had bound everyone standing in it that night simply
wasn't there in the recording afterward, when the man played it back the following week to a room of
people who had never stood shoulder to shoulder with anyone in it. Good music. Competent musicians
could hear that much. Nothing more than that.

"You could have stopped me from recording it," the stranger said, once the room had emptied and it
was just the two of them stacking chairs.

"I could have. I didn't want to prove it by refusing. I wanted to prove it by letting him try and
watching him fail to take the one thing that actually mattered." Onilu folded the last chair,
unhurried, the way he did everything. "People assume the gift is the sound. It was never the sound.
Lionel Hampton offered me more sound than I could have used in three lifetimes, and none of it ever
once did what happened in this room tonight for free."

"So the whole gift only works because nobody's trying to get anything out of it."

"The whole gift only works because everybody in the room came for the same reason I did — to hear
each other, not to take something home to sell later." He looked around the empty storefront, chairs
stacked, instruments packed, nothing left of the night but the people who'd already carried it out
the door with them. "That's not a limitation I resent. It's the only reason it was ever real in the
first place."

The stranger was gone before the last light went out, the way he always seemed to be. He left
nothing carved, no phrase repeated hand to hand — only, the following week, one more folding chair
added to the room without anyone quite remembering ordering it, room enough for one more person who
hadn't been there the first time.
