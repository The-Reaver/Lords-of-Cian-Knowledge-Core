# The Copy That Broke What It Borrowed

*Locked canon, Batch 216, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-947`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LIV, wave 18. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The message reached Warehouse Twelve by way of a trader who'd come up from a canal region three
weeks' travel south of the five districts — far enough that the Trench Monarch's reputation had
arrived there only as a secondhand story, retold and reshaped past anything Kanja would have
recognized as his own method.

A group of workers in that southern region, inspired by what they'd heard, had tried to copy the
tally-and-expose approach against their own local owners. They'd gotten the spirit of it right and the
substance of it wrong — publishing accusations before verifying the figures behind them, naming names
on the strength of rumor rather than cross-checked manifests. Two of the accused owners turned out to
have been telling the truth. One of them, wrongly named as a wage thief in front of his entire
workforce, had lost enough standing that his operation collapsed within a month, throwing forty
genuinely innocent workers out of a job that had, whatever its other faults, never actually shorted
them.

The trader hadn't come to accuse Kanja of anything. He'd come because the workers who'd started it,
mortified at what their own imitation had cost, didn't know how to fix it and had heard enough about
the original to hope its source might.

"It's not really mine to fix," Kanja said, when Halst asked whether they were taking the trip south.
"But it happened because people trusted something with my name loosely attached to it, and forty
people lost work over that trust being handled carelessly. I don't think I get to shrug at that just
because I never told anyone to copy me."

He went, with Halst and two others, the three weeks' travel south — the furthest the method had ever
been carried by his own hand rather than by rumor. What he found wasn't a den of bad actors, just
earnest, angry people who'd skipped the slow, unglamorous part of the work — the weeks of quiet
verification that made the accusations mean something — in favor of the faster, more satisfying part.

He didn't take over their effort. He spent eleven days teaching them the slow part: how to cross-check
a figure against three independent sources before naming anyone, how to hold an accusation until it
could survive being wrong. The wrongly-accused owner got a public retraction, printed and distributed
with the same care the original false accusation had lacked. The forty workers didn't get their old
jobs back — that damage was done — but two of the crew's own tally-trained organizers stayed behind to
help them find new ones.

"You can't stop people from copying what you do," Halst said, on the long walk home. "Not once it's
out past where you can watch it happen."

"No," Kanja agreed. "But I can at least make sure the people copying it know what the actual work is,
instead of just the shortest version of the story. The story's the part that travels. The method's the
part that has to be taught, every time, by somebody willing to make the trip."

Garren Hask's ledger carried no formal entry for the southern district — it fell outside anything the
five-district tally system tracked — but Kanja had him write the eleven days down anyway, in a
separate section reserved, from that point on, for whatever the reputation caused beyond where it
could be directly managed.
