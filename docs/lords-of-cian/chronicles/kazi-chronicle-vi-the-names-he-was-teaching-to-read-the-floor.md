# Kazi Chronicle VI: The Names He Was Teaching to Read the Floor

*Locked canon, Batch 288, 2026-09-12 (`MCD-1525`). Sixth entry in Kazi's own Chronicles, its third
with Tunji (`PH2-065`) as protagonist. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but granted no command,
no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention. Narration is
close-third on Tunji throughout. A mentorship entry — Tunji trains successors in his own method, set
after Bakari's already-locked trial (Kazi Chronicle II, `MCD-363`). Slots into no existing mainline
battle — original homage-era material set in Kazi itself.*

---

Bakari had asked Tunji to teach him three separate times before Tunji finally said yes, and even then
he said it the way he said most things — plainly, with a condition attached.

"I'll teach you what I know. I won't teach you to be quick about it. If you want quick, go learn
something else. This only works slow."

They walked the floor together on Bakari's off-shifts, the two of them moving between stations like
men checking machinery rather than people, which was, Tunji pointed out more than once, most of the
point. "Nobody minds being looked at when they think you're looking at the line. Everybody minds it
the second they think you're looking at them."

"That man at the third press," Bakari said, on their second walk. "The one who keeps checking the
clock. I'd have pegged him ready. He looks angry enough."

"Angry's the easiest thing to read wrong. Angry burns fast and goes out faster, unless it's got
something under it holding the fire in place. Watch him again next week. If he's still checking that
clock the same way, angry at the same thing, not a different thing — that's not temper anymore.
That's patience running out, which is a different animal walking around wearing the same face."

Zola, three months further along than Bakari in this particular craft, had her own read on the same
man from a different angle. "He shares his lunch, same as I used to. Every day, same two men, doesn't
ask for it back. That's the part Tunji taught me to look for before I ever learned to watch a clock."

"So which is it," Bakari said. "The clock or the lunch."

"Both, and neither, alone," Tunji said. "One tells you what a man's capable of on a bad day. The
other tells you what's costing him to keep being that way. You need both readings before you've got
anything worth trusting your own name to. I've been wrong plenty of times reading only one."

He told them about the gantry crew, plainly, without softening his own part in it — the three weeks he
had called it green, the man who'd broken two ribs proving him wrong, the fast read at the gate that
had gone half right and half nearly disastrous. He did not tell it as a lesson dressed up to look like
a confession. He told it because it was the actual truth of the work, and because a student who only
ever heard about the times he'd been right would learn nothing about the only part of the craft that
actually mattered — knowing what to do with being wrong, quickly, without pretending afterward that it
hadn't happened.

"So how do you know when you've got it right," Bakari asked, near the end of the third walk, watching
a stranger three stations down carry a heavy tray without being asked, the same unhurried way every
week, never once looking to see if anyone had noticed.

"You don't, not for certain, not ever. You get closer to right the longer you watch, and you accept
that closer is the only kind of right this work actually offers." Tunji nodded once toward the
stranger with the tray, not as an answer exactly, more as an example handed over without further
comment. "That one's been doing that three weeks running, same tray, same hour, never once asked. I
haven't approached him yet. Might be another two months before I do. That's not slowness for its own
sake. That's the only way I've ever found to be sure."

Bakari watched the stranger a while longer after Tunji had already moved on to the next station,
turning the lesson over less as an instruction and more as a question he suspected he'd still be
answering years from now.

"Two months," he said, mostly to himself. "I don't think I have that kind of patience in me yet."

"You will," Zola said, not unkindly, from two stations down. "It's not a thing you're born with. It's
a thing the floor teaches you, the same way it taught him. Give it time."
