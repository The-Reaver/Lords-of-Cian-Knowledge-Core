# Ezio Chronicle II: The Question He Waited Twenty Years to Ask

*UNLOCKED / PENDING APPROVAL. Drafted for Batch 319, 2026-09-30. Second Chronicle in Ezio Valcari's
own Character Chronicle series, opening wave 2. Narrated by Fermand Aurelias (`CC-034`/`VB-024`).
Dramatizes `MCD-194`'s recruitment of Lauris Letitia from Ezio's own side for the first time --
Lauris's own Chronicle I ("The Shape Taught Twice") and later corpus tell her side of the
aftermath; this tells the two-decade decision that preceded it, putting his defining "selects people
for years before acting" relationship pattern directly on the page. Consistent throughout with

I did not know, for the first several years I transcribed his correspondence, that Ezio Valcari
kept a second set of files. The ones he showed me were meticulous and complete, and I believed, as
he clearly intended anyone reading them to believe, that they were the whole of his attention. It
was some years before I understood that the files I never saw outnumbered the ones I did, and
longer still before I understood that one of those hidden files had, by the time I finally learned
of it, already been open for the better part of two decades.

It concerned a Sealbound Directorate external contractor. A woman. Her operational record was, on
its surface, unremarkable among such records -- efficient, well-reviewed by her own handlers, the
kind of file that closes a case and opens no questions. Ezio had read it once, early, for reasons I
never asked him to explain, and had found in it a detail no one else appeared to have noticed: the
precision of her methods did not match the crudeness of the equipment the Directorate's own
procurement records showed her being issued. Someone, he told me once, years after the fact, was
either wildly overqualified for the work she had been given, or had trained somewhere the
Directorate's own records did not account for.

He did not act on the observation. He filed it, and he began, without any further conversation
about it that I am aware of, to read every subsequent report that crossed a desk anywhere near her
name.

*

I want to be precise about what I mean by "began to read," because I think the ordinary word
undersells what I eventually came to understand Ezio actually does with a person he has decided is
worth this particular kind of attention. He does not investigate a subject the way a man
investigates a case he intends to close. He observes the way a physician observes a slow-healing
wound -- at intervals, without urgency, cross-referencing each new report against the last, looking
less for a single decisive fact than for a shape that only becomes visible once enough data points
have accumulated to draw it.

Over the better part of twenty years, by his own later accounting, he watched that shape become
visible three separate times, and set it aside three separate times, because each time he judged
that acting would cost her more than waiting would.

The first time, she had just been reassigned to a facility he judged too heavily watched to approach
without drawing attention neither of them could afford. The second time, her handlers had begun
treating her with the particular, escalating caution the Directorate reserves for an asset it does
not yet distrust but has started, quietly, to measure. Ezio judged that any outside contact at that
moment would confirm precisely the suspicion he most wanted her handlers not to have. The third time,
I believe, he simply was not yet certain enough, and told me once, with the particular honesty he
reserves for admissions that cost him something, that he had let fear of being wrong about a person
masquerade as prudence for longer than the prudence alone could account for.

"You are not a man who is often wrong about a person," I said.

"I have built my entire life on not being wrong about people," he said. "Which is precisely why,
when the stakes were finally high enough, I could not be certain whether I was reading her record
correctly or simply wanted to."

*

The fourth time the shape came clear, it did not fade when he set it aside. It was, he told me, a
single sentence buried in a routine after-action report -- an unremarkable notation, filed by a
superior who plainly did not understand what he was recording, describing the contractor's
"continued operational reliability despite repeated exposure to legacy-system anomalies outside
standard briefing parameters." A phrase built to close a file, not open one. Ezio read it four
times before he understood what it actually meant: that she had been finding things the Directorate
itself did not know were there to find, and reporting them faithfully, to an institution that did
not have the structural humility to recognize what it was being handed.

I have come to believe this is the sentence that actually decided him, more than any single act of
courage or cruelty on her part could have. Not that she was capable -- he had known that for years.
That she was honest with an institution that had never once earned her honesty, and was, by his own
private accounting, very likely to be destroyed for it eventually by people who would never
understand what they had lost.

He did not approach her directly, even then. He arranged, instead, through Kareth-Vassen Aerelin's
own quiet mediation, a meeting at a private coastal residence -- a location chosen, he told me
afterward, specifically because it offered her three separate ways to leave before he ever said a
word, none of which required explaining herself to him.

*

I was not present for the meeting itself. Ezio asked Fermand -- asked *me* -- to withdraw to a side
room, which I have since come to understand was less about privacy than about a final act of the
same patience that had governed the preceding twenty years: he did not want even a trusted witness
present for the one conversation in which he would, for the first time in two decades of careful
observation, finally be wrong or right in the open, with no further file to consult.

He told me afterward only what he judged necessary for the record -- that she had accepted, on
three conditions she named without hesitation, as though she had spent time of her own imagining
what she would ask for if the offer ever came. He did not tell me what he had said to her before
she spoke, and I have never asked him to reconstruct it for me, because I have come to believe some
silences in a man's account are not omissions. They are the last private thing he keeps.

*

I asked him, some years later, whether twenty years had felt like patience or like fear.

"Both," he said. "I have told myself, since, that I was waiting for certainty. I do not think that
is entirely true. I think I was also waiting to be brave enough to be wrong about someone I had
already decided mattered too much to approach carelessly."

I wrote that down exactly as he said it, because I believe it is the truest thing he has ever told
me about the shape of his own patience: that it is not, as it so often reads from the outside,
merely a method. It is, at its center, a man who has built an entire professional life on evidence
choosing, once, to act on something he could not fully document -- and finding, twenty years too
late by any ordinary accounting and, by his own, exactly on time, that he was right.
