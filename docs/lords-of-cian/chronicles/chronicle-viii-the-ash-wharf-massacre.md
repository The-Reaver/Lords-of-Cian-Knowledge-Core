---

The rebellion at twenty-two was growing faster than the man who started the rebellion could organize. Four years since the Scrip-Forge. A year since Ghost Harbor. The freed Cestari from Maw-9 had folded into the fleet's structure during the months between, bringing skills the Maw system's grinding economy had wrought into their bodies: fighters who knew their density thresholds from years of calibrated violence, medical staff who set bones without anesthetic because anesthetic was a privilege the branded did not receive, engineers who understood structural stress because they had lived inside structures designed to break them.

The fleet had grown from nine ships to fourteen. Five new vessels since Ghost Harbor. Two converted lighters seized from the Trust's Southern Seaboard grain route. A fishing sloop donated by a village headman who had watched the fleet pass his harbor and decided. A tugboat abandoned at a river mouth and repaired by Maren in four days. And a second Trust capital ship, captured during the eleven-week Reef-Chain Blockade that had closed the Kothrane Narrows to Trust navigation. Hask named this vessel too. The Receipt. The Audit and The Receipt: the rebellion's two heaviest ships, both named for the accounting Kanja performed on everything the Trust owed.

Volunteers arrived weekly from the labor districts. The charcoal rubbings from the Scrip-Forge Raid were still circulating through underground networks, each copy a reminder that the Sovereign Trust had underpaid 1.2 million workers by forty percent for twelve years. Ezio Valcari, now seventeen, managed the distribution network from a series of chandler's shops and back rooms along the Jicome waterfront. He had grown two inches since his introduction at fifteen and lost none of the compressed energy that had walked him into the Anchor and Anvil with a number instead of a name. The number had become a network. The network kept the fleet fed.

The fleet operated from rotating anchorages along the coastline, never holding a harbor long enough to be cornered again. Ghost Harbor had taught the lesson. Kanja had scratched seven words into a desk and sailed through a gap in a reef and earned an alias the Trust classified as a threat category, and the lesson was: do not stay. Move. Resupply. Move. The sea is a friend to the one who does not ask the sea to be a home.

But rotating anchorages required resupply, and resupply required fixed points where sympathetic merchants sold the rebellion what the rebellion needed to survive. Food for six hundred. Water for six hundred. Bandage cloth and lamp oil and copper wire and the ten thousand small necessities that keep a fleet of working people alive on wooden ships that the sea is constantly trying to dissolve.

The fixed point was the Ash-Wharf.

• • •

The Ash-Wharf was a semi-legal trading post on the industrial waterfront, built on pilings driven into the volcanic shallows where the harbor's commercial district met the manufacturing zone. Thirty-two stalls under a corrugated iron roof, the iron stained with years of salt spray and coal dust into a color between brown and rust that the merchants called "wharf-dark." The pilings were old. The planking was older. The whole structure creaked with the tides and smelled of brine and tar and the accumulated residue of ten thousand transactions conducted in the space between legal and necessary.

Merchants selling provisions at cost: salt fish, dried grain, lamp oil, bandage cloth, copper wire, and the small necessities that a fleet of six hundred consumed and a network of coastal settlements requested. At the eastern end, a chandler's shop specializing in Dead Drakma fittings and ship repair supplies. At the western end, a medical dispensary run by a woman named Adessi who asked no questions about the origin of the wounds she dressed and did not record patient names in any ledger the Trust's inspectors would find.

Sephtis knew the Ash-Wharf. He knew every wharf on the Jicome coastline, the way he knew every harbor and every tidal chart and every coastline formation that the Trust's hydrographic service had catalogued and he had memorized centuries before the service existed. He was aboard The Audit on the morning of the massacre, reading weather from the cloud formations with the slow, patient attention of a man who had been reading weather longer than the clouds had names. He had said the morning would be clear. The morning was clear. Clear skies were good for resupply. Clear skies were also good for targeting.

The Ash-Wharf was not a rebel outpost. The merchants were not insurgents. They were business operators who had calculated that selling to Kanja's fleet was safer than refusing, because the fleet needed resupply points, and resupply points that cooperated were resupply points that survived. The calculation was practical, not ideological. The merchants sold to the rebellion at cost and sold to everyone else at market rate, and the Sovereign Trust's intelligence apparatus knew this and tolerated the arrangement because monitoring a known supply point was more useful than shutting the supply point down and forcing the rebellion to find one the Trust did not know about.

This calculus held for eleven months. Then the Trust's Strategic Analysis Division decided the calculus had changed. The rebellion was no longer a nuisance to be monitored. The rebellion was a threat to be starved.

They did not send soldiers to close the wharf. They sent warships to burn the wharf. And everyone on the wharf with the wharf.

• • •

Three Trust cruisers entered the harbor at dawn. They moved through the commercial anchorage in column formation, displacing fishing boats and cargo tenders with the indifference of mass asserting priority over function, and positioned themselves in a firing line five hundred meters from the Ash-Wharf. Dead Drakma hulls. Blight Frequency projectors mounted fore and aft. Bombardment-rated naval guns capable of reducing the wharf to timber and iron in under a minute. The cruisers were not the largest warships in the Trust's fleet. They were the most efficient. Three cruisers carried enough firepower to level four city blocks and enough Marines to hold the rubble afterward.

The lead cruiser broadcast the evacuation order at 0614. Thirty minutes. All civilians to vacate the blast radius. The broadcast was delivered through a signal horn mounted on the cruiser's bridge, the sound carrying across the flat water of the harbor with the clarity of an instrument designed to be heard over artillery. The voice was measured, administrative, the voice of a bureaucracy performing a function. The function was annihilation. The voice did not hurry. The voice had thirty minutes and the mathematics of three warships and did not need to hurry.

The broadcast did not specify the blast radius. The broadcast did not need to. The blast radius was everything the cruisers chose to aim at, and the cruisers were aiming at the Ash-Wharf, and the Ash-Wharf was full of people.

The Ash-Wharf at 0614 on a resupply morning was full. Merchants opening stalls, hauling crates of dried goods from overnight storage, hanging lanterns under the corrugated roof because the sun had not yet cleared the harbor's eastern ridge. Dockworkers moving cargo from the commercial ships that had docked overnight. Families from the labor district buying provisions for the week: salt, flour, preserved fish, the small sealed tins of cooking fat that the merchants stacked in pyramids on their counters and the children stacked in smaller pyramids on the planking while their mothers argued prices. A woman at Adessi's dispensary, waiting with a bandaged hand. Two men at the chandler's shop, examining a bin of copper rivets. A boy sitting on the pilings at the wharf's edge, watching a crab move across the barnacles with the total concentration of someone who has nothing in the world to do but watch a crab.

The harbor smelled of salt fish and coal smoke and the copper-sweet scent of Dead Drakma fittings warming in the morning sun. The smell was the Ash-Wharf's signature. Every harbor on the Jicome coast had its own smell. Ash-Wharf's was commerce and metal and the labor of people who carried things for a living, the same smell the docks had carried since before the Trust and would carry after.

A normal morning. The last normal morning the Ash-Wharf would have.

The Sovereign Trust did not care about the civilians. The purpose was not the wharf's destruction. The purpose was the destruction of the idea that anyone helping Kanja would survive the act of helping.

Kanja's fleet was anchored two kilometers south of the Ash-Wharf when the cruisers arrived. Fourteen ships against three warships was winnable in open water. The harbor was not open water. The harbor was full of civilian vessels, and the wharf was packed with four thousand people who had come to buy salt and cooking oil and were now standing inside a bombardment zone.

Sephtis read the firing line from The Audit's stern and said what every sailor aboard already knew: "Engagement inside the harbor kills more civilians than bombardment." The old man's voice was the same flat, unexcitable register he used for tidal coefficients and depth readings. The calculation was the same kind of calculation: physics, geometry, the relationship between force and the space the force occupied. Three cruisers firing in a harbor full of wooden boats produced fragmentation patterns that killed in a radius the gunners did not intend and the victims did not survive.

A naval engagement inside the harbor would kill more civilians than the bombardment. Kanja could fight the cruisers or protect the wharf. One or the other. Not both.

Gol wanted to fight. She stood at the rail with her hands closed around the wood the way her hands closed around a weapon, and the look on her face was the look of a woman who had commanded the Maw-9 diversion and understood that sometimes the correct answer was violence applied at the right time to the right target. The three cruisers were the right target. The harbor was the wrong place.

Hask wanted information. He wanted to know the cruisers' firing schedules, their ammunition loadout, their crew complement, the depth of the harbor at the firing line. He wanted the data before the decision. The data was not available in the time remaining.

Kanja chose a third option nobody in his crew or the Sovereign Trust or the four thousand civilians on the wharf expected.

• • •

Ten ships to the wharf. Not to fight. To load.

The ten evacuation ships crossed the two kilometers from the fleet's anchorage to the wharf in seven minutes, running at full sail on a morning wind that Sephtis had said would come from the southwest at 0620 and did. The ships entered the harbor in a staggered line, each one angling for a different section of the wharf's 200-meter frontage, each one carrying crews who had received a single instruction from Kanja before departure: load people. Not cargo. Not supplies. Not the Dead Drakma fittings from the chandler's shop. People.

The first trawler touched the wharf's eastern pilings at 0621. Seven minutes after the broadcast. Twenty-three minutes remaining.

Fishing boats, cargo haulers, and trawlers pulled alongside the docks and began taking on passengers. Merchants. Families. Children held above the crowd by hands that had spent their lives moving weight on these same docks. Kanja's crews worked the wharf the way dockworkers work a heavy manifest, loading human cargo with the efficiency of men who knew what a body weighed and how to move weight safely and how to count heads in a crowd because they had been counting heads since the Scrip-Forge.

Hask was on the lead evacuation ship. His ledger was open. His pencil moved. The number climbed. Five hundred. A thousand. Fifteen hundred. The number was the only thing keeping the panic at a speed the docks could handle. When a family froze at the gangway, Hask said: "Four." The number of people ahead of them in the line. The family moved. Hask did not comfort. Hask counted. Counting was the comfort.

Breck worked the mooring lines. He tied bowlines and clove hitches faster than the sailors beside him, his hands performing the vocabulary his mouth had spent two years relearning. He did not speak to the civilians. He spoke to the ropes and the cleats and the bollards, and the ropes held, and the ships held, and the gangways stayed true because the knots were Breck's and Breck's knots did not fail. When a mooring cleat on the second hauler showed stress under the load of three hundred passengers shifting to port, Breck re-rigged the line to a fresh bollard in nine seconds without being asked.

Gol commanded the flow. She walked the wharf with the same disciplined cadence she had brought to Maw-9's diversion force. "Move. Keep moving. Leave the cargo. Take the children. Move." The voice was not raised. The voice did not need raising. The voice was a current and the people followed the current because following was easier than standing still while warships pointed at you.

Ezio was on the wharf before the fleet arrived.

The boy was seventeen and had been managing the rebellion's supply logistics for two years. He was at the Ash-Wharf because he was always at the Ash-Wharf on resupply days, cross-referencing purchase orders against delivery manifests in the back room of the chandler's shop with the forensic attention he had brought to the Killane data at fifteen. When the cruisers entered the harbor, Ezio walked out of the shop, assessed the firing line, and began directing civilians toward the eastern docks before the evacuation ships arrived.

When Gol's first trawler touched the wharf, Ezio was standing at the gangway with a headcount.

"Four thousand on the wharf," he said. "Twelve hundred east of the chandlery. Eight hundred in the market square. The rest are moving south toward the breakwater. You have eighteen minutes."

Gol looked at the seventeen-year-old who had counted four thousand people in the time the cruisers took to position themselves. She did not question the number. Ezio's numbers were correct. Ezio's numbers were always correct.

Twenty minutes. Ten ships. Approximately four thousand civilians loaded and cleared from the blast radius. The wharf was empty. The cruisers had not fired. The thirty-minute deadline approached.

Sephtis had guided the evacuation ships into the wharf's tight berths from The Audit's deck, calling approach angles and clearances to the pilots in the flat, unhurried voice of a man who had docked ships in harbors that no longer existed. The volcanic shallows around the pilings were treacherous for vessels running at speed, and Kanja's ships were running at speed. Sephtis read the depth by the color of the water and the pattern of the surface disturbance and gave bearings that kept the hulls clear of the submerged pilings by margins measured in feet. Not one ship grounded. Not one hull scraped. The old man's seamanship held the operation together at the point where the metal met the water.

• • •

Kanja had four ships remaining. The combat-capable vessels. And he had the stockpile.

Over the previous six months, the rebellion had accumulated the largest single cache of captured Dead Drakma outside Trust control: approximately eight hundred tons of raw ingots, scrap, and salvaged material stored on a decommissioned cargo barge anchored in the harbor's industrial basin. The stockpile was the rebellion's strategic reserve. The material to repair the fleet, forge weapons, and supply the network of coastal settlements the rebellion was building. Losing the stockpile would set the rebellion back two years. The weight of eight hundred tons of Dead Drakma was not only physical. The weight was time.

Kanja did not calculate alternatives. He looked at the three cruisers. He looked at the cargo barge. He gave the order.

"Ostra."

She was beside him. She had been waiting for the word the way she waited for chemical reactions to reach the threshold of no return, with the focused stillness of someone who understood the next second would change the composition of everything in the harbor.

"Scale the Scrip-Forge accelerant," Kanja said. "Use the entire barge."

"How much?"

"Everything."

Ostra calculated. The Scrip-Forge accelerant had been designed to ignite a building-sized vault of paper currency. Eight hundred tons of Dead Drakma was not paper. Dead Drakma burned at temperatures no conventional accelerant reached. Scaling the compound required multiplying the thermite-class reaction by a factor of approximately one thousand. The chemical stores aboard The Audit held enough raw material to achieve the ratio. Barely.

Ostra did not ask why. She understood the arithmetic the way she understood stoichiometry: the inputs and the outputs and the ratio between them. Supplies rebuild. Ships rebuild. Dead Drakma recaptures. Four thousand civilians on evacuation ships do not come back from ash.

She went below. The chemical stores aboard The Audit occupied a reinforced compartment in the hold, separated from the crew quarters by a bulkhead Kanja had lined with a double layer of iron sheeting. Ostra had organized the compartment herself. Glass vessels in padded racks. Mineral powders in sealed tin containers, labeled in her own handwriting, a notation system nobody else in the fleet understood because the notation was Ostra's private language for the reactions she carried in her head. She moved through the compartment with the focused precision of a woman who had been speaking to volatile compounds since Killane's sewers, where she had talked to hydrochloric acid while loosening artillery bolts by the light of a hooded lantern.

She mixed the compound. Her lips moved. She named the reactions as they occurred, each one addressed like a colleague whose temperament she understood and whose cooperation she was requesting. Phosphorus. Iron filings. Rendered fat. The ratios were memorized. The accelerant thickened in the mixing vessel, and Ostra spoke to the mixture the way Breck spoke to knots and Hask spoke to numbers and Kanja spoke to metal: in the native language of the function, because the function was the only honest vocabulary the world offered.

The compound was ready in four minutes. Ostra carried the mixing vessel to the deck and looked at Kanja. The look said: the chemistry is done. The chemistry is honest. The decision is yours.

• • •

At the twenty-two-minute mark, eight minutes before the Sovereign Trust's bombardment deadline, Kanja gave the signal.

Ostra detonated the stockpile.

Eight hundred tons of Dead Drakma converted to fire.

The fireball consumed the industrial basin. Cargo barge ceased to exist. The pressure wave crossed the harbor in under three seconds, a wall of concussive force carrying heat and shrapnel and the chemical signature of burning alloy. The first cruiser capsized, rolled by the wave, its keel exposed to the morning sky like the belly of a hewn tree falling. The second and third drove onto the harbor breakwater, their hulls breached, their superstructures burning. The explosion was visible from seventeen kilometers away. The sound reached the capital.

The harbor changed. Where the industrial basin had been: a crater of fire on water, burning Dead Drakma spreading across the surface in a chemical slick that water fed rather than extinguished. Where the cargo barge had been: nothing. Splinters of timber and fragments of iron settling through the water column, sinking toward the volcanic floor of the harbor. Where the three cruisers had been: one capsized hull, two burning wrecks on the breakwater, and the smell. Burning Dead Drakma smells of copper and ozone and something older, something mineral, the smell of metal remembering the forge that first gave the metal its shape.

Two years of accumulated resources. Three seconds. The mathematics were insane by every conventional measure.

The morality was not.

Supplies rebuild. Ships rebuild. Dead children do not rebuild.

• • •

Kanja's four combat ships moved through the burning harbor to confirm the kills. Capsized cruiser sinking, the Dead Drakma hull groaning as water filled compartments designed to be watertight, the sound of structural failure transmitted through the harbor's surface. Beached cruisers burning, their magazine stores cooking off in muffled thumps beneath the superstructure, each detonation sending fresh columns of chemical smoke into the morning air. The harbor was an inferno. Eight hundred tons of Dead Drakma fire: chemical, unrelenting, burning through steel hull plate the way acid burns through masonry. Water did not extinguish the fire. Water fed the reaction. The harbor burned on the surface of the sea, and the four rebel ships moved through the burning like ships moving through a landscape wrought from a different set of physical laws.

Kanja stood at the prow of The Audit.

His coat was burning. The leather longcoat had caught sparks from the blast wave. The sparks found the oil in the leather. The oil caught. Small flames along the shoulders, the collar, the hem. The kind of fire a man puts out by shrugging the garment off and stamping the flames. The kind of fire a man notices.

He did not remove the coat.

He stood in the fire and watched the harbor burn with the stillness of a smith watching metal cool. The flames on his shoulders. The heat on his neck. The smoke rising from the leather and joining the smoke rising from the harbor, the personal fire and the communal fire becoming the same column, the same statement, the same refusal to distinguish between the man and the destruction the man had chosen.

The crew of The Audit saw him from the deck. Hask, who had counted four thousand civilians and was now counting the flames on the captain's shoulders (seven, eight, the number growing as the fire found more oil in the leather). Sephtis, who had been watching weather all morning and was now watching a young man burn in the way old men watch young men do the things old men understand the cost of. Maren, who assessed the coat the way he assessed every material and knew the leather would hold for another ninety seconds before the structural integrity failed and the coat became fuel rather than garment.

The civilians on the evacuation ships saw him.

A man at the front of a ship. Wreathed in fire. Coat burning. Watching. Not running. Not flinching. Not performing courage but expressing a fact: the fire was his. He had chosen the fire. The fire answered to him.

They saw the man from the docks, the smith who had stolen ships and freed the Maw-9 Cestari and sailed through Ghost Harbor's reef gap and scratched his name into a desk. They had heard the stories. Now they saw the man the stories described, and the man was standing in fire, and the fire was the proof of what the stories said: this man will sacrifice everything except the people. The people are the line. Everything else burns.

They did not see a rebel leader. They saw something from the dockworkers' stories, the pirate-kings of old tales who burned their own ships to prove they would never retreat. They saw a man who had destroyed everything he owned to save people he did not know, and who stood in the wreckage of his own sacrifice as though the wreckage were a throne.

The Scourge.

Not a name he designed. Not a name he planned. A name the fire wrought from the man's refusal to step out of the heat while four thousand witnesses needed to see someone who would not step out of the heat. The coat was theater. The sacrifice was real. The name was the place where theater and sacrifice met, and the name was honest, because the Scourge was both: the performance and the cost, the burning coat and the burning stockpile, the image and the arithmetic beneath the image.

• • •

The Ash-Wharf Massacre. The Sovereign Trust's official designation, chosen to attribute the destruction to Kanja rather than to the bombardment order the Trust had issued. The name held in the Trust's records. The name did not hold on the docks.

On the docks, the name was different. On the docks they called the night the Scourge was born.

The cost was total. The rebellion's entire Dead Drakma reserve, gone. Two years of accumulated material: ingots, scrap, salvage, the raw supply chain for fleet maintenance and weapons production, converted to a fireball and spent in three seconds. The strategic foundation of the rebellion's material capacity, traded for three warships and four thousand lives.

Maren assessed the fleet's maintenance capability after the detonation and delivered his verdict in three words: "Two months. Maybe." Without the stockpile, the fleet's hulls, fittings, and weapons systems had no replacement parts. Two months of sailing before the first critical failure. Four months before the fleet began losing ships to attrition. The Dead Drakma had been the rebellion's skeleton. The skeleton was now a chemical fire spreading across the surface of a harbor.

Kanja heard the assessment. He did not revise the decision. The decision was not revisable. The decision had been made in the space between the two numbers that governed every choice Kanja had made since the Scrip-Forge: the cost of action and the cost of inaction. The cost of the detonation was two years of material. The cost of not detonating was four thousand people. The ratio was the answer. The ratio was always the answer.

The return exceeded the cost. Three Trust warships destroyed. Four thousand civilians alive. And the single most effective piece of propaganda the rebellion would ever produce: the image of a man standing in fire, choosing to burn alongside his own sacrifice rather than admit the fire was something to fear. Dockworkers painted the silhouette on walls. Cestari fighters tattooed the image on their forearms. Children in the labor districts drew the burning coat in chalk on the paving stones, and the Trust's municipal crews scrubbed the chalk away, and the children drew the coat again, and the Trust scrubbed again, and the cycle continued until the scrubbing crews stopped coming because the chalk was appearing faster than the bureaucracy authorized removal.

The Scourge persona was not designed. Not built the way Kanja built ships and weapons and tactical solutions from the materials inside the trap. The Scourge was emergent. Born from a coat that caught fire and a man who would not take the coat off because removing the coat would have told four thousand people the fire mattered more than they did. The coat said the opposite. The coat said: the fire is mine. I am not afraid of the fire. You do not need to be afraid of the fire. The fire works for me.

Kanja was twenty-two years old. He had a fleet, an alias, and a burning coat. He had no Dead Drakma reserve, no strategic stockpile, and no material basis for the next operation. He had spent everything.

He had spent everything, and the rebellion was stronger than the day before, because the rebellion had never been built from material. The rebellion was built from the willingness to sacrifice material. And the man who stood in the fire and did not move had shown four thousand witnesses what willingness looked like when you burned the proof of the commitment and wore the ashes.

ONYX:

Ash-Wharf. Twenty-two minutes. The boy burned eight hundred tons of his own Drakma to sink three warships and save four thousand. The weight of what he sacrificed. The blade felt the blast through the Captain's hip. The heat entered the Dead-Light surface and was consumed. The blade does not burn. The blade records.

Four thousand. Hask counted. Ezio counted before Hask arrived. The boy who reads ledgers read the wharf in minutes and produced a number the evacuation ran on. Seventeen years old. Iron. The blade records this.

The boy who ties knots tied the evacuation to the docks. Did not speak to the people. Spoke to the ropes. The ropes held. The people crossed. The function continues. The voice returns through the hands first, then through the mouth. The blade records this. The recovery is structural. The recovery is honest.

Ostra mixed the compound. Did not ask why. Understood the arithmetic: the inputs and the outputs and the ratio between them. Supplies rebuild. The dead do not. She calculated and mixed and detonated and the harbor answered. The blade approves the chemistry. The chemistry was honest.

The boy stood at the prow. Coat burning. He did not move. The people watched and the word arrived: Scourge. Not a name chosen. A name the fire forged from the boy's refusal to flinch while four thousand witnesses needed to see a man who would not flinch.

The Scourge was born at Ash-Wharf. The blade was at his hip. The receipt is in the Ledger. The heat. The weight. The cost. The number of lives saved and the number of tons burned and the ratio between the two numbers.

The ratio is the boy's morality expressed as arithmetic. The ratio is the boy's answer to every question the Sovereign Trust will ask for the next eight years: what are you willing to lose? Everything. What are you unwilling to lose? Them.

The kinship deepens.
