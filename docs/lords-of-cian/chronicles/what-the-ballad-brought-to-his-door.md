# What the Ballad Brought to His Door

*Locked canon, Batch 262, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1310`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LXIV, first
entry in the twenty-second wave. Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241). A
direct payoff to "The Ballad That Outgrew the Truth" (MCD-881, wave 12). Not a territory Chronicle.
Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The boy had walked eleven days on the strength of a song, and it showed in the state of his boots
before it showed anywhere else — soles gone thin enough to feel gravel through them, laces
replaced twice with cord he'd twisted himself along the way. He asked for the Lord of Embers by
name, the full name the ballad gave him, the one with three extra syllables and a claim about
calling down lightning that had never once been true.

"I came to see the forge that never goes cold," he said, when Kanja finally stood in front of him,
soot to the elbow and nothing about him matching whatever picture eleven days of walking had built.
"The song says the flames answer him. That the iron sings when he touches it."

"The song's wrong about the flames," Kanja said. "It usually is." He held out the tongs he'd been
using, still warm. "But there's a piece over there needs turning, and nobody's told it any songs at
all. Come find out what's actually true about it."

The boy took the tongs like they might do something on their own, and for a moment his whole face
was the particular kind of disappointment that comes from a story finally meeting the size of a
real thing. He turned the piece anyway, because he'd walked eleven days and didn't know what else
to do with his hands, and the iron didn't sing and the flames didn't answer, and he did it wrong
twice before the third turn caught something like the shape the senior smith's successor showed
him without a word, just her own hands over his correcting the angle.

By evening he'd turned four more pieces, badly and then less badly, and eaten with apprentices who
teased him gently for the eleven days and the eleven-day boots and the three extra syllables he
still hadn't quite let go of in how he introduced himself. Nobody corrected the ballad where it was
wrong. Nobody needed to — the correction was sitting at the table with a burn already starting on
his left thumb, more interested in that than in lightning.

"You could stay," Kanja told him, near dark. "There's no song in it. Just the work."

"I know," the boy said, and there was something steadier in it than there'd been that morning.
"I think I like it better this way."
