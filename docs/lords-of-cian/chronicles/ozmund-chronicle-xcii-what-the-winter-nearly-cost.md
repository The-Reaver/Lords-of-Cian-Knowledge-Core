# What the Winter Nearly Cost

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1821`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCII, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2. Set strictly pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's earlier
boyhood (roughly age eleven), years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard alive,
Ozmund still the House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from accounts given separately by falconer Annis Fairweather (Chronicle XLII) and
Master Alric Fenmoor (Chronicle XLIII), cross-checked against each other decades after the fact — the
strand's first entry to put two of its established figures directly to work together, each stepping
outside their own domain to solve a problem neither could solve alone. No new named characters.*

---

Annis Fairweather told me she had never much liked Master Fenmoor, in those years — not out of any
particular grievance, she was careful to say, but out of the plain incompatibility of two people whose
work rarely crossed, one keeping birds that answered to weight and weather and the other keeping
figures that answered to nothing but themselves. She said the winter that changed that was the hardest
she remembered serving through, a season that came in early and stayed late, with the grain stores
counted wrong by whoever had counted them the autumn before and no way to discover the error until it
was nearly too late to fix.

The shortfall threatened the mews before it threatened anything else, she said, because hunting birds
kept at working weight couldn't simply be cut back the way a stabled horse could — drop a hawk's feed
too far and you lost the bird's edge for the season, or lost the bird outright, and she had eleven of
them to answer for that winter, several belonging to houses who would not have been gracious about a
falconer's failure to keep their investment fed. She'd gone to the steward first, she said, and gotten
nothing but the same bad arithmetic that had caused the trouble, and it was only when she complained of
it within the boy's hearing — not to him, she was careful to specify, simply near him — that anything
changed.

Fenmoor's own account started from the opposite direction. He told me the boy had come to him directly,
not with a question about Scrip mechanics or histories, but with a plain request: could the two of them
work out, on paper, exactly how much grain the House actually had, exactly how much the stable and the
mews and the kitchens each genuinely needed to get through to spring without loss, and exactly where the
gap was worst. Fenmoor said he recognized the exercise immediately as real work rather than a lesson
dressed up as one, and that it was, without exception, the most engaged he'd ever seen his student —
not because the mathematics were difficult, which they weren't, but because for the first time the wrong
answer had a face and a name and eleven birds attached to it.

What Fenmoor produced, over three evenings by candlelight with the boy copying figures at his elbow, was
a rationing table that favored no single part of the household over any other and instead protected
whatever in each domain would be most costly to lose outright — the mews's hunting birds specifically,
whose training could not simply be restarted the way a horse's condition could be rebuilt, and among the
stable's own horses, the breeding mares over the geldings, and among the guard's own stores, nothing at
all, because Kade's men, when Fenmoor asked after them, had told him plainly they'd rather go short
themselves than see a working animal starved for their sake.

Fairweather said she'd expected, when the figures finally reached her, some bloodless account she'd have
to argue with. What she got instead was a table that had clearly been built by someone who'd bothered to
ask her, specifically, what a hunting bird actually needed and why, rather than assuming a stable's
arithmetic could simply be copied over onto a mews. She told me that was the morning her opinion of
Fenmoor changed for good, and that she'd gone to find him afterward, in his own study rather than
sending for him, to tell him so directly — the first real conversation the two of them ever had, she
said, and one neither expected to have over a grain count.

The winter itself, by both their accounts, was closer than either liked to remember. Two birds went off
condition badly enough that Fairweather never fully trusted them again in the field. No horse was lost.
No man went hungry, though several, Fairweather said, ate leaner than they let on, out of the same
instinct Kade's guardsmen had shown when asked. She told me she'd come to understand, looking back, that
what had actually saved that winter wasn't the arithmetic at all, precise as it was. It was that a boy
young enough to have no real authority in the household had gone and gotten the two of them talking to
each other in the first place, because he'd apparently decided, without being told to, that a problem
touching the whole House was everyone's problem to solve together, and had simply acted on that as
though it were obvious.
