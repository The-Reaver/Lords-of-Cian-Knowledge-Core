# What the Court Expected

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1765`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XXXVI — first entry in the second House-politics/broader-life wave. Set strictly
pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's early twenties, years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard
alive, the throne not vacant, none of Book 1's arc yet in motion. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari)
per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructing a story Ozmund told him about his first formal address at
the regional Assize of Harvests. Shows growing political self-possession at full public-court scale —
distinct from Chronicle XIX's household-scale private defiance — through a deliberate refusal of the
ritual's expected performance rather than a rejection of the ritual itself. No new named characters.*

---

The General was particular, telling me this one, about a distinction he wanted me to understand
before I wrote any of it down: that there is a difference between defying what a room wants of you
and simply declining to give it the shape it expects. He said the Assize of Harvests taught him that
difference before almost anything else did, and that he'd spent longer thinking about it, in the
years since, than the occasion itself would seem to warrant.

The Assize was old custom by the time it reached him — older than House Verehimu's own tenure over
the eastern holdings, a yearly gathering of the region's noble houses and Trust assessors at which
each House's heir, once judged old enough to be trusted with the honor, stood before the assembled
peerage and gave account of the season's stewardship. In principle it was an accounting. In practice,
the General said, everyone in that hall over the age of thirty understood it as something else
entirely: a performance of ease. The numbers themselves were rarely disputed and rarely even
listened to closely. What the room actually watched for was whether the young man standing before
them could carry a season's worth of failures and successes alike with the same unbothered charm —
could turn a bad harvest into a wry aside, could make light of a debt without denying it, could be, in
short, entertaining about his own competence. It was, he told me, a test less of governance than of
grace under a very specific and very shallow kind of scrutiny, and every heir he'd ever watched give
the address before his own turn had understood the assignment and played it exactly as written.

He was twenty-two, and the season behind him had been a genuinely difficult one — a blight in two of
the lowland orchards that had cost the House more in remediation than the fruit itself would ever have
earned, a dispute with a neighboring holding over a diverted stream that had gone nowhere good, and a
tenant uprising over grain quotas that had only been settled, weeks before the Assize, through
concessions the Seneschal still privately considered too generous. Aethelgard told him, the night
before, that he needn't dwell on any of it — that the room would forgive a difficult season readily
enough, so long as it was delivered with the right touch, and that a little self-deprecating humor
about the orchards would likely earn him more goodwill than a perfect one would have.

The General said he understood exactly what his father meant, and that he'd even rehearsed, the night
before, the sort of address that would have satisfied it — a few practiced lines about stubborn trees
and stubborn tenants, delivered with just enough rue to be charming. He said he stood in front of a
mirror and heard himself say it and felt, for reasons he couldn't fully name at twenty-two and could
name perfectly well by the time he told me this, that it would have been a kind of lie. Not about the
numbers. About himself.

So he didn't give that address. He stood before the hall the next morning and gave the season plainly
— the blight, its true cost, the decisions that had made it worse before they'd made it better; the
stream dispute, and his own judgment that the House had been in the wrong on it, not gravely, but
enough to owe an apology he then gave, in that room, to the neighboring holding's own representative,
by name; the grain concessions, framed not as generosity but as a correction of a quota that should
never have been set so high in the first place. He offered no charm. He offered no rue. He said what
had happened and what he judged it meant and sat down before anyone had quite finished expecting him
to soften it.

The General told me the silence that followed lasted longer than was comfortable, and that he'd spent
the length of it certain he'd misjudged the room badly. What broke it, eventually, was the Trust
assessor — an old man the General never learned the name of, who had sat through some forty years of
these addresses and said, to no one in particular but loud enough to carry, that it was the first one
he could remember in a decade that hadn't insulted his intelligence. The laughter that followed wasn't
the kind the room usually gave a well-turned joke. It was, the General said, something closer to
relief — as if a number of people present had been waiting, without knowing it, for someone to simply
tell them the truth and let the hall decide for itself what to make of it.

He said Aethelgard neither praised him nor corrected him on the ride home, which by then he'd come to
recognize as his father's particular way of marking something as genuinely his own rather than a
performance given at Aethelgard's own direction. What he did say, once, was that the court would
remember the address for longer than it remembered most of the charming ones — not because it had
been more skillfully delivered, but because it had cost the man giving it something real to say, and
a room that watches performances for a living can always tell, eventually, which ones didn't cost the
performer anything at all.
