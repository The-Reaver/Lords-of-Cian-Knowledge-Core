# The First Class at Warehouse Twelve

*Locked canon, Batch 231, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1031`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LX, wave 20,
closing the wave. Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. The tally-verification method is formalized into a
repeatable, multi-district curriculum for the first time, rather than passed one person at a time.
Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

Garren Hask had been keeping the running count in the back of the ledger for weeks before he showed
it to anyone: fourteen requests from five different districts, each one some variation of the same
question — could someone come teach us how you actually do it, not the story version, the real one.

He put the number in front of Kanja without comment, the way he put every number in front of him.

"We've taught this one clerk at a time," Hask said. "Tavin Greer teaching his replacement. You,
teaching a boy footwork he'll never use for us. That doesn't reach fourteen districts in a year we
don't have."

Kanja looked at the list a long time. Every name on it was someone he'd never meet unless he said
yes, which was itself most of the argument.

They held it at Warehouse Twelve over four days, because it was the site with room enough and history
enough that nobody needed convincing the method had actually worked there first. Sixteen people came
— clerks, dock foremen, two dredge-site overseers who'd been quietly cross-checking their own tallies
for a year without telling anyone why. Hask ran the core of it himself: how to build a cross-witnessed
figure that would survive a hostile owner's lawyer, how to date and store a record so it couldn't be
disputed later, the specific, unglamorous discipline of writing down a number you didn't like exactly
as it was rather than rounding it toward the story you wanted it to tell.

Efa Gol taught the second morning, on how to move a meeting's real business past a patrol without
anyone in the patrol noticing there'd been a real business to hide — not evasion as instinct, the way
she'd first found it, but evasion as a teachable sequence of choices, broken into steps a nervous
first-timer could actually follow under pressure. Pell Ostra taught the third, on protecting records
physically before protecting them legally: what burned fast, what burned slow, where to keep the real
copy versus the copy you let someone find.

Kanja taught last, and taught the least of anyone, on purpose. He told them what the method had never
been: not a story about him, not a name that made anyone safe by itself, not a promise that honesty
would always cost nothing. He told them what it was instead — a discipline that worked whether or not
anyone in the room ever heard the words "Trench Monarch" again, because the tally didn't care who
carried it, only whether it was true.

One of the overseers asked, on the final afternoon, what happened when a district tried the method
without anyone from Warehouse Twelve there to correct their mistakes. Kanja didn't pretend that
question was hypothetical. He'd already answered it once, weeks of travel and forty ruined jobs
proving the slow way was the only way that held. He told them the truth plainly: they would get it
wrong sometimes. The discipline had to be strong enough to survive being gotten wrong and corrected,
not perfect enough to never need correcting.

Sixteen people left Warehouse Twelve with the same core curriculum, four days each, none of it
requiring his presence to repeat. Hask kept a second running count from that day forward, separate
from the wage ledgers — how many districts, how many trained, how far the method had traveled without
him in the room.

It was the first number in the whole ledger that Kanja actually hoped would grow faster than his own
reputation did.
