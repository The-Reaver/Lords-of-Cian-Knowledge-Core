# Kazi Chronicle IV: The List He Kept Before Anyone Asked

*Locked canon, Batch 288, 2026-09-12 (`MCD-1523`). Fourth entry in Kazi's own Chronicles, its first
with Tunji (`PH2-065`) as protagonist rather than Irin. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention.
Narration is close-third on Tunji throughout. Slots into no existing mainline battle — original
homage-era material set in Kazi itself, predating Kazi Chronicle I (`MCD-351`).*

---

Tunji did not keep a list on paper. A list on paper could be found, read, used against the names on
it. He kept his the only place a foreman's search could never reach: in the particular way a man's
shoulders sat when he thought no one was measuring him, in who split their lunch with a stranger
without being asked twice, in whose hands stayed steady when a supervisor raised his voice and whose
did not. Most people on the floor were not ready to be organized. That was not a failing in them. It
was only where they were, on whatever day he happened to be watching.

Zola had been on the line eleven weeks before Tunji said a single unnecessary word to her.

He had clocked her the second week, the way he clocked everyone — not for anything she'd said, since
she said almost nothing, but for the afternoon a younger hire two stations down ruined a batch through
plain nerves and stood frozen waiting to be caught. Zola had reached over without looking up, corrected
the tally sheet before the foreman's next pass, and never once mentioned it to the boy afterward, not
even to be thanked. That was the whole test, as far as Tunji was concerned, and she had passed it
without knowing a test existed.

He did not approach her that week, or the next four after it. He wanted to be sure it wasn't a single
kindness. A single kindness told him nothing about a person; it only told him what they were capable
of on a good day. He wanted the fifth week, the bad one, the one where she was tired and it would have
cost her something real to bother.

It came on a Thursday. A line supervisor docked a full hour from three separate paychecks over a
scheduling error that was plainly his own, and Zola — who had nothing to gain from any of the three
men's hours, who barely knew two of their names — spent her own break walking the docked hour back
through the paperwork trail until she found the supervisor's mistake in black ink, in his own hand,
and set it on his desk without comment.

Tunji found her afterward by the water station, and said the only thing he ever said at this stage,
plain and without ceremony: "You've been doing that work for five weeks without anyone asking you to.
I'd like to ask you to, now, and see what changes when someone finally does."

"Why now," Zola said, not unfriendly, just precise. "Why not the first week."

"Because the first week I didn't know if you were kind or just quiet. Those look the same for a long
time before they don't." He said it without apology, the way he said most things. "I'd rather be sure
about one person than hopeful about ten. Hopeful gets people hurt when the floor finally needs them
and they're not where anyone thought they'd be."

She thought about it longer than most did, which he took as one more mark in her favor. "What is it,
exactly, that you're asking me to be ready for."

"Nothing yet. That's the whole point of asking now instead of later. When something does come — and
something always comes, eventually, on a floor like this one — I don't want to be teaching you who I
am at the same time I'm asking you to trust me with something that matters. I want that part already
done. I want you already standing where you're standing, just — counted, now, instead of only
noticed."

A man three stations over, hired on that same week, said nothing throughout any of it, carrying a
tray of stamped panels past the water station at exactly the moment that would have made eavesdropping
easiest and did not slow to take advantage of it. He had covered Zola's station without being asked,
twice that week, whenever her break ran long — quietly, competently, and without once mentioning it
to her or to anyone.

By the time the plant's next real trouble came, months later, Zola was not someone Tunji had to
explain anything to. She was already standing exactly where the floor needed her, counted long before
anyone had to call her name.
