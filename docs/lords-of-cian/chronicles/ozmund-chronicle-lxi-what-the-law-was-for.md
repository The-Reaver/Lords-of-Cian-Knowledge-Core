# What the Law Was For

Not every disagreement the General told me about his father ended the way the ledger story or the
formula story did, with some tidy correction at the far end of it to make the telling satisfying. This
one he told me the same way he told me the argument over the border dispute — as an open question
neither of them ever closed, except that this time I could tell, decades on, that he still wasn't
entirely sure which one of them had been right.

The boy in question — the General never gave me a name for him, and I've come to think that was
deliberate, that giving him one would have turned a principle into a story about one particular man
instead of the question itself — was a stable hand of perhaps seventeen, caught taking grain from a
House store meant for controlled release across half a dozen tied settlements in a lean season. Not
much. Enough to keep a sick sister fed through the worst of a fever no different, in its way, from the
one that had already taught the General's father what delay cost at Sennick. He didn't run. He didn't
lie when he was caught. He said what he'd done and why, plainly, the way a man says something he's
already made his peace with being punished for.

Custom was clear enough about what happened next — dismissal, restitution owed over time, the kind of
consequence meant to be felt without breaking a man entirely. The General, old enough by then to have
opinions and unafraid of voicing them to his father directly, argued it shouldn't happen at all. The
amount taken was small enough the House would never miss it. The reason was desperation, not greed, and
the entire point of the store's existence was to keep people from starving — punishing a man for using
it exactly the way starvation makes people use things missed the purpose of the store more badly than
his theft ever had. Let it go, quietly, the General argued. It would cost the House nothing and it
would tell every hand and tenant watching that need, honestly admitted, would be met with understanding
rather than the ledger.

Aethelgard didn't dispute the sympathy. The General was careful about that, telling it — his father
never once suggested the boy's hunger wasn't real, or that his sister's fever wasn't reason enough for
a desperate act. What he wouldn't grant was the idea that mercy, extended quietly and by feeling alone,
cost nothing. "The moment I forgive this because it moves me," he said, in words the General repeated
carefully, the way he repeated very little else, "I've made the rule into a question of whether I
happen to like the man standing in front of me that day. The next boy who takes from that store might
move me less. Or I might simply be tired that week, or distracted, or in a worse mood than I am right
now. If mercy depends on my mood instead of the rule, it isn't mercy at all — it's favor, and favor
handed out quietly enough that no one can point to the pattern is the exact thing that turns people
against a House faster than any hard punishment ever does, because they can feel it happening even when
they can't prove it."

The General said he pushed back — that a rule with no room in it for an honestly desperate act wasn't
justice, it was just an easier kind of cruelty to administer, one that let the lord who enforced it feel
consistent instead of feeling responsible. His father didn't have an answer that made the argument go
away. What he had, in the end, was a decision that satisfied neither of them completely: real
restitution, owed and recorded, but spread across seasons rather than demanded whole, and no dismissal
from the House's service at all — a consequence, the General said, heavy enough to be a consequence and
light enough that the boy's sister never went hungry again waiting for it to be paid.

Neither of them called it a resolution, the General told me. His father held that the rule had to mean
something even when it cost a good man something to enforce it. He himself held, for the rest of his
life as far as I ever knew him, that a rule which can only be defended by pointing at what would happen
if everyone broke it isn't the same thing as a rule that's actually right in the one case standing in
front of you. He said he thought about that boy more than almost anyone else his father ever told him
about — not because the outcome had been wrong, exactly, but because he never did manage to decide, in
all the years afterward, whether it had been right.
