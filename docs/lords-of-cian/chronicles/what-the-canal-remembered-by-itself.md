# What the Canal Remembered By Itself

*Locked canon, Batch 216, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-950`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LVII, wave 19. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

She had lived along the Ferrow Cut canal her whole life, long enough to remember it before there was
any tally crew, before any name had been chalked onto anyone's forehead, before "the Trench Monarch"
meant anything to anyone at all. She wasn't a dredge worker herself — she kept a small shopfront near
the tally house, sold thread and mended nets, and watched the canal's business pass her door for
longer than most of the men currently working it had been alive.

She hadn't asked to be interviewed. A young clerk, new to Garren Hask's growing ledger operation and
curious about the district's history beyond what any tally sheet recorded, had simply asked her, one
slow afternoon, what had actually changed along the canal in the time since the reputation started.

She thought about it longer than the clerk expected before answering.

"Wage theft used to be Tuesday," she said. "Not an occasional cruelty — a schedule. Every owner along
this stretch shorted somebody's pay most weeks, and everybody just built their lives around expecting
it, the way you build around bad weather. Nobody wrote it down because writing it down never did
anything. Now it gets written down. Now a man who gets shorted knows there's a place three streets
over where somebody will actually check the number against another number, and more often than not
the owner pays up before it even gets that far, because he knows it will be checked."

"Is that because of him?" the clerk asked. "The Trench Monarch?"

"It started because of him. I don't think it keeps going because of him, not really — not anymore. I
think it keeps going because enough people watched it work long enough that they stopped needing him
in the room for it to keep working. That tally house has a clerk in it every single day whether he's
within a hundred miles of here or not. That's not a reputation anymore. That's just how this canal
does business now."

She said it without much sentiment, the way she might have described any other slow, practical change
— a new bridge, a rerouted supply road — and when the young clerk asked whether she thought it would
last, whether it would survive whatever came next for the man whose name had started it, she shrugged.

"Bridges outlast the men who build them too, if they're built right. I don't know what's coming for
him. Nobody in this district does, and I don't think it's my place to guess. What I know is this canal
used to run on quiet theft as a matter of course, and now it doesn't, and that second fact doesn't
actually need him standing here for it to keep being true. Write that down, if you're keeping a
record. Not because anyone will ask. Because it's the part that's actually going to still be true
long after whatever happens to any one man."

The clerk wrote it down exactly as she'd said it, and Garren Hask, reviewing the growing ledger weeks
later, moved it to the very front of the section reserved for entries that recorded no dispute, no
figure, and no name worth chalking onto anything — the section that had started, quietly, to hold more
of what the alias actually meant than any single confrontation ever had.
