# Daba Chronicle XXII: The Blade That Would Not Hold

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1592`). Entry 22 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series.
Dramatizes Daba's first independent forging attempt and its failure -- a piece he pushes to
completion through will rather than the structural-analysis patience `MCD-1591` first taught him --
extending `MCD-1568`'s forging thread with a genuine setback rather than a straight line of
competence. Introduces one new minor named character, Ilse Corvane (a 1804 medic and fellow
survivor of the settlement Daba founded the network from), collision-checked clean against the full
live ledger. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make
it efficient."*

---

He should have stopped when the color went wrong.

Daba knew that later, with the particular clarity failure always came wrapped in, but at the time
the color had seemed close enough, and close enough had felt, for one reckless quarter of an hour,
like the same thing as correct. Kanja was ten feet away at the second forge, working through his
own piece with the unhurried attention Daba had spent a year trying to imitate, and Daba had wanted,
badly, to finish something before Kanja looked over and saw him still deciding.

The knife was his own design -- shorter than most of 1804's blades, meant to sit flat against a
forearm under a sleeve, a tool for people who needed to look unarmed until the exact moment they
weren't. He'd traded three days' foraging for the scrap that made it. He'd read the metal the way
he'd been taught, found no flaw worth marking, and told himself that meant it was ready.

It wasn't the flaw that broke it. It was the quench.

He plunged it too fast, chasing a hardness he'd decided the blade needed rather than testing what
it would tolerate, and the crack that ran up from the tang wasn't loud so much as final -- a single
short percussive note, nothing like the ring Kanja had taught him to listen for, because there was
nothing left in the metal to ring. A finger-length shard came off the spine and skittered across the
cellar floor, still glowing at one end, and came to rest against Ilse Corvane's boot.

She'd come down with a message about the eastern cell's supply count and had the poor timing to be
standing in the doorway when it happened. The fragment caught the back of her hand on its way past
-- not the strike itself, just the heat still riding off it -- and she hissed and closed her fist
around the burn without dropping the ledger she was carrying, which told Daba more about her than
the burn did.

"I'm fine," she said, before he could get the question out. "Smaller than the one Halden gave me
learning to bandage a real wound instead of a training one." She said it lightly, already reaching
for the water jug by the door, and the lightness was its own kind of mercy, because Daba's stomach
had gone somewhere cold and stayed there.

Kanja crossed the cellar without hurrying, which Daba understood, even in the moment, as a
deliberate choice -- a fast reaction would have made the mistake feel larger than it was, and Kanja
never made a mistake larger than it needed to be. He knelt by the broken pieces, turned the hilt
half over in his palm, and didn't say anything for long enough that Daba nearly filled the silence
with an apology he'd have regretted.

"What were you listening for, at the end," Kanja asked instead.

"Nothing. I wasn't listening. I was watching the color."

"That's the answer." Kanja set the hilt down. "You wanted it finished. So you stopped asking the
metal what it would allow and started telling it what you needed. That's not a forging mistake,
Daba. I've watched you never make it in the field. You'd never tell a treeline what cover it owed
you."

The comparison landed harder than any scolding would have, because it was true, and because it
meant the failure wasn't a gap in what he could learn -- it was the one place, so far, where he'd
let wanting something get ahead of reading it.

Ilse wrapped her hand with the calm economy of someone who had done far worse to herself by
accident, glanced at the ruined blade, and said, not unkindly, "Next one, maybe don't rush it on my
account."

Daba picked up the two halves of the knife -- useless now, good only for scrap -- and turned them
over in the coal-light, memorizing the exact place the crack had started, the way he'd have
memorized the exact place an ambush had failed, so that the next attempt would not forget what this
one had cost.
