# What the Lantern Watch Prayed For

*Locked canon, Batch 110, 2026-09-10 (`MCD-379`). Sovereign Ghost of the Great Sea Alias
Chronicle III, closing the wave. Rebellion era, aboard a Trust supply vessel somewhere off the
Great Sea's contested waters. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The lantern watch on a Trust supply vessel was the loneliest post in the navy, and the young sailor
assigned to it that season had learned, the hard way, that loneliness and fear kept exactly the same
hours.

He had never seen *The Audit*. Neither had most of the men he served with, though everyone had a
story secondhand — a cousin's shipmate, a friend of a friend, always someone once removed who'd
supposedly watched her run silent and dark past a hull that should have spotted her at any range.
What the sailor had instead was six weeks of night watches spent staring at empty water, waiting for
a shape that might never come, learning to distrust every shadow the moon threw across a swell.

"You pray for storms, this route," an older hand told him, the third week, watching him flinch at a
gull's cry mistaken for a hull scrape. "Storms you understand. Storms have rules. The ghost's got no
rules anybody's ever written down that hold up twice."

"Do you think she's really out there? Or is it just — men telling stories to explain their own
watches going wrong?"

"Both, probably. That's the part that gets you. A real ship did sink six of ours at Ghost Harbor.
That part's not a story, that's a casualty list with names on it. But every empty watch since then,
every gull cry and moon shadow — that's just us, doing her work for her without her needing to lift
a finger. She doesn't have to actually be out here most nights. She just has to have been out here
once, real enough, for every quiet night after to feel like she might be again."

The sailor thought about that for the rest of his watch, staring at water that stayed, as it had for
six weeks running, entirely and uneventfully empty. He would go two more full seasons on that route
without ever once sighting anything he could swear to under oath, and he would still, decades later,
tell his own recruits to pray for storms on the Great Sea route — not because the ghost was
everywhere, but because he had learned, the hard way, that a fear real enough to survive on its own
reputation didn't need to show itself again to keep doing its work.

"She got six ships and a legend for the price of one battle," he'd say, when the newer sailors
asked. "The legend's the part that's still working. It'll probably still be working long after
nobody left alive actually remembers the battle that started it."
