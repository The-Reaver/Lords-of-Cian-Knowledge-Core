# The Tribunal That Tried to Name Him

*Locked canon, Batch 259, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1229`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle LXIV, wave 22, first
entry. Age 98, V3 gear. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The Sovereign Trust admiralty court did not summon him, because there was no summons that would
reach a man with no fixed name, no home port, and no flag anyone could serve papers to. Instead it
summoned the question itself: three seated justices, two junior advocates, and a public gallery half
full of merchant-house observers, convened to decide, once and for all, what the Scourge legally
was.

A stateless pirate, argued the Trust's own advocate, subject to summary execution on capture under
any admiralty's law, precedent or no precedent — cite the Gale Straits seizures, cite the burned
manifests, cite two decades of lost cargo insurance claims filed against an entity the insurers
themselves couldn't agree how to describe on the form. An unrecognized belligerent, countered the
second advocate, representing three merchant houses who had quietly petitioned to be heard — a
combatant in an undeclared conflict against an institution, the slave trade, that the Trust's own
statutes already condemned on paper if rarely in practice, and therefore owed the protections any
belligerent was owed, however inconvenient that protection proved to enforce.

Garren Hask's sealed testimony — already admitted once, in a different court, over the Scrip-Forge
evidence — was entered again here, not because anyone expected it to settle the question, but
because it was the only documentary record either side could agree was genuine. It didn't settle
anything. The justices read it, weighed it, and disagreed with each other in three separate written
opinions that contradicted one another on every point that mattered and agreed on exactly one: no
ruling could be enforced against a party the court could not locate, summon, or compel, and so
whatever the Scourge legally was, the court's own finding would remain, in the driest language the
senior justice could produce, "of academic rather than operative effect."

He heard about the ruling three weeks later, secondhand, from a trader who'd sat in the gallery and
found the whole proceeding almost funny — three learned men arguing for a full season over what to
call a shape none of them had ever managed to pin down long enough to ask. Kanja turned the report
over for a while, unbothered by any of the words it used for him, until he came to the one thing the
justices had gotten right without meaning to: the ambiguity itself was the only protection he had
ever actually needed. A tribunal that couldn't agree what he was couldn't agree what to do about him
either.
