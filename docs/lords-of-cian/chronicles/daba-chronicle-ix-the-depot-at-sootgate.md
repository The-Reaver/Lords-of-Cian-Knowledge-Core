# Daba Chronicle IX: The Depot at Sootgate

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1579`). Entry 9 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes 1804's
first real operation -- small, precise, and deliberately unwitnessed -- proving the doctrine-over-mass
method in practice for the first time and extending `MCD-1567`'s "anything large enough to be seen is
large enough to be burned" lesson from stated principle into demonstrated result. Reuses Daba, Sael,
Nessa, Corvin, and Farro (`MCD-1576`-`1578`); introduces no new named characters. Abad's approval:
"rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

The depot at Sootgate held nothing the Trust considered worth a proper garrison -- six guards on a
rotating watch, a grain and fodder store feeding two nearby outposts, and a records office where a
single overseer logged shipments in a ledger nobody above him ever read closely. It was, in every way
that mattered to the people who built it, an unremarkable place. That was exactly why Daba chose it.

He had spent three weeks deciding against three other targets before he settled on Sootgate, and he
explained his reasoning to the four of them the night before, laid out plainly rather than held back as
instinct the way some leaders kept their reasons close. A garrison worth attacking was a garrison worth
avenging, and an avenged target brought reinforcements, and reinforcements brought searches, and
searches were the one thing 1804 could not survive in its first year of existing. Sootgate would not be
avenged. Sootgate would barely be noticed missing until the overseer's own monthly count came up short,
and by then there would be nothing left to trace it to.

"We're not taking the depot," he told them. "We're taking one night's worth of what moves through it,
and we're taking it in a way that looks like theft, not war. A hungry district stealing from a supply
line is a nuisance a clerk writes up and forgets. A resistance cell striking a Trust facility is a
report that reaches Central Karkosa by the end of the week."

Sael had already walked the depot's perimeter four times over three separate nights, timing the guard
rotation to the minute, marking which of the six watchmen walked his round with real attention and
which walked it the way tired men walk a round they've walked a thousand times before. Nessa had spent
two days in the district market listening rather than asking, learning which families nearest Sootgate
had gone hungriest that season, because the grain, once moved, needed somewhere to go that would not
immediately be searched. Farro had mapped three separate routes away from the depot along roads he
already had a legitimate reason to travel, so that a wagon moving at an unusual hour would draw no
attention from anyone who might otherwise wonder why a supply man was out so late.

Corvin's part was the smallest and, Daba suspected, the hardest for him to accept as enough. He was to
do nothing but wait at the depot's rear gate with a set of cut tools and a covered handcart, ready to
move the moment Sael gave the signal that the tired watchman had reached the far end of his round. No
confrontation. No fighting. If anything went wrong, his single instruction was to leave without taking
anything at all, rather than risk being seen.

It went, in the end, almost exactly as Sael had timed it. The tired watchman reached the far end of his
round eleven minutes past midnight, as he had every night Sael had watched him. Corvin was through the
rear gate in under two minutes, the fodder store's poorly maintained lock giving way to his tools
without a sound loud enough to carry. He took less than a quarter of what the store actually held --
Daba's instruction, given twice to make sure it was understood, since a full quarter looked like an
accident of bad accounting and a full half looked like a robbery. By the time the tired watchman turned
back along his round, the rear gate was closed again, the lock reset crookedly enough to pass a casual
glance, and Corvin was three streets away with the handcart already half-unloaded into the first of
Nessa's chosen families.

Daba did not go near the depot himself that night. He sat instead in the cellar with a single lamp,
counting the minutes the way he had once counted doorframes, and did not let himself believe it had
gone well until Sael came back to tell him so with her own mouth.

The overseer's monthly count, when it finally came up short three weeks later, was logged as spoilage
-- a damp season, a bad batch of feed, an unremarkable line in an unremarkable ledger that no one above
him would ever read closely enough to question. No search followed. No reinforcements arrived. Sootgate
continued exactly as it had, six guards on a rotating watch, one tired man walking the same round he
had walked for years, entirely unaware that anything about his district had changed at all.

"That's it?" Corvin asked, the night the news came back that nothing had come back -- no alarm, no
inquiry, nothing. He sounded almost disappointed, the way a man sounds when he has braced for a fight
and been handed silence instead. "We did all that, and nothing happened?"

"Nothing happening is the operation," Daba said. "Everything happening is what happened to the Ashgate
irregulars."

Corvin was quiet a moment, turning it over the way he had turned over the argument in the cellar weeks
before. Then, slowly, something that was almost a smile crossed his face -- not triumph, something
quieter than that. "Four families ate this month who wouldn't have," he said. "And nobody even knows to
be angry about it."

"That's the whole of what we're building," Daba said. "Not a war anyone can see. A hundred nights like
this one, that never once look like anything at all."
