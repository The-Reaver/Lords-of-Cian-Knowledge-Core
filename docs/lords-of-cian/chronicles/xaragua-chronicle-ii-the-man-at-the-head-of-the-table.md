# Xaragua Chronicle II: The Man at the Head of the Table

*Draft, 2026-09-06, pending Abad's review before any lock. Second entry in Xaragua's own Chronicles,
chronologically the *first* of the two in-universe -- it precedes Xaragua Chronicle I and Yara Chronicle
I, and is written here to retroactively establish the mechanism that let Kanja move through both of
them. Protagonist: Arturo "de la Muerte" Salvatierra Duho -- two surnames, per Abad's direction, the
Spanish one kept alongside a Taino one added to it ("Duho," the real Taino word for a cacique's
ceremonial seat of judgment, chosen deliberately to echo the Chronicle's own title) -- a Xaragua native
who leads the Five Families
spanning all five NYC-equivalent territories (Xaragua, Areito, Yara, Guanin, Boriken) as their citywide
peacekeeping figurehead -- introduced here for the first time, not yet locked as a PH2- character. His
signature ability, Blood Debt (biochemical control: protective clotting/detox at close range, its dark
reverse almost never used, and his own arrested aging as its personal application), is shown in effect.
Departure from the established pattern: Kanja is named on-page here, a deliberate choice -- Arturo is
specifically the one figure in NYC who comes to know him properly, which is the whole point of a
"point of contact." The individual territory leaders (Ogoun Xarey, Yalokona) still never learn his name
in their own Chronicles; this one explains why Arturo could vouch for a stranger neither of them ever
identified. Kept period-appropriate per the world's locked pre-industrial tech level (PH2-049) -- no
electric light, radios, firearms, or engines; oil lamps, acoustic instruments, blades.*

---

A man who sits at the head of a table earns that seat one of two ways -- by being the one everyone
is afraid to sit across from, or by being the one everyone trusts to carve the meat fairly. Xaragua
had tried the first kind for two generations. It had buried all of them.

The back room above the dockside cantina smelled of tallow smoke, rum gone sweet in old wood, and
the green bite of plantain leaves someone had wrapped the evening meal in an hour before. A single
oil lamp burned low at the table's center, throwing more shadow than light, which was, Arturo had
long ago decided, exactly the correct amount of light for a room where men came to say things they
would deny having said.

He was not old to look at. Forty, maybe, if you didn't know better, and everyone in that room knew
better. He sat with his hands flat on the table, palms down, the way a man sits when he wants both
sides of a room to see he isn't reaching for anything.

The stranger had been three days making his way toward that table, and every one of those days had
been reported to Arturo in more detail than the stranger likely imagined. A man who asked no one for
anything and took nothing that wasn't offered. A man who'd stopped a beating in the fish market
without raising his own hand past a gesture. A man the dockhands had started calling, before anyone
official had said a word about him, *el que no necesita nada* -- the one who needs nothing.

Arturo did not believe in men who needed nothing. He believed in men who hadn't yet been asked the
right question.

The stranger sat when told to sit, which was itself a piece of information -- an equal doesn't sit
on command, and a subordinate doesn't sit that unbothered by the asking. This was a third thing
Arturo hadn't yet named.

"You've been walking through my streets for three days," Arturo said. Modern Laconic, no ornament in
it. "Asking nothing. Taking nothing. That's either a very patient man or a very careful one."

"Both, most days."

"Which is this one."

"Careful. I don't know yet if I want anything from Xaragua, and I don't take what I haven't decided
I want."

Arturo studied him across the lamp's low light -- not the face first, the way most men studied a
stranger, but the stillness underneath it, the specific quality of a man who has stopped needing to
perform calm because calm stopped being a performance for him decades or centuries ago. Iron does
not announce itself. It simply doesn't move when lesser things would.

"Then tell me what you might want, hypothetically, and I'll tell you what it costs."

"Passage. Not through Xaragua only. Through all five houses, whenever I have reason to come."

A silence that Arturo let sit exactly as long as it needed to. Every man who'd asked him for
something in this room had asked for money, or protection, or a debt forgiven. No one had ever asked
for the whole city's five doors at once, as though it were a single reasonable thing to want.

"That's not a small thing to ask a stranger's way into."

"I'm not asking you to trust a stranger. I'm asking you to test one, and decide after."

Something in that answer earned the stranger a longer look than the first one had. Arturo drew the
knife he kept at his belt -- not toward the stranger, across his own open palm, unhurried, the way a
man performs a gesture he has performed enough times that it has stopped being either brave or
reckless and become simply true. Blood welled along the cut for the length of one breath.

By the second breath, it had stopped. By the third, the line across his palm had already begun to
knit shut, no bandage, no salve, nothing but the specific stillness in his own blood answering to
whatever he'd decided about it years before anyone in this room had been born.

"That's the first thing you should know about this table," Arturo said, closing his hand and setting
it flat again. "Nothing bleeds here that I haven't decided should. Not from anyone sitting at it.
That's not a boast. It's the actual physics of the room you're sitting in."

"And the ones who don't sit at it."

"Are not my concern until they try to make themselves one." No warmth in it, and no cruelty either
-- a fact laid down flat, the way a man states a load he's tested with his own weight more times
than he can count. "I didn't build five families out of Xaragua's own dead by being soft about who
gets protected and who doesn't."

"Kanja," the stranger said, finally, unprompted -- the first thing about himself he had offered
freely in three days. "If we're trading things that cost something to say."

Arturo turned the name over once behind his own stillness, filing it the way he filed everything
that might matter later. He did not ask what it meant, where it came from, or what waited behind
it. A name freely given, in a room built on things carefully withheld, was itself the whole answer
to whatever test the evening had actually been.

"Kanja," he repeated. "You'll have your passage. Xaragua, Areito, Yara, Guanin, Boriken -- five
doors, one key, and the key is that I've decided you're not the kind of storm that needs stopping
at the water's edge." A pause, the only warmth he spent that night, and even that one measured close
to the vest. "I won't tell the other four houses your name. That's not distrust. It's that a name
travels faster than a man, and I'd rather they judge you the way I did -- by what you do at their
own table, not by what I already decided at mine."

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's careful. Fair is what men say when they want credit for the same caution
everyone else already practices." Arturo stood, the meeting's actual end arriving the way all his
endings did -- without ceremony, because ceremony was one more thing that could be counterfeited and
carefulness, in his experience, could not. "Xaragua raised me before it made me its own kind of
weapon. I know what it costs a stranger to be trusted here. Don't make me count that cost twice."

He did not offer his hand to shake. Some men, Kanja understood without needing to be told, save
their touch for the moments it actually means something.
