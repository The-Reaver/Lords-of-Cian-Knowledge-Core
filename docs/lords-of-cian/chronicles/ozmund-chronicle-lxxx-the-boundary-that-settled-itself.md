# The Boundary That Settled Itself

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1809`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXX, House politics strand wave 3, first entry.*

---

The General told me once that the smallest disputes were, in his experience, the ones most likely
to outlive everyone who remembered how they'd started, and that he'd learned more from settling an
argument over a hedgerow than he ever learned from a single Assize of Harvests. I laughed at that,
the first time he said it. By the time he'd finished telling me this one, I understood he hadn't
been exaggerating.

The Corwyth and Adderwell households had farmed adjoining strips of Verehimu lowland for four
generations, close enough to the manor that both families paid their tithes directly rather than
through a district steward, which meant every quarrel between them eventually found its way, sooner
or later, to someone actually wearing the Verehimu name. The dispute itself was almost absurdly
small by the time it reached Ozmund, twenty-three that year and newly given standing authority over
minor boundary matters: a hawthorn hedge, planted by some Corwyth ancestor two generations back
along what he'd believed was his own line, had grown, slowly and without anyone quite noticing,
some three feet onto Adderwell land. Three feet of hedge. Neither family disputed the fact of it.
What they disputed — bitterly, by the time Ozmund arrived to hear it, with each side able to recite
a litany of older grievances the hedge had apparently come to stand for — was what the encroachment
meant, and who owed whom for it, and whether it should be cut back, replanted, or simply declared,
after forty years, to have earned its place.

The General said his father's own instinct, offered the night before as advice rather than
instruction, had been to rule on it outright — to measure the old boundary markers, which still
existed in the manor's own surveys, and simply tell both families where the true line stood.
Aethelgard's reasoning was sound as far as it went: a clear ruling, delivered with the House's full
authority behind it, would end the dispute cleanly and leave no ambiguity for either family to keep
arguing over. Ozmund said he'd agreed with the reasoning and still, riding out the next morning,
found himself unable to shake the sense that a ruling handed down from above would settle the hedge
and do nothing at all about the forty years of accumulated resentment sitting underneath it — that
he'd be fixing the symptom the House's own records could measure while leaving the actual wound,
whatever it was, exactly where he'd found it.

So he didn't rule. He rode out to the hedgerow itself, found Bram Corwyth and Iona Adderwell already
waiting on opposite sides of it with the particular stiff patience of two people who had rehearsed
their arguments too many times to still believe in them, and asked each, in turn, to walk the line
with him — not to argue the history, which he made plain from the outset he already had access to
and didn't need repeated, but simply to tell him, standing at the hedge itself, what a fair
settlement would look like to them if the other weren't there to hear it. The General said the
answers surprised him. Bram Corwyth didn't want the hedge cut back at all — he wanted, it turned out,
an acknowledgment that the encroachment had been an honest mistake and not the slow, deliberate
land-grab Adderwell's household had spent a decade quietly accusing him of. Iona Adderwell didn't
particularly want the three feet of ground back either, not in any way that would have justified the
years of bitterness — what she wanted was for her own family's patience in not escalating the matter
sooner to be recognized as patience, rather than read, as she believed it had been, as weakness.

He gave them both exactly that, and nothing more. He had the hedge trimmed back to the true line, not
as a punishment but as a plain correction of an old mistake both families could agree had been
innocent once it was named aloud; and he said so, standing between them, in words neither family
could later claim the other had put in his mouth — that an honest error had grown, unattended, into
something neither side had actually wanted, and that the fault, such as it was, belonged to forty
years of silence rather than to either household. He didn't ask them to shake hands. He told me he'd
considered it and decided against it, on the theory that forced reconciliation tends to curdle faster
than the grievance it's meant to replace. What he did instead was simply stay another hour, talking
with each family in turn about ordinary things — the season's yield, a sick horse, nothing to do with
the hedge at all — until the silence between the two households in his presence had stopped feeling
like a truce and started, however slightly, to feel like neighbors.

The General said the two families never became close, not in any way he'd claim credit for. But the
hedge stayed where he'd put it, and the old accusations stopped circulating, and when he asked after
the matter years later, an old steward told him the Corwyths and Adderwells had taken, without either
side apparently noticing they'd started, to sharing a well-digger's fee once a year rather than each
hiring their own. He said that was the whole of what he'd hoped for, and more than he'd expected: not
friendship, which no ruling could manufacture, but the simple, durable absence of a reason to keep
counting old wounds.
