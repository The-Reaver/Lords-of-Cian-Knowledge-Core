# What He Learned From Being Underestimated

The General laughed, telling me this one, in a way I didn't hear from him often — not the dry amusement
he usually allowed himself, but something closer to real delight at his own younger foolishness, or
perhaps at how little the world had expected of him at the time and how much that had, just once,
worked entirely in his favor.

House Dellark held the upper watershed that fed three of the Verehimu lowland holdings, and the two
Houses had spent the better part of a generation in a slow, mostly civil dispute over how much of that
water Dellark was entitled to divert for its own mills before the flow reaching Verehimu land dropped
below what the season's crops required. It was not, the General said, a dramatic conflict — no one had
come to blows over it in living memory — but it was a real and recurring one, renegotiated every few
years as Dellark's milling operations grew and the old agreements strained further to cover them, and
Aethelgard had brought his son into that year's negotiation as an observer, mostly, he suspected, to
give him practice sitting through a long room without saying anything useful.

Lord Corvain led the Dellark party himself — a broad, genial man some twenty years Aethelgard's senior,
who greeted Ozmund on arrival with the particular warmth reserved for young men judged pleasant and
harmless, asked after his health and his hunting rather than his opinion on water rights, and spent
the first hour of formal negotiation addressing every substantive point to Aethelgard directly, as
though the younger Verehimu beside him were furniture with good manners. The General said he found
this, at the time, only mildly irritating. What he hadn't expected was how useful it would turn out to
be.

Because Corvain, having decided early that Ozmund was not a man worth watching his words around,
began — over the long middle stretch of the negotiation, in the easy, expansive way of a man who has
stopped performing caution because he no longer believes anyone in the room requires it of him — to
talk. Not carelessly, exactly; nothing he said would have read, on its own, as an admission of
anything. But he mentioned, in passing, while making a point about the fairness of Dellark's current
allocation, that this year's mill expansion had already been financed and was proceeding regardless of
how the negotiation went, which meant Dellark's actual leverage — the threat that had underwritten
every prior round of these talks, that Dellark might simply divert more and dare Verehimu to escalate
— was, for this particular season, closer to bluff than fact. He mentioned it to Aethelgard. He never
once glanced at the quiet young man across the table while he said it, because there was, in his own
understanding of the room, no reason to.

The General said he didn't say anything himself, not then. He watched his father not react to it
either — Aethelgard was, by his own account, a genuinely skilled negotiator, and had likely caught the
opening as it passed — but the room's rhythm didn't change, and the talks continued another two hours
along much the terms Dellark had arrived expecting to hold. It was only afterward, in the carriage
home, that Ozmund raised it with his father directly: that Dellark's true position this season was
weaker than its posture, that a firmer counter on the allocation than they'd offered would likely have
held, and that it might be worth reopening the point through a follow-up letter before the terms were
formally set. Aethelgard sent that letter within the week. Dellark, without the leverage it had
implied but never quite possessed, conceded a larger share of the flow back to the lowland holdings
than it had given up in a negotiation in over a decade.

The General told me he thought about that negotiation for years afterward, and that what he'd taken
from it wasn't triumph, exactly — Corvain had done nothing dishonorable, only underestimated a young
man the way most of that room would have, and paid an ordinary cost for an ordinary assumption. What
he'd taken from it instead was a small, specific lesson about listening: that a man who has decided in
advance you're not worth his caution will often tell you, without meaning to and without cost to
extract it, exactly what he'd never have told you if he thought you were listening. He said he didn't
build any grand design out of the discovery, not then — it was one negotiation, one lucky opening, and
he was careful, telling it to me, not to let it sound like more than that. But he said he never quite
forgot how much a room could hand you, for free, the moment it stopped believing you were worth
watching.
