# The Story Told for Coin

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Bane Alias Chronicle XXVII, closing the ninth wave.
Rebellion era, age 19, months after the Black Trench. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The traveling storyteller had never met Bane, had never come within a hundred miles of the Black
Trench, and had built a modest but genuine living in three market towns telling an account of the
ravine battle so embellished — forty men single-handedly, a mountain moved by will alone, a voice
that could stop hearts at range — that the real crew members who occasionally heard it in passing
usually walked out laughing before the second act.

Bane heard it by accident, passing through the third of those towns in plain clothes, unrecognized,
and stayed for the whole performance out of something closer to curiosity than offense.

"You've got the mountain wrong," he said afterward, finding the storyteller counting the evening's
coin behind the tavern where he'd performed. "It didn't move. We collapsed it on purpose, on timber
we'd cut days in advance. That's not will. That's carpentry and patience."

The storyteller went pale for exactly as long as it took to place who was speaking to him, and then,
to his credit, didn't grovel. "The carpentry version doesn't fill a room. I've tried telling it
straighter. People want the mountain to move."

"I understand why. I'm not here to stop you telling it." Bane took a seat across from him, unhurried,
the coin still uncounted between them. "I'm here because you're making a living off a version of me
that isn't true, and I'd rather understand what that costs, and what it's worth to you, before I
decide whether it costs me anything I actually mind losing."

They talked the better part of an hour — the storyteller defending his craft with more genuine
conviction than Bane had expected, arguing that the embellished version did real work even if it
wasn't factual, that frightened garrisons who'd never face him directly still stood down faster
because of stories exactly like his, that the mountain moving by will alone had probably saved more
lives through pure reputation than the true account of patient carpentry ever would on its own.

"You're not wrong about what it's worth," Bane admitted eventually, and left the coin uncollected,
untouched, exactly where he'd found it. "I still don't love hearing myself made into something I'm
not. I'll live with it anyway. Just don't ever let me hear you tell someone I killed people I
didn't."

"I never have. Ask anyone who's heard the whole set." The storyteller managed something like a
grin, still shaken, already composing the next embellishment before Bane was fully out the door. "A
mountain moving is one thing. I know exactly where the line is."
