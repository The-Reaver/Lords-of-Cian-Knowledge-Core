# Anirak Collection III: The Body the World Was Not Built For

*Locked canon, Batch 373, 2026-10-04 (`MCD-1900`). Clean on the fourth independent review. Abad's approval: "approved". Third
entry of Anirak's Collections (Game Plan pitch 1, locked at Batch 372), closing the
launch wave. Close-third on Anirak per `VB-065`. The late Long Mask, roughly Kanja 300. Ren (Abyss,
Ren Oshaal) comes aboard an adult, already recruited off the page, and she takes him as her charge
unasked, the undated origin of their pairing (`CC-114`). Notable register, no kill, no fight. No new

He came aboard on the morning launch, in fog.

She felt the launch before she saw it. A small hull answering the swell slow, like a laden boat. Six
oarsmen laboring at it as if one more cask sat in the bottom of it, a cask of lead.

The launch came alongside. The oarsmen shipped their oars fast and crowded to the far gunwale, all six,
so the boat listed. In the stern a young man sat alone with a sea-bag on his knees.

Hamund, at the rail beside her, said it under his breath. "The one from the islands."

Word had come the week before. Someone had been found on the shelf islands and was being sent out.

The young man came up the side ladder. The rungs creaked under him, every one, as if he weighed a third
again what he looked.

He stepped onto the deck.

The planks groaned. A long low sound deep in the wood, the sound a hull makes in a heavy sea. The deck
lay flat calm.

The men nearest the ladder stepped back a few paces. Their bodies did it for them. Something in the air
around him leaned on them, and they leaned away.

He watched them do it. His face stayed as it was. He had seen it before, by the look of him.

• • •

The bosun cleared him a berth under the forecastle, on deck, by the anchor housing. Nobody told him to.

By the end of the first day a shape followed the young man everywhere on the deck.

A ring. A dozen paces across. Empty.

Where he walked, the ring walked with him. Men coiling line drifted out of it. Men at the pumps found
reasons to work the other pump. At the evening meal the crew sat close along the benches amidships,
elbow to elbow. He took his bowl to the bow and sat on the anchor housing with the ring around him and
the dark coming on.

The planks under the anchor housing groaned through the meal. Low. Steady. A ship talking to itself
about a weight.

His name was Ren. Somebody had it from the launch crew.

• • •

She watched him for three days.

The young man ate alone. Slept alone. Worked the pumps alone, at the hours nobody else took. He moved
through the ship slowly and set his feet down with care, the way a man walks on ice.

He made no complaint. He asked nobody to stand nearer. He asked for nothing.

Her three watched her watch him. Edda said nothing. Odile said nothing. Hamund said, on the second
night, "They're afraid of him."

"They're heavy near him."

"Same thing, to a body."

She had no answer to that.

On the third evening he looked up from the bow. Across the waist of the ship, across the benches and the
crew between them, to where she stood at the mainmast.

He looked at her. Then at the sea. Then back at her.

She waited for his face to set and hold, the way every face set and held. It stayed loose. His eyes
went to her chains. To her hands. Back to her face. Off to the sea again. Back.

Every time, they came back. Loose.

Faces had left her before. The Captain's, at Chain Harbor and since, to her hands. They came back set.

A man alone in a ring nobody would cross. A man who looked at her, and looked away, and looked back.

Go and stand in it.

• • •

She walked across the deck to the bow.

The crew on the benches turned to watch her go, all of them, faces holding on her the whole length of
the deck. She passed the end of the benches. Passed the foremast. Came to the edge of the ring.

She stepped into it.

The weight came down on her at once. A load, flat and even. As if the air had grown a hand and set it on
her shoulders and pressed. Her knees took it. Her spine took it. The chains on her forearms hung heavier
and swung less. The plank under her boot gave a groan of its own.

She took another step. Each one the same weight. Her heart lifted a little with the work of it.

He watched her come. His eyes moved the whole way. Down to the chains. To her boots on the groaning
planks. Back to her face.

• • •

"You will want to step back," he said. Quiet. He stayed seated. "It is heavy, this close."

"Yes."

"Most people step back."

"I saw."

She did not sit. Sitting under a weight was the harness again. She stood an arm's length from him with
her bowl in her hand and ate on her feet, shifting her weight from one foot to the other, the load riding
down through her into the planks. The planks groaned under the two of them together, one long low note.

He looked at her for a while. She let him. She had nothing to measure it against.

"Does it hurt you?" he said.

"It's heavy."

"That is not an answer."

"It's all of it."

He almost smiled. He looked down at the bowl in his hands.

"At home they built the walls thicker for me," he said. "Then thicker again. Then there was no wall thick
enough."

She did not ask where home was.

Out on the benches the crew had gone back to their bowls. Her three sat at the end of the nearest bench,
outside the ring, where everyone sat. Hamund was watching. He caught her eye, and his face set and held
the way every face did, and she looked away from him first.

• • •

She ate at the bow after that.

Every evening. Inside the ring, on her feet, the weight on her shoulders through the whole meal, the
planks groaning their low note under two bowls. She explained it to no one. Nobody asked.

In the mornings she worked the pumps at his hours. She walked the waist deck with him when he walked it,
on the side nearest the crew. The men coiling line looked up as she passed, and their faces turned to her
and held, and the ring went by beside her unremarked.

On the fifth day a deckhand cut too close behind him with a cask on his shoulder, misjudged the weight,
and went to one knee in it, the cask rolling. He scrambled back out over the ring's edge on his hands and
knees and stood up outside it, swearing at Ren. Loud. The whole waist heard.

Swearing at a man for the weight he was born with.

She stepped out of the ring and stood in front of the deckhand, the chains hanging at her knees.

His face turned to her and held.

"Go round," she said.

He went round. He went wide.

She stepped back into the weight. Ren had not moved. He was looking at her.

"I did not ask you to do that," he said.

"No."

He waited. She gave him nothing more.

• • •

On the ninth day, word came forward from the stern. The Captain wanted the young man's measure. Feet.
Chest. The weight of him. There would be boots. A vest.

Ren listened to the bosun say it from the edge of the ring. Then he looked at her.

"A vest," he said. "To make me less."

"To let people near when you want them near."

He thought about that a long time, in the stillness that she was learning was the way he counted.

"How do you know what he builds?"

She held up one wrist. The chain ran from the cuff to the Fang at her knee. The links hung cool and
heavier than they should, inside the weight.

"He measured my heart," she said. "Then he built these to it."

He looked at the chain. Then at her face. Then away to the sea, and back.

That night, in her hammock, she listened to the ship. The groan in the forward timbers went on past
midnight, low and even. A weight the hull had learned to carry.

She fell asleep counting it.
