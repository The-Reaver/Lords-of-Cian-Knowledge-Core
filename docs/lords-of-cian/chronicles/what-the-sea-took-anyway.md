# What the Sea Took Anyway

*Locked canon, Batch 186, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-544`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle XIV. Long Mask era,
age ~160, the Golden Terror period. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material — the first
genuine failure of a rescue operation in this alias's run. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.
No new named characters.*

---

The slaving vessel's captain, cornered and certain of capture, made a calculation no prior slaver had
attempted: rather than surrender the holds, he scuttled the ship deliberately, flooding the lower
decks before the Scourge's boarding party could reach the captives chained there.

Not everyone was freed in time. Eleven captives drowned before the flooding could be stopped.

"You reached the ship faster than any prior boarding," the flagship's own quartermaster said
afterward, watching the Scourge review the final count in a silence heavier than any prior operation's
aftermath. "Nobody could have anticipated he'd scuttle his own ship rather than surrender it."

"I should have anticipated it anyway. I've spent decades assuming slavers value the cargo enough to
protect it even in defeat. Today one valued denying me the rescue more than he valued his own profit,
and eleven people paid for a miscalculation that was mine to have seen coming." The Scourge didn't
accept the deflection, sat with the loss fully rather than let the sixty-one successfully freed
overshadow the eleven who weren't. "I've built decades of this persona on the promise that reaching a
slaving ship means reaching everyone aboard it. Today that promise had a real, permanent exception,
and I intend to remember it rather than let the successful rescues since make it easier to forget."

"What changes, going forward?"

"Boarding speed, for one — faster approaches, less time for a captain to make a decision like today's.
I don't know that it will always be enough. Some outcomes, once a man decides drowning his own cargo
is worth denying me, may not be preventable no matter how fast I move." The Scourge closed the day's
account, eleven names recorded plainly beside the sixty-one freed. "I'd rather carry that honestly
than pretend today was simply another clean rescue. It wasn't. Eleven people are dead because I
didn't anticipate cruelty cruel enough to cost its own author everything rather than lose."

The loss was never publicly repeated as part of the Scourge's growing legend, kept instead in the
same private ledger Garren Hask had long maintained — a true account existing alongside, not replacing,
the reputation built on every rescue that had actually succeeded.
