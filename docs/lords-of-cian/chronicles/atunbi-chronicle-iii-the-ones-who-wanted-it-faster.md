# Atunbi Chronicle III: The Ones Who Wanted It Faster

*Locked canon, Batch 173, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-520`). Homage-era Los Angeles, Atunbi/Oluwole. Territory
Chronicle — Oluwole as protagonist, Kanja an unnamed background guest with no command, credit, or
resolution authorship. New standalone material. No new named characters.*

---

A younger cohort within Oluwole's own movement, impatient with years of patient, incremental
cultivation, pressed for a faster, more visible campaign — rapid expansion, aggressive public
demands, the kind of urgency that had never been the movement's own established method but appealed
powerfully to people who hadn't lived through the slow years that had built what little ground
already existed.

"You've spent a decade doing this one careful season at a time," one of the younger organizers said,
frustration plain. "People are still hungry now. They don't have another decade to wait on your
patience."

"I understand the urgency. I don't think I've ever once claimed patience was painless, only that it
was what the work actually required." Oluwole didn't dismiss the younger organizer's frustration,
sat with it instead. "I've watched fast pressure work exactly once, and it cost this garden a third of
its ground before anyone could stop it. I've watched patient cultivation win outright, completely,
against a threat that would have crushed anything built too quickly to hold. I'm not opposed to speed
because I distrust you. I'm cautious about it because I've paid the actual cost of moving faster than
the ground could bear."

"So nothing changes? We just keep waiting?"

"No. I think you're right that something has to change — just not the patience itself. The pace of
new plots, maybe. The number of people we bring in at once. Places where speed genuinely helps without
costing us the roots underneath it." Oluwole met the younger cohort's urgency without either
capitulating to it or dismissing it outright. "I'd rather find where your urgency and my patience
actually agree than force either one to simply win over the other. I don't think this movement
survives choosing only one of those approaches to the exclusion of the other."

They spent the following weeks working through exactly that boundary together, urgency and patience
each finding real, if imperfect, accommodation with the other — no clean resolution, but a genuine,
ongoing negotiation between two approaches the movement needed both of.

"I still think we're moving too slowly," the younger organizer admitted afterward, some of the earlier
frustration softened into something more collaborative. "I don't think I fully understood the cost of
moving too fast, before this. I do now."

An unnamed man who had listened throughout the negotiation without contributing left once the two
sides had found their first real accommodation.
