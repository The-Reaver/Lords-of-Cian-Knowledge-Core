# The Six Hours They Held Him

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle L, wave 17. Rebellion era, during the active Sewer War of Killane (MCD-234, age 20). Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The patrol didn't stop him for anything he'd done — they stopped him because his trade papers listed
a district that had reported three unaccounted brace-setters that week, and because a man his size,
alone, near a checkpoint, was worth six hours of a sergeant's caution even without cause.

Kanja let himself be taken without resistance, which was itself the hardest part of the six hours that
followed.

They held him in a converted storage room with a table, a lantern, and a junior officer whose job was
apparently to ask the same four questions in different orders until an answer changed. Kanja gave the
same answers every time, in the same plain, unhurried voice a real six-year journeyman would have used
on his hundredth interrogation of the war.

"Trade."

"Brace-setter. Timber and shoring, mostly. Some stonework when the timber's not available."

"District."

"Reassigned twice since the flood rerouted the southern passages. I can name every crew chief I've
worked under if that helps you cross-check faster than guessing does."

The officer tried three different angles across the six hours — a trick question about a brace
pattern that didn't exist, a claim that a named crew chief had already contradicted him, a long silent
stretch meant to make an innocent man nervous and a guilty one talk to fill it. Kanja answered the
false pattern by correctly identifying it as false and explaining why no working brace-setter would
ever use it; let the contradiction claim sit until the officer, checking his own notes, found there
was no contradiction at all; and sat through the silence the way six real years underground would have
taught a man to sit through worse.

"You're very calm for someone who's been here six hours," the officer said, somewhere past the fourth
hour, less an accusation than genuine curiosity.

"I've spent longer than six hours waiting on a joint to set before I could trust my own work on top
of it. This isn't the longest wait I've had this month." Kanja kept his hands loose on the table,
nothing in his posture worth reading as either fear or defiance. "I understand why you're holding me.
A district short three brace-setters is a district worth checking. I'd rather you check thoroughly
and let me go clean than let me go fast and still doubt me tomorrow."

It was the sergeant who finally cut it short, arriving with the actual district roster the junior
officer had been too proud to request four hours earlier — Kanja's name and trade both present,
correctly filed, boringly legitimate. No apology was offered, and none was expected.

"Six hours for nothing," the sergeant said, waving him toward the door.

"Not for nothing. You confirmed a real brace-setter, and I confirmed I can sit still for six hours
without giving you a reason to hold me longer." Kanja gathered his tools, unhurried even now. "Next
patrol that stops me, this goes faster. That's not nothing either."

He walked back to the work site as the sun was setting, six hours behind schedule and not a single
lie told the entire time — the cover holding not because he'd outwitted anyone, but because there had
never been anything false in the papers for six hours of pressure to find.
