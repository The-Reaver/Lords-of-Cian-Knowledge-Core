# The Ice That Doesn't Warn Like Water

*Locked canon, Batch 263, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1340`). Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle LXVII, wave 23, first entry in the wave. The doctrine meets a northern strait where pack ice forms on its own unpredictable timeline -- the sub-series' first entry where the student's trained method genuinely doesn't map onto the phenomenon in front of her.*

---

The models were built for water that moved before it turned dangerous -- pressure dropping, wind shifting, waves telling their own story hours ahead of the storm they belonged to. Ice didn't announce itself that way. It could form in a night, flat and silent, closing a channel that had been open water at dusk, and none of Sephtis's pressure-reading gave the student anything to work from.

She said so plainly, to a northern harbor pilot who'd asked for a forecast the way southern captains always did. "I don't know how to read this the way I read a storm. I don't think I can give you what you're asking for."

The pilot, an old woman who'd worked this strait longer than the student had been alive, didn't look surprised. "Nobody who learned weather in warm water can," she said. "It's not the same question. Storm's a matter of when the sky decides something. Ice is a matter of when the water gives up holding heat it never had much of. Different math. Different sky doesn't even come into it half the time."

It would have been easy, and honest, to say the doctrine simply didn't extend this far north -- to draw a line on the chart and call everything above it someone else's problem. The student didn't do that. She spent three weeks doing the thing the doctrine itself had always taught her to do when a reading failed: not guess harder, but go find out what she was actually looking at. She sat with the pilot through two false alarms and one real one, watching what the old woman watched -- not sky, mostly, but the specific color of water at dawn, the sound ice made settling overnight, a particular stillness in gulls that meant something colder than usual was coming even under clear skies.

"This isn't storm-timing," she finally told the pilot, less an admission of failure now than a real finding. "This is its own doctrine. I've been trying to force your ice into my sky, and it doesn't go."

"So don't force it," the pilot said. "Learn mine instead. I've got no one to leave it to when I'm done reading it myself."

Kanja, who had come north on other business and stayed to watch the student work, understood what the offer actually was before either woman said it aloud. It wasn't a lesson. It was a succession, offered sideways, to someone who hadn't been looking for one.

The student didn't answer right away. She looked at the ice a long time first -- honestly, the way she'd been taught to look at anything she didn't yet understand.
