# The Mural on the Sea Wall

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle XXIV, closing wave 7.
Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The settlement's children had started it without asking anyone's permission — a scavenged-pigment
sketch on the rebuilt sea wall's inner face, a barge with a forge-glow spilling off its deck, three
crude figures beside it that even a stranger could have guessed at. By the time The Anvil was ready to
move on to its next stop, half the wall's inner face had filled in, added to by hands of every age,
none of it commissioned, none of it corrected by anyone who thought it needed correcting.

"You're letting them paint the ship wrong," one of the settlement's elders said, half amused, pointing
out a rendering of Obsidian Malice three times its actual size. "That thing's going to outlast every
one of us standing here, and it's not even accurate."

"It doesn't need to be accurate." Kanja studied the mural the way he studied a finished joint, looking
for what actually held it together rather than what was technically correct about it. "It needs to be
theirs. I didn't come here to leave behind an accurate record. Plenty of people already keeping those.
I came to leave behind something that made people want to build things themselves after I was gone.
If that's what a three-times-too-large war club painted by an eight-year-old does, I'm not going to be
the one who tells her she got it wrong."

A girl, watching from the base of the wall, worked in silence on a smaller figure of her own — not the
crew, not the ship, just a single small hand holding a single small hammer, unremarkable next to the
larger scenes around it.

"What's that one?" Kanja asked her, crouching to her level.

"That's the part I'm going to remember. Not the ship. Not the fighting." She didn't look up from her
work. "Just that we're the ones who get to hold the hammer now."

He didn't correct her either, and left the wall standing exactly as it was — half-finished, inaccurate
in every measurable dimension, and, by every account that mattered to the people who'd painted it,
entirely true.
