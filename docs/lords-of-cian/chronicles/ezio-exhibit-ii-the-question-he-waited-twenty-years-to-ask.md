# Ezio Exhibit II: The Question He Waited Twenty Years to Ask

*Locked canon, Batch 380, 2026-10-10 (`MCD-1908`). Clean on independent review. Abad's approval: "lock it". Teller and rulings: approval-list items 4, 6 and 7, "items 4, 6 and 7 "Yes" to all three". The Series: Ezio's Exhibits. Teller: Fermand
Aurelias, per `CC-034` and `VB-024`. The second Exhibit. Dramatizes the recruitment of Lauris
Letitia from Ezio's side (`MCD-194`, `MCD-195`, `MCD-1661`): the roughly two decades of observation before the
meeting, Aerelin's mediation, the private coastal residence, Fermand withdrawing to a side room, the
agreement to say nothing further on one subject, and the Attia role. Fermand knows Ezio keeps a cover and does not know what it hides (`CC-027` as amended Batch 380; `VB-067`). Strictly pre-Book-1. No new named characters.*

---

I did not know, when I began transcribing his correspondence, that Ezio Valcari kept a second set of files. I learned of them only when he asked me to accompany him to a coastal house, and by then one of them had been open for the better part of two decades.

It concerned a Sealbound Directorate external contractor. A woman named Lauris Letitia. Her operational record was, on
its surface, unremarkable among such records -- efficient, well-reviewed by her own handlers, the
kind of file that closes a case and opens no questions. Ezio had read it once, early, by his own
later account, and had found in it a detail no one else appeared to have noticed: her reports read the Trust's containment architecture the way the Captain reads a building, and the Directorate had filed them without comment.

He did not act on the observation. He filed it, and he began, as he told me afterward, to read every subsequent report that crossed a desk anywhere near her
name.

*

I want to be precise about what I mean by "began to read," because I think the ordinary word
undersells what I eventually came to understand Ezio actually does with a person he has decided is
worth this particular kind of attention. He observes a subject the way a physician observes a
slow-healing wound -- at intervals, without urgency, cross-referencing each new report against the
last, looking for the shape that becomes visible once enough data points have accumulated to draw
it.

Over the better part of twenty years, by his own later accounting, he watched that shape become
visible three separate times, and set it aside three separate times, because each time he judged
that acting would cost her more than waiting would.

The first time, she had just been reassigned to a facility he judged too heavily watched to approach
without drawing attention neither of them could afford. The second time, her handlers had begun treating her with an escalating caution, and Ezio read in it an institution that had started, quietly, to measure her. He judged that any outside contact at that
moment would confirm precisely the suspicion he most wanted her handlers not to have. The third time,
I believe, he simply was not yet certain enough, and told me once, with the particular honesty he
reserves for admissions that cost him something, that he had let fear of being wrong about a person
masquerade as prudence for longer than the prudence alone could account for.

"You are not a man who is often wrong about a person," I said.

"I have built my entire life on not being wrong about people," he said. "Which is precisely why,
when the stakes were finally high enough, I could not be certain whether I was reading her record
correctly or simply wanted to."

*

The fourth time the shape came clear, it did not fade when he set it aside. It was, he told me, a
single sentence buried in a routine after-action report -- an unremarkable notation, filed by a
superior who plainly did not understand what he was recording, describing the contractor's
"continued operational reliability despite repeated exposure to legacy-system anomalies outside
standard briefing parameters." A phrase built to close a file. Ezio read it four
times before he understood what it actually meant: that she had been finding things the Directorate
itself did not know were there to find, and setting them down in reports, for an institution that did
not have the structural humility to recognize what it was being handed.

I have come to believe this is the sentence that actually decided him. He had known for years that
she was capable. The sentence told him she read the institution's architecture more clearly than the institution did, and that, by his own private accounting, it would destroy her for that eventually.

He did not approach her directly, even then. She walked away from the Directorate on her own, and some two months later, while she moved between safe-houses Aerelin had prepared, he arranged, through Kareth-Vassen Aerelin's own quiet mediation, a meeting at a private coastal residence -- a location chosen, he told me
afterward, specifically because it offered her three separate ways to leave before he ever said a
word, none of which required explaining herself to him.

*

I accompanied him to the house, and I was not present for the meeting itself. Ezio asked me to
withdraw to a side room. I have since understood the request as a final act of the same patience
that had governed the preceding twenty years: he did not want even a trusted witness present for the
one conversation in which he would, for the first time in two decades of careful observation, be
wrong or right in the open, with no further file to consult.

He told me afterward only what he judged necessary for the record. He had briefed her, from
Sephtis's archives, on Anu Un Ra's broader operational history, which gave a context to the
discovery she had made on her own. He had offered her the Attia role.
She had accepted on three conditions, which she named without hesitation, as though she had spent
time of her own imagining what she would ask for if the offer ever came: that her commitments
concerning the engineered Karesian subjects outranked the crew's requirements wherever the two
conflicted; that her dealings with Aerelin and the Kareth network remained on terms she controlled;
and that every decision about where and how she fought was hers alone. He agreed to each without
amendment.

He mentioned one matter more, and only that it existed. There was a single subject on which the two
of them had agreed to say nothing further, to each other. He did not tell me what it
was, and I did not ask. I wrote in my own record that it was one more thing I did not know, and I
have understood since that a man who keeps a cover keeps it from his friends.

He did not tell me what he had said to her before she spoke, and I have never asked him to
reconstruct it for me. Some silences in a man's account are the last private thing he keeps.

*

I asked him, some years later, whether twenty years had felt like patience or like fear.

"Both," he said. "I have told myself, since, that I was waiting for certainty. I do not think that
is entirely true. I think I was also waiting to be brave enough to be wrong about someone I had
already decided mattered too much to approach carelessly."

I wrote that down exactly as he said it, because I believe it is the truest thing he has ever told
me about the shape of his own patience. At the center of the method is a man who has built an entire professional life on evidence choosing, once, to act on something he
could not fully document, and finding that he was right.
