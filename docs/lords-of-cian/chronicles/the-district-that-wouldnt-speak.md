# The District That Wouldn't Speak

The tanning district had been promised things before, by enough people over enough generations, that
nobody came to the yard when Kanja's crew arrived. Not out of fear — Ezio checked twice, asked
carefully, found no threat hanging over anyone's silence. Just a settled, generational certainty that
nothing written down had ever once changed anything, and that showing up to say otherwise would only
be one more thing to be disappointed by.

He tried the worst-off-first approach that had opened every other district cleanly, going first to
the curing pits where the work was hardest and the pay thinnest. The pit workers looked at him the
same way they'd have looked at weather — present, unremarkable, certain to pass — and answered his
question with silence or with shrugs that weren't refusals so much as a total absence of expectation
that an answer would matter.

For the first time since the campaign had started, the method simply didn't work. Not defeated by a
hostile trap, not undone by a fabricated counter-account — just met with nothing at all to push
against.

Kanja didn't force it. He set the ledger open on an overturned barrel in the district's square, left
it there with the pen beside it, unguarded, and told the handful of people still watching that he
wasn't going to ask anyone anything else. The book would be there for three days. Anyone who wanted
to write in it could. Anyone who didn't, wouldn't be asked twice.

Nothing was written the first day. Nothing the second. On the third night, an old tanner who had said
nothing in three days of watching came alone in the dark, when he thought no one would see him do it,
and wrote a single line in his own hand — not even his full grievance, just a number and a name.

By morning, four more had followed him, then a dozen, then most of the district, each one having
watched the page fill a little further before deciding, on their own, that it might hold weight after
all. Kanja never asked a single question the whole three days. The district wrote its own account into
being, at its own pace, once it stopped being something demanded of them and became something
offered.
