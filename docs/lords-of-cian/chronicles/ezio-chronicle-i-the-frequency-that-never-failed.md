# Ezio Chronicle I: The Frequency That Never Failed

---

I have kept the books on a great many frightened men, and I have come, across the years, to
recognize a particular species of fear that announces itself before any other evidence does: the
fear of a man who has built something small enough to survive on, and who has just understood, in
the space of a single sentence, that smallness was never going to save him.

Callas Modrin had that fear on him before Ezio said a word.

It began, as most of Ezio's work does, with nothing that looked like a beginning. A settlement six
days west of the Korren depots had paid, over eleven years, four separate emergency levies for
suppression-field redeployment against "escalating contamination" its own Blight Frequency
Projector had supposedly detected. A second settlement, eighty miles distant and known to the first
only by trade caravan, had paid three. A third had paid two, and had very nearly stopped paying
after the second, until the inspector returned with a reading so precisely calibrated to terrify a
grain-poor community into compliance that they found the coin rather than risk finding out whether
he was bluffing.

None of these settlements had ever compared notes. None of them had any reason to. The amounts were
modest, by Trust standards -- small enough that no single complaint would ever rise past a district
clerk's own threshold of interest, and I want to be precise here, because I believe this is the
whole of Callas Modrin's actual genius, such as it was: he had understood, better than most men in
his position ever bother to, that the Directorate's attention is a finite resource, and that the
surest way to spend a decade stealing from frightened people is to never once steal enough from any
single one of them to be worth the paperwork.

Ezio found the pattern the way he finds most patterns -- not by suspecting Modrin, but by noticing
that three unrelated settlements, describing three unrelated inspectors, described identical
language. "Escalating contamination requiring immediate suppression redeployment" is not a phrase a
frightened grain-weigher invents on her own. It is a phrase a man says the same way every time,
because he has found that it works.

*

We rode to the second settlement first, the one that had paid three times, because its most recent
levy was also its most recent Blight Frequency Projector recalibration, and recalibration logs are
filed with the Directorate proper rather than left in a local inspector's own discretion. Ezio did
not introduce himself to the settlement's reeve as an investigator. He introduced himself as a
Trust auditor confirming that the district's suppression equipment was fit for continued service --
true in every particular, and calculated, I think, to let the reeve volunteer everything out of
relief rather than fear.

She volunteered everything. The date. The reading. The sum. The inspector's name, given without
prompting, the way a debt is given when someone has finally asked the right question of a person
who has been waiting years for anyone to ask it at all.

That evening, in a rented room with the shutters latched, Ezio took the cane he carries everywhere
and is, I suspect, the least remarked-upon object in the whole of Kanja's crew -- a plain length of
turned wood that reads, to anyone who has ever looked at it, as the affectation of a theorist who
has read too many books and walked too few miles. He pressed it, hollow end first, against the
sealed Directorate data-plate the reeve's own district office kept on file, and held it there
without moving for the better part of a minute, his eyes closed, his fingers resting along its
length the way a physician's rest along a wrist.

"The plate says the Projector's last true reading, the one the machine itself actually recorded,
was clean," he said. "No contamination. No escalation. The number Modrin gave her was invented
after the fact and backdated into a report that does not match what the equipment itself kept."

I asked him how he could be certain the filaments had not simply misread an old plate.

"Because the plate agrees with itself," he said. "A lie has to be built. A true record only has to
sit there being true. I have spent enough years telling the difference between the two that I no
longer need to explain how I know. I only need to show the work."

I wrote that down. I have found, over the years, that Ezio's sentences about his own method are the
ones I am most careful to record exactly, because he offers so few of them, and because each one
tends to double as a sentence about something larger than the method itself.

*

The confrontation, when it came, was almost disappointingly quiet, which is to say it was exactly
the kind of confrontation Ezio prefers.

Modrin received him in a district requisitions office three settlements further on, a small man
behind a smaller desk, precisely as unremarkable as every report had promised -- the sort of face
that asks to be trusted by virtue of asking nothing else of a room at all. He did not recognize
Ezio as a threat. I do not believe he recognized Ezio as anything at all, which I have come to
understand is very close to the entire secret of how Ezio Valcari has survived seventy-five years of
work that would have gotten a more memorable man killed a dozen times over.

Ezio laid eleven years of paper on the desk between them, in order, and asked a single question:
whether Inspector Modrin could explain why the equipment's own sealed maintenance record showed a
functioning suppression system on every date his own filed reports described an escalating
contamination event.

Modrin's first answer was that equipment fails silently sometimes, and a silent failure would not
appear on a maintenance log built to record the equipment's own last known state.

"It would," Ezio said. "That is precisely what a maintenance log is for."

His second answer was that perhaps the readings had been recorded by a junior technician, in error,
and the error was not his to account for.

"There is no junior technician's name anywhere on any of these eleven files," Ezio said. "There is
only yours."

His third answer, and I do not think either of us expected it, was silence -- the particular
silence of a man who has run out of stories and has just understood, watching Ezio wait for him to
fill it, that the waiting itself is the last part of the method, and that he has already lost.

Ezio did not press him for a confession. He has told me, more than once, that a confession offered
to relieve a silence is worth less than the paper it is never written on, and that the documents
were already sufficient, and that Modrin's own superiors would draw their own conclusions from them
regardless of what the man himself chose to say in a rented requisitions office with no witness but
a scribe.

*

They did draw their own conclusions, though not, I confess, the ones either of us expected.

The Sovereign Trust has no particular institutional appetite for prosecuting an inspector for
frightening grain-poor settlements out of small sums of coin over the better part of a decade. Such
matters are, in the Trust's own quiet arithmetic, beneath the cost of pursuing. But every one of
Modrin's false contamination reports had required him to log an emergency suppression-field
redeployment that never occurred, and every logged redeployment had drawn, automatically and
without further review, an equipment-replacement allotment from the district's own maintenance
budget -- an allotment Modrin had been quietly pocketing the difference of for eleven years, coin
the extortion victims never saw a scrip of.

He was arrested within the month, not for what he had done to eleven settlements, but for what he
had done to a budget line the Trust actually cared about.

I asked Ezio whether that troubled him -- that the only justice available to a poor woman cheated
for eleven years was the accident of a bureaucracy discovering it had also, incidentally, been
cheated.

He considered the question longer than he considers most.

"It should trouble me," he said. "I am not certain it does, and I am not certain that not being
troubled by it is a thing I should be proud of. The settlements are not made whole. They never will
be. But the frequency Modrin claimed had been failing them for eleven years never failed once. It
recorded the truth every single time, faithfully, and simply waited for someone to be willing to
read it. I find I would rather live in a world where that is sometimes enough than spend my life
certain it never is."

I wrote that down exactly as he said it, and I have carried it with me since, because I believe it
is the truest thing I have yet recorded about the man: that he has built an entire life's practice
on the conviction that a properly kept record eventually tells on everyone who lies in front of it
-- even when the world that finally listens does so for reasons that have nothing to do with the
people the lie actually harmed.
