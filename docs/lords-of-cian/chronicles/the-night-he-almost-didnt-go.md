# The Night He Almost Didn't Go

The report came in the way every report came in by then — a transport running captives through a
familiar stretch of water, a window of maybe six hours before it cleared into open sea and out of
reach — and for the first time in longer than he could easily place, he sat with the report a long
while before giving the order to move.

It wasn't fear. He turned that possibility over honestly and set it aside; two hundred and twenty-
eight years of engagements had worn the fear down to something closer to habit than dread. It was
something else, harder to name, a bone-deep tiredness that had been accumulating in increments too
small to notice on any single night and had, somewhere in the last decade, become large enough to
notice all at once.

"You're not moving," Efa Gol's successor said, finding him still seated with the report an hour after
he should have already given the launch order.

"I'm aware."

"Six hours. The window's real."

"I'm aware of that too." He didn't offer an excuse, because he didn't have one that satisfied even
himself. "I've done this several thousand times. I know exactly what the next six hours look like
before I've lived them. Every part of me that used to move on instinct is asking, tonight, why it has
to be tonight, why it has to be me, why two hundred and twenty-eight years hasn't yet earned a night
where someone else's hands do this instead."

The successor didn't argue the feeling, which he seemed to appreciate more than an argument would
have. "Nobody else's hands can do this one. You know that."

"I know that." He stood, finally, the report still in his grip, the tiredness not lifted so much as
simply set aside for the hours it would take to matter less. "Knowing it and wanting it to be someone
else's turn aren't the same thing. I just needed a minute to be honestly tired before I stopped being
allowed to be, for tonight."

The transport was cleared within the window, cleanly, two hundred and nine captives freed by hands
that moved with no visible trace of the hour he'd spent sitting still beforehand — and only Efa Gol's
successor, watching him afterward, understood that the night's real difficulty had been fought and won
before the coat was ever put on.
