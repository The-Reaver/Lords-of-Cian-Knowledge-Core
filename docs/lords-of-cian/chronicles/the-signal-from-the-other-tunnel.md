# The Signal From the Other Tunnel

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LIV, wave 18, closing the wave. Rebellion era, during the active Sewer War of Killane (MCD-234, age 20). Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The knock came from the wrong direction — three taps and a pause, against a wall Kanja's crew hadn't
cut through and didn't control, in a section of the network that belonged, as far as anyone had
mapped it, to no one at all.

He answered it the same way, three taps and a pause, and waited to find out which side of the war had
found the other from the far side of solid stone.

It turned out to be neither. A second resistance cell, operating three districts over and entirely
independent of Kanja's own network, had been tunneling toward the same strategic junction from the
opposite direction for reasons of its own, and had broken through into a wall neither crew had known
the other was working.

"We've been cutting toward this junction for six weeks," their own engineer said, once the wariness
on both sides had settled enough for actual conversation, studying Kanja's brace work with open,
professional interest. "Didn't know anyone else had the same idea from this side."

"Neither did I, until your wall started answering mine." Kanja studied the other engineer's cuts in
turn — different technique, same underlying competence, a crew that had clearly learned this trade the
same honest way his own had. "Two independent tunnels converging on one junction is either the best
luck this siege has had or the worst coordination two allied cells have ever managed."

"Could be both." The other engineer didn't seem especially troubled by it. "Question's whether we
finish this as two separate operations bumping into each other in the dark, or one operation that
actually talks to itself."

They spent the better part of a night comparing maps neither cell had any obligation to share, finding
that the two approaches — Kanja's from the flooded gallery side, the other cell's from an older,
drier access route — solved different halves of the same structural problem the junction posed. Where
his own crew had struggled to shore the wet approach without flooding the whole gallery, the other
engineer's drier route offered a stable staging point; where the other cell had no answer for the
junction's actual load-bearing weakness, Kanja's six weeks of local reading did.

"Your access route buys us a staging point we didn't have. My load analysis buys you a demolition plan
that won't bring the ceiling down on your own people," Kanja said, laying both maps side by side.
"Neither of us solves this cleanly alone. Together, this takes half the time either operation was
budgeting for it separately."

The combined operation took the junction four days later, coordinated through the same breached wall
that had first announced the accident of their meeting — two independent cells, two different
engineering traditions, one plan built from both without either side subordinating its own crew to the
other's command.

"Command's going to want to know how two separate operations ended up finishing each other's work,"
the other engineer said afterward, already sketching the shared map for their own chain to file.

"Tell them the truth. Two crews that had never coordinated before found out they didn't need to be
told to, once the wall between them turned out to be thinner than either side thought." Kanja shook
the man's hand, professional respect earned in a single night rather than assumed from allegiance
alone. "That's not luck holding up a siege. That's just what happens when good work finds other good
work, even from the wrong direction."
