# Ijoko Chronicle I: What Laughter Couldn't Move

*Locked canon, Batch 86, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-349`). First entry in Ijoko's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Adwoa (`PH2-027`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`) through Atunbi Chronicle I (`MCD-348`). Narration is
close-third on Adwoa throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Continues Los Angeles's own run of territory
Chronicles. Slots into no existing mainline battle — original homage-era material set in Ijoko
itself.*

---

The council chamber had a particular kind of laughter it saved for Adwoa alone — not loud, never
loud, the practiced, deniable kind that men who'd rather not be called what they actually were used
instead of saying it outright. She'd learned to hear every register of it in her first month in the
chair. She had never once, in three years since, given it what it was fishing for.

"The clerk misplaced the budget again," one of them said, this time, to the room rather than to her
directly, the way the joke always arrived — sideways, deniable, aimed close enough to land without
ever quite landing on the record. "Maybe if certain people spent less time smiling and more time
counting."

The room did the thing it always did — half a laugh, half a glance at Adwoa to see how she'd take
it, the same test run a dozen different ways every session since she'd taken the chair. She smiled,
the same warm, unhurried smile she always wore, and did not so much as pause the gavel's rhythm.

"The budget's exactly where I left it, which is more than I can say for some of the arguments in
this room tonight." She said it lightly, the way she said everything, and moved the agenda forward
before the laughter had fully finished deciding what to do with itself. Nothing in her hands shook.
Nothing in her voice climbed. If anything, the room noticed — the stranger watching from the back
row noticed it plainest of all — that her grip on the gavel looked, if it changed at all, steadier
than it had been thirty seconds before.

He'd been sitting in on council sessions for a week by then, unremarkable in a back row that saw a
dozen unremarkable faces most nights, present without anyone quite marking when he'd started
attending.

"They do that every session," he said afterward, once the chamber had emptied and it was just the
two of them and the janitor's cart in the hallway.

"Every session. Sometimes twice." Adwoa gathered her papers without any particular hurry. "It's the
same move it's always been, dressed up in whatever that week's excuse happens to be. They're testing
whether a joke can do what an argument can't — get under something, find a crack, watch it show.
There isn't a crack. There's never been a crack. I decided that the first week and I haven't had a
reason to revisit it since."

"Doesn't it wear on you?"

"It would, if I let it be about me. It stopped being about me the day I understood it was never
actually aimed at whether I could take a joke. It's aimed at whether this city can take a woman
holding this chair without falling apart. Every time I don't flinch, that question gets answered
again, a little more permanently than the time before." She looked back at the empty chamber, dark
now except for the exit lights. "That part I can hold all night, every night, for as long as they
want to keep asking."

The part she didn't say out loud that night, though the stranger would come to understand it in the
weeks that followed simply by watching the ledgers cross her desk: the composure that turned every
jab back on the men throwing it did nothing at all against the slower thing eating the city from
underneath the council chamber floor — the tax base thinning year over year as families and
businesses both packed up for somewhere the numbers worked better, no insult in any of it, no crack
to hold steady against, just capital doing what capital does when a place stops being where the
money wants to stay. She could out-will every man in that room until the day she left the chair. She
could not out-will a spreadsheet.

"You don't smile at that one," the stranger said, once, watching her study a revenue report long
after the chamber had cleared.

"No." She didn't look up from the numbers. "This one isn't trying to get under my skin. It doesn't
care whether I flinch. It's just leaving, the way water leaves a cracked cup, whether I hold the cup
steady or not." She set the report down, finally, and allowed herself, for just that moment, an
expression the council chamber never once got to see. "I know exactly which fight I'm built to win.
I'd rather know that clearly than spend my whole term pretending I'm built to win both."

The stranger was gone before the next session convened, the way he always seemed to be. He left
nothing carved, no phrase repeated hand to hand — only, the following week, one more late night in
the back row while Adwoa worked through another report that no amount of steadiness in the chamber
above could do a single thing to fix.
