# The Method the Trust Wanted to Own

*Locked canon, Batch 242, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1064`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LXIII, wave
21, closing the wave. Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose.*

---

The man who came to Warehouse Twelve wore a Sovereign Trust registrar's collar, traveled with no
guard at all, and asked for an appointment through the ordinary grievance line like anyone else —
which was, Kanja understood within a minute of sitting down with him, exactly the point he'd come to
make.

"I'm not here about a dispute," the registrar said. "I'm here about your method."

He laid it out plainly enough that Kanja almost respected the honesty of it. Cross-witnessed tally
verification, independently checked figures, public correction printed alongside any claim — the
Trust's own inquest at Canal House had quietly concluded, months back, that the method worked better
than anything the Compliance apparatus had ever run for settling wage disputes without them
escalating into exactly the kind of unrest that kept costing the Trust soldiers it couldn't spare.
He wanted to formalize it. Standardize it. Put it under a Trust charter, train Trust auditors in it,
run it in every district from here to the Shattered Kingdoms border.

Garren Hask, three tables over, had gone very still in the particular way he went still when a
number didn't add up yet and he hadn't found where.

"You want to take the thing we built to catch the Trust stealing," Kanja said, "and have the Trust
run it."

"I want to take the thing that's already working and stop it from only working where you personally
happen to be standing." The registrar didn't flinch from the framing. "You've trained sixteen people
at a time. There are four hundred districts. You are not going to reach them all, and every district
you don't reach is still bleeding wages into the same theft this method was built to stop."

It was true, and Kanja sat with it being true for longer than he sat with most things the Trust told
him. Hask had already started working the arithmetic without being asked — sixteen students, four
hundred districts, the honest math of how far one crew's hands could actually stretch against a
continent's worth of owners.

"Here's what it costs you," Kanja said finally. "The verification stays in the workers' hands, not
the owners' and not the Trust's. You can fund it, you can charter it, you can put a Trust seal on
the paperwork if that's what it takes to make owners take it seriously. But the people doing the
counting answer to the districts they're counting for, not to you, and the figures stay open to any
worker who wants to check them — not filed somewhere a Trust office can quietly lose the
inconvenient ones."

"That's a harder charter to write than the one I came with."

"Then write the harder one."

The registrar didn't promise it would survive the offices above him, and Kanja didn't ask him to
pretend otherwise. What he took instead, before the man left, was a copy of Hask's own arithmetic —
the honest count of everywhere the method wasn't yet reaching — and a plain agreement that whatever
came of the charter, that count was the thing that would judge whether it had been worth doing.

Hask watched him go. "That could be the best thing that ever happens to this method," he said. "Or
it could be the thing that finally takes it away from us wearing our own name."

"I know," Kanja said. "We won't know which for a while yet."
