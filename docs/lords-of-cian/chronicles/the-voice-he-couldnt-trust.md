# The Voice He Couldn't Trust

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Crow King Alias Chronicle XLVI, wave 16. Rebellion
era, years after the Night of the Crow King. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters beyond the already-locked apprentice, unnamed matching
established pattern.*

---

The Directorate patrol's opening volley caught Kanja across the throat at a range close enough that
armor alone didn't fully turn it — a glancing blow, not a killing one, but enough that when he tried
to call the first line of the evacuation's covering pattern, what came out was a torn, breathless
scrape that carried perhaps ten feet before it died.

He tried again. The second attempt was worse.

The apprentice reached him within seconds, already reading the problem in the shape of his mouth
before he'd finished failing to make the sound work. Forty people were still inside the compound
behind them, and the pattern that was supposed to draw the patrol's attention toward the empty grain
stores depended on a voice carrying across open ground that neither of them had time to close.

"Don't try to force it," she said, low. "You'll tear it worse and buy us nothing."

"Then it doesn't run." He mouthed more than said it, hoarse and clipped.

"It runs. It just doesn't run through you." She was already moving, already dropping into the opening
bars herself — a register she'd built years ago, distinct from his own, that had never before needed
to carry the operation's entire weight alone. "You taught me the pattern. You didn't teach me that I
needed you standing next to me to run it."

He fell back to signal instead — hand patterns, the old percussive taps against his own armor plate
that the craft had carried as a secondary channel since long before this compound, since a wind shift
years back had first forced real-time improvisation out of necessity rather than design. It wasn't
enough on its own to fool anyone. It was enough to tell her, in real time, when the patrol's attention
had genuinely turned and when it hadn't, freeing her to adjust the pattern's rhythm without breaking
her own voice to check.

The patrol turned toward the grain stores at the eleventh minute, four minutes later than either of
them would have liked, close enough that the last of the forty cleared the tree line with the sound
of boots still audible behind them.

Afterward, throat wrapped, voice still not fully returned, Kanja watched her go over the pattern's
timing with the same care she'd have given a stranger's mistake rather than her own success.

"It should have failed," she said. "You've always been the one who could adjust the pattern
mid-performance if something went wrong. I've never had to hold the whole thing alone before, not
really — you were always somewhere nearby, even when I was the one singing."

"It didn't fail." He managed the words carefully, saving what voice he had. "That's the actual
answer to a question I don't think either of us had tested yet. Whether the thing I built only works
because I'm the one who built it, or whether it actually works because it's sound enough to hold up
without me in the room at all."

"Which answer did we just get?"

"The second one. I'd rather have found out this way than the other way."

The crew, once safely clear, instituted a standing rule within the week: no operation would run again
without a second full voice rehearsed and ready to carry the pattern's entirety alone, not merely its
pieces — a redundancy the craft had never needed to formalize until the night its own founder
couldn't speak.
