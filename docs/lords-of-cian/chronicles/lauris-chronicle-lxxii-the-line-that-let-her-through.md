# Lauris Chronicle LXXII: The Line That Let Her Through

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1689`). Seventy-second entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, a Strand D (Sealbound Directorate years) entry giving full-scene treatment to
Operation 1, her very first Directorate contract (previously only summarized in passing at
`MCD-1542`). Set within the Apprentice Contracts (`MCD-178`), with her assigned mentor Vael
Korr-Drennen (`MCD-177`) present. Dramatizes the exact moment his later mentorship-report line --
"she did not need me from the second day" -- describes, placing it explicitly on this contract's
second day rather than leaving it an unlocated generality. No new named characters. No
contradictions with any already-locked rule.*

-----

*Archive fragment, Apprentice Contracts, Operation 1: "I counted the line at fifty-eight. I was
told to wait for the column. The column was four hours out. Fifty-eight people do not stay
frightened and still for four hours."*

I did not witness Lauris Letitia's first Directorate contract. No one who could write of it
properly did; Vael Korr-Drennen's own field notes are the only surviving account from a hand present
that day, and I have relied on them, cross-referenced against her own archive entry, in setting
this down as faithfully as a man born considerably later than the events he describes is able to
manage.

The contract was, by the standard of what her career would become, unremarkable. A tithe post on
the Tallow Road, an hour's ride from Kesmara's Eastern District, had been surrounded since dawn by
some fifty-eight laborers and dockhands of the surrounding settlements, indebted past the season's
harvest and unwilling, this year, to watch a third consecutive collection leave their granaries
bare. They held no weapons beyond field tools. They held, however, two Directorate tithe-clerks
inside the post itself, taken as the only leverage available to people with none, and they had made
clear, through a spokesman shouting from the picket line, that the clerks would not be released
until someone with the authority to renegotiate the collection arrived to hear them.

Vael's standard response to a hostage post was an enforcement column -- fifteen armed contractors,
overwhelming and visible, sufficient to end any standoff by the simple fact of its presence. He had
requested one. It was, by the Directorate's own logistics, four hours out.

"I am closer than the column," Lauris said. This was, per Vael's own note, the entirety of her
proposal. She did not ask permission so much as inform him of a fact and wait to see whether he
would contradict it.

*

I want to record plainly what happened next, because I believe the plainness is the point. She
walked to the picket line at an ordinary pace, from an ordinary direction, wearing the same
unremarkable traveling clothes she had worn since her onboarding three months prior. She did not
approach as a Directorate asset approaches a hostile position. She approached as a young woman of
unremarkable size walks toward a crowd she intends to pass through -- and the line, watching for a
column of enforcers on the Tallow Road and finding instead a single girl who might have been anyone's
daughter come to collect water, let her through without a hand raised.

She reached the post's threshold, found the two clerks bound but unharmed in the store's back room,
and had freed both before the spokesman who had let her pass thought to wonder why a stranger walking
alone through a hostile picket had not once asked leave to enter the building he was meant to be
guarding. He turned, I am told, at the sound of the second clerk's binding coming loose rather than
at any sound Lauris herself made, and by the time he understood what he was looking at, both clerks
were already standing free behind her and the moment in which taking her as a third hostage might
have been possible had already, quietly, closed.

She did not fight him. She did not need to. She told him, in the same flat register Vael's notes
record her using for every report she would ever file, that the collection would be reviewed rather
than enforced that season, that she had no authority to promise more than the review, and that the
clerks would walk out with her now, unharmed, because harming them had never bought the line anything
it could keep. He let them pass. I do not know, from anything preserved, whether he believed her
authority was real or simply concluded that arguing with the person who had already undone the
morning's only leverage was a poor use of what remained of it.

*

Vael arrived with the column some three hours later to find the post already quiet, the clerks
already debriefed and resting, and Lauris already filing her report -- four lines, he noted, where
his own after-action summaries for a comparable contract ran to two pages. His field journal for
that evening records a single observation, which I reproduce here because I believe it is the true
seed of the mentorship report he would not formally write for another nine contracts: *She did not
wait for the column because she had already done the arithmetic the column existed to avoid. I am
told I am to teach her the trade. I am beginning to suspect the trade will finish teaching itself.*

I have asked Lauris, across the considerable span of years I have known her, what she remembers of
her first contract. She remembers the count -- fifty-eight, precise, unhesitating even now. She does
not remember being frightened of the line, and when I asked whether she understood, at the time, that
walking through fifty-eight indebted and desperate people alone was a genuine risk, she considered
the question longer than I expected before answering.

"I understood the shape of the risk," she said. "I did not understand yet that most people who take
that shape of risk do not survive asking the question the way I was about to ask it. I have understood
that better every year since."

It is, I think, as close as she has ever come to telling me she was young once.
