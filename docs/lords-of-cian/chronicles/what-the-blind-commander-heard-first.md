# What the Blind Commander Heard First

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Crow King Alias Chronicle L, wave 17. Rebellion
era. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The garrison commander had lost his sight to fever a decade before the Rebellion began and had built
his entire command doctrine around what remained: a trained, deliberate attention to sound that no
sighted officer in the Directorate had ever needed to develop, because no sighted officer had ever
had to. His staff read him maps by touch and description. He read the ground itself by ear, and in
four years no one had managed to move past his perimeter undetected.

The false signal was, by every standard the crew had refined across dozens of operations, close to
flawless — the same measured breath control, the same practiced evenness that had fooled sensor
grids, sighted patrols, even a cryptographer who'd studied the pattern's own structure. It fooled
every one of the commander's sighted officers standing beside him.

It did not fool him.

"Too even," he said, before any of them had reacted at all. "Real distress doesn't hold a rhythm
that clean. A man calling for help in the dark speeds up when he's frightened and slows when he's
tired, and he does both within the same breath. That call did neither. Whoever's out there is
performing fear, not feeling it."

His second, unconvinced, pointed out that nothing on the perimeter had actually moved. The commander
didn't answer that so much as wait, and within the hour a runner confirmed a supply cache two miles
distant had been emptied in the exact window his officers had spent chasing the sound.

When word of it reached the apprentice, she didn't hear it as a defeat so much as a correction long
overdue. "Every countermeasure we've beaten came from someone looking for a pattern, a structure,
something a sensor could measure and flag. He's not measuring anything. He's just genuinely listening,
the way the craft was supposed to be listened to in the first place, and it turns out our own
performance is too clean to survive that kind of attention."

"So we get worse at it," Kanja said, working through what that meant even as he said it. "Not
sloppier — worse in the specific places a real, frightened voice would actually be worse. A hitch
that isn't planned. A breath that comes at the wrong moment because a real person's lungs don't wait
for a cue."

It took three attempts against the same commander's outer patrols before one finally slipped past
him — not because the pattern had gotten more skilled, but because, for the first time in the
craft's history, it had deliberately gotten less perfect, and the imperfection was the part that
worked.
