# What Corren Halst Carried Instead of a Blade

Corren Halst had fought beside Kanja since before the Black Trench, and he took the assignment to the
ledger office the same way he'd have taken any order — without complaint, and without understanding
why a man who could clear a field with a blade was carrying water sacks and a satchel of blank pages
instead.

"There's nothing here to protect anything with," he said, the second day, patting the empty place at
his hip out of old habit before catching himself doing it. "If someone means trouble, I've got my
hands and nothing else."

"That's the assignment," Kanja said. "Not because I don't trust you with a blade. Because the whole
promise of this office is that nothing in it is armed, and the first time someone sees a weapon on
anyone standing near this table, every word in that ledger stops mattering as much as the fact that
we were carrying steel the whole time we claimed otherwise."

Corren didn't love the answer, but he kept the post, and on the fourth day a group of hired men came
down from the shipping combine's own security roll to make the ledger office's continued operation
"inconvenient" — no weapons drawn on their side either, just size and number and the particular kind
of crowding that was meant to make a point without anyone having to say what the point was.

He put himself between them and the tent the way he'd once have put his blade between an enemy and a
wounded man, except there was nothing in his hands this time but the empty water satchel he'd been
carrying all morning, and he stood there anyway, close enough that the largest of the hired men would
have had to go through him bodily to reach the tent flap.

"I've got nothing on me," Corren told them, plainly, hands open at his sides. "You can see that for
yourselves. That's not an invitation. It's a fact. Whatever you mean to do here, you're going to have
to decide to do it to an unarmed man in front of everyone in this yard, and I don't think you came
here today prepared to be the first ones willing to be seen doing that."

They weren't. The crowding held for a tense several minutes and then thinned, the hired men drifting
off without ever having tested whether Corren's stillness was bluff, and he stood his post the rest
of the afternoon feeling, for the first time since the assignment began, that he'd understood what he
was actually guarding.

"Still rather have carried a blade," he told Kanja that evening, half a joke and half not.

"I know. You didn't need one today anyway."
