# What the Law Didn't Reach

*Locked canon, Batch 263, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1337`). Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle LXIV, wave 22. The Sovereign Trust's new storm-interval verification regulation, meant to protect fleets, is discovered to quietly exclude the small independent fishers who can't reach a certified reading station within the required radius.*

---

The complaint didn't arrive as a complaint. It arrived as three empty stalls at the fish market where there should have been forty, and it took the third student two days of asking around the docks before anyone would tell her why.

"We don't have a station," a skiff owner said, not looking up from the net she was mending. "Fifty leagues, the law says. Nearest certified reader's fifty-one leagues from where we put in. So by the letter of it, we're not covered. We're not required to consult anybody. We're also not *permitted* to move a fleet in a marked radius without consulting somebody, and a captain who does it without gets his license reviewed." She tied off a knot with more force than it needed. "So we just don't go out when it looks bad. We eat less those weeks. Nobody wrote that part down."

The student had helped build the regulation. She had sat across from the liaison who'd once refused to move his fleet on her word alone, watched him read her ledger on the dock, watched the law get written from that exact moment of proof. She had been, by any measure, part of the reason it existed. She had not once, in all of that, asked what a law written for fleets with captains and manuals and certified radii looked like to a woman with one boat and no manual at all.

She brought it to the retired successor first, because that was still the reflex, three years into the woman's retirement. "It's not wrong," the successor said, reading the regulation's text again as if she hadn't memorized it already. "It's just built for the size of thing we were when we wrote it. A fleet. Not a family with a boat."

Kanja, who had been on the harbor wall for most of the conversation without being asked into it, thought of how many of his own aliases had started the same way -- built to answer one kind of trouble and then discovered, later, to have left another kind of trouble standing exactly where it always was. "You didn't get it wrong," he said. "You got it first. That's different from getting it finished."

The student didn't argue that, though she didn't quite accept it either. She spent the following weeks doing something the written law had never asked of her and the ledger tradition had always quietly implied: she rode a borrowed skiff herself, thirty leagues past the last certified station, to the small harbors the regulation's radius had never touched, and she read the sky for them for free, in person, the old way, the way before there had been a law at all.

"This doesn't scale," she told the successor afterward, meaning it as an admission, not a boast.

"No," the successor agreed. "But it buys the people who need it something to hold until the part that does scale gets written. That's usually how the first fix works. It isn't the fix. It's the thing that keeps people alive until you write the fix."
