*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Valen defeated me thirty times this
evening. I do not know whether he counted. I did. Tomorrow I intend to lose a thirty-first time,
and enjoy that also."*

I record this one against my own better instincts, since nothing in it will read, to a stranger to
this account, as belonging in a chronicle of a woman who has broken sieges. But I have come to
believe the sieges are not the part of her that most needs recording, and a training deck at the
close of an ordinary day is as fair a place as any to say what I mean by that.

Valen keeps a ledger of his own, though not the kind Lauris keeps. He does not write anything down.
He counts. Thirty bouts, by his own private reckoning, each ending the same way — her guard opened
by a margin he could likely name to the fraction of an inch, had anyone thought to ask him for it.
He did not offer the number. She asked him for it directly, the way she asks most things, and he
gave it to her without ornament, the way he gives most things.

"Thirty," he said.

"All to you."

"Yes."

She considered this with an expression I have learned to recognize as delight rather than its more
common opposite. "Again."

I want to be precise about what I watched across the hour that followed, because I think precision
is the only honest tribute to a man who measures his own performance against a standard of waste
so narrow that ninety-nine thousand parts in a hundred thousand still leaves him dissatisfied. Valen
does not spar to be generous. He has never, in my observation, taken a bout less seriously because
his opponent enjoyed losing it. He met her thirty-first attempt with the same unhurried economy he
had given the first — weight shifted a fraction before she committed to it, the strike she had not
yet thrown already accounted for and closed off before it existed. The deck did not shudder under
either of them. Nothing in the room registered as urgent. It was, by any account a stranger might
give it, an uneventful hour between two people who are both, by other measures entirely, among the
most dangerous individuals on this world.

She lost the thirty-first exchange in four seconds, by my own count, which I kept mostly out of
habit rather than any expectation the number would matter to either of them. She sat down on the
deck itself rather than return to the bench along the wall, which I understood, watching her, as
its own kind of statement — that the loss had cost her nothing worth recovering from standing.

"You are not adjusting," Valen said. It was not quite a question, though I have found that very
little he says is quite a question.

"I am not trying to win."

"Then what are you trying to do."

She considered this with the same unhurried care she gives most things she has already decided the
answer to and simply wants to say correctly. "I am trying to feel exactly what my body can do,
against someone who will not let me feel anything less than the truth of it. You do not let me lie
to myself in here. I find that I enjoy not being permitted to lie to myself. It is restful, in its
own way, to be corrected by someone who is never wrong about the correction."

Valen received this the way I have watched him receive most compliments, which is to say without
any visible register of having received one at all, though he did sit down beside her on the deck
rather than remain standing over her, which I have learned, across enough of these evenings, to
read as the nearest equivalent he offers.

"You waste less than you did a year ago," he said, after a silence neither of them seemed inclined
to fill. "Not much less. Some."

"Is that generosity."

"It is arithmetic."

She laughed at that — not loudly, nothing in this account should be read as loud, but with the same
unguarded pleasure I have watched her spend on far larger occasions than a training deck at the end
of an unremarkable day. I do not think Valen intended the remark as humor. I am fairly certain she
knew that, and enjoyed it exactly as much for not being intended.

I have written elsewhere, and will likely write again, about the burdens this crew's leads each
carry — grief, doubt, deception, rage, the weight of what perception costs, the long patience of
time itself. I do not think Valen would describe himself as carrying anything at all; he would, I
suspect, call the description imprecise, and ask what specifically was meant by the word. But I
watched a man who judges every motion he makes against a standard that has never once let him rest
easy sit on a bare deck beside a woman who had just lost to him for the thirty-first time in a
single evening, and I watched something in the set of his shoulders that I am willing to call, for
lack of a term he would accept, something adjacent to rest. She did that. She does not do it by
trying. I have come to think that is the whole of the explanation, and I do not believe a longer one
would improve it.
