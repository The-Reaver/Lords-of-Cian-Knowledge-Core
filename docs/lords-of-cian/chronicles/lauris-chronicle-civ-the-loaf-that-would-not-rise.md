# Lauris Chronicle CIV: The Loaf That Would Not Rise

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1721`). One hundred fourth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of the second 50-Chronicle wave (Chronicles LX-CIX), the ninth entry of the
"Strand W" (Witness / present-day, quiet-register) thread within this wave. A second Ozmund Verehimu
entry, deliberately without Lilith Cyzak and in a lighter register than `MCD-1665`'s rampart --
showing `CC-134`'s framing of Lauris as counterweight to Ozmund's doubt through ordinary domestic
failure and shared laughter rather than grave affirmation, without touching or contradicting his
Crown-Scar, command, or succession material. No new named characters. Written under Abad's blanket

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Ozmund attempted to bake bread today for
a gathering he insists on hosting himself rather than delegating. The bread did not rise. He took
this more seriously than I believe the bread deserved. I told him so. He did not entirely believe me,
but he laughed, which was the part I actually wanted."*

I have written one account already of Ozmund Verehimu in a register I called, at the time, smaller
than intimacy and no less honest for it. I want to set down a second here, and I want to say plainly
at the start that this one has almost nothing grave in it, because I think a man who carries what he
carries deserves, in this record as much as in his own life, at least one account of himself doing
something badly and being allowed to laugh about it.

The gathering itself does not matter to the account -- some modest hosting obligation of his own
choosing, the kind he insists on managing personally rather than handing to staff better suited to
it, on principle I have heard him state more than once: that a man who will not bake his own household's
bread has no business asking others to carry weight he will not carry himself. It is, I think, an
admirable principle. It does not make him good at baking.

Lauris found him in the kitchen with flour to his elbows and an expression I had genuinely never
recorded on him before -- not the flat register that means he is saying a true, uncomfortable thing,
not the restless quiet of the rampart, but something closer to plain, ordinary frustration, aimed at
an inert lump of dough that had declined, for the second time that morning, to do what dough is
generally understood to do.

"It is not rising," he said, in the tone of a man delivering unwelcome intelligence.

She looked at the dough for a long moment with the same serious attention I have watched her bring to
cargo stacks and cracked hinges and cargo manifests that would not reconcile. "How long has it rested?"

"Forty minutes."

"Is the room warm?"

"I do not know. Should it be warm?"

"I believe so," she said. "I do not actually know either. I have never made bread. I know a great
deal about density and very little about yeast."

He set the bowl down with a considerably harder motion than the situation strictly called for, and I
watched something in his shoulders that I would ordinarily read as the doubt I have already written
of elsewhere in this account -- except that this time it broke, halfway through the gesture, into
something that was unmistakably, if briefly, a laugh.

"I have negotiated a twelve-year truce," he said. "I have refused a crown twice. I cannot make a loaf
of bread rise."

"I would not read too much into it."

"I am reading a great deal into it."

"Then read this into it instead," she said, and I want to record that she said it with no particular
weight, the way she says most things she has already decided are simply true. "You are bad at
something small, in a kitchen, with no one watching who requires you to be otherwise. I do not think
I have ever once seen you permitted to be merely bad at a thing. I find I like watching it."

He considered this, flour still on his hands, and did not immediately answer. I have learned, across
enough of these accounts, to recognize the particular stillness that means he is doing careful
arithmetic on something that matters to him more than his bearing lets on, and I want to be honest
that I expected, watching it settle over him in a kitchen of all places, that the arithmetic would
resolve into something graver than the moment deserved.

It did not. "Show me the dough again," he said instead.

They spent the better part of an hour after that failing at it together, by my own count -- a second
loaf that fared no better than the first, a third that fared marginally worse, Lauris offering
theories about warmth and pressure with the same unhurried seriousness she gives an actual siege, none
of which improved the outcome in any way I could observe. What struck me, watching from my usual place
at the edge of the room, was how little either of them seemed to mind the failure once it became
shared rather than solitary. He had arrived frustrated at a lump of dough that would not answer to
discipline the way everything else in his life is made to answer to it. He left, some hours later, with
three ruined loaves, flour on both of them, and an ease in his own bearing I do not believe the
gathering itself, had the bread actually risen, would have produced half so completely.

"I do not always know whether the crown I refused was mine to refuse," he had said to her once before,
on a rampart, in a register that carried the whole weight of his life in it. I do not think bread
resolves that any more than one flat sentence resolved it that night. But I watched a man who measures
nearly everything he does against a standard of consequence fail, cheerfully, at something with no
consequence attached at all, in the company of a woman who found the failure genuinely delightful
rather than something to be managed or excused, and I watched him leave the kitchen lighter than the
morning's frustration had any right to predict.

"The gathering will simply have someone else's bread," he said, resigned, at the door.

"A better decision than most you have made this week," she said, and he laughed again -- twice in one
morning, which I record here only because I do not believe I have ever set down that number for him
before, and because I think it is, in its own small way, the truest thing this account has to say
about what she is worth to him.
