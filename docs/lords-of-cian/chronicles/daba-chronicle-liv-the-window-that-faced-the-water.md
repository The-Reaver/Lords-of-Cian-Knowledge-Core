# Daba Chronicle LIV: The Window That Faced the Water

*Locked canon, Batch 316, 2026-09-28. Daba Chronicle LIV, first entry of a third wave. Close-third on
Daba, no dedicated narrator. Pays off Chronicle LIII's closing line ("he did not go that night
either. But he thought, for the first time, that he might") without fully resolving the larger
open question of what he wants for himself — a real, concrete step forward rather than a
retraction or a final answer. Reuses Mika. No new named characters.*

---

He went on a day nothing required him to.

That was the whole of the decision, in the end — not an argument won, not a crisis that happened
to route him past the coast road, just an ordinary morning with an empty ledger and no name added
to anything, and Daba found that an empty ledger, once he let himself notice it, was its own kind of
permission he had never given himself before.

The house was smaller than he'd pictured it, which surprised him more than it should have, given
that Mika had described it to him plainly and he had simply failed, in all the months since, to
build the real thing out of her words instead of some larger shape his own mind had substituted
without telling him. One story. A door that didn't latch quite square in its frame. A kitchen with
exactly the kind of window Mika had promised, facing water that was, this far up the coast, more
grey than blue, and utterly unremarkable, and somehow more interesting to look at than he'd expected
anything to be that asked nothing of him in return.

Mika didn't make a production of his arrival. She handed him a cup of something bitter she called
tea and was, by her own cheerful admission, still learning to brew correctly, and let him stand at
the window for as long as he wanted without narrating what he should be feeling about it.

"You don't have to say anything," she said, eventually, when the silence had gone on long enough
that she judged it needed permission rather than patience. "I didn't buy this to make you perform
gratitude at it."

"I'm not performing anything." He turned the cup once in his hands, not drinking, just holding it.
"I'm trying to work out what this is, exactly. What room it's supposed to occupy."

"It doesn't have to occupy a room. It can just be a house."

"Everything I've ever kept has had a function." He said it without complaint, the way he might
report a tide table. "The ledger. The list. The network. Even you, when we started — you had a
function, and I was honest about that at the time, and you were honest back."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know what function this is. A window. Bad tea. You, not doing anything to earn the
visit." He set the cup down, untouched, on the sill. "I keep waiting to feel like I'm spending
something. Time I owe somewhere else. And I don't. That's the part I don't have a category for."

Mika came and stood beside him at the window, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, not
quite. "Maybe that's what it's for. Not a function. Just the absence of the feeling that everything
has to be one."

He stayed the whole day, which was longer than he'd told himself he would, and shorter than Mika,
watching him from the doorway as he finally sat down at her kitchen table and let a silence pass
between them that had nothing urgent inside it, privately hoped it might someday become. He did not
promise to come back on any schedule. He did not, this time, need Mika to tell him the door stayed
open — he had walked through it once, on a day nothing required, and understood, in the plain
arithmetic he trusted more than any feeling, that a door walked through once could be walked through
again, and that this, too, was now simply true, filed alongside every other true thing he kept.

He left before dark, because the ledger back in the district would not read itself, and because he
had not, in fact, stopped being the man who counted things — only added, for the first time, one
more entry to the small private column of things that were not debts, and did not need to be repaid,
and existed, as far as he could tell, simply because he had let them.

The list of names had not grown that day either. He noted that too, on the walk back, the two facts
sitting side by side in his own accounting without contradiction: a debt that stayed unpaid, and a
window that faced the water, both true, both his, both worth keeping.
