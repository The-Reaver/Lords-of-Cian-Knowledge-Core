# The Ledger Against the Ledger

The moneylender kept his own book, bound in good leather, and he set it on the table across from
Ezio's plainer one like a man laying down a winning hand.

"Every worker in this district owes me before they owe anyone else," he said. "Tools, lodging,
advances against wages not yet earned. It's all here, dated and witnessed. Your ledger can say
whatever it likes about what they're owed. Mine says what they owe first."

"Then let's read them side by side," Kanja said, and pulled his own ledger open beside the
moneylender's, "starting with the same worker in both books, and see if the numbers agree with each
other, let alone with what actually happened."

They did not agree. A tool advance the moneylender's book dated to a spring three years past matched
no delivery record in the district's own supply logs from that season. A lodging debt charged to
four different workers, each book claiming the full amount owed individually rather than split, added
up to more than the building itself could have housed at once. A wage advance recorded against a
woman who, by the district's own employment record, hadn't started working there until two months
after the advance was supposedly given.

"Clerical error," the moneylender said, to the third one, then the fourth, then stopped saying it
once the pattern had repeated past the point where anyone in the yard, watching in a widening circle,
still believed it.

"Bring your scribe," Kanja said. "Let him look at his own entries next to the dates they claim to
record. I'm not asking you to explain anything to me. I'm asking your own book to explain itself to
the man who wrote it."

The scribe, a thin man who had clearly hoped never to be asked, went pale reading his own hand
across four years of entries laid open next to records he hadn't thought anyone would cross-check.
He did not defend the numbers. He put the pen down instead, and when the moneylender told him to pick
it back up and keep silent, he didn't.

"I can write what's true or I can write what I'm told," the scribe said. "I've been writing what
I'm told for four years because nobody ever asked to see both books at once. Somebody's asking now."

No blow was struck and no threat was issued across the whole exchange — the contest ran entirely on
paper, one ledger's internal contradictions laid bare against another's consistency, until the
moneylender's own hired hand refused to keep defending a book that couldn't defend itself.
