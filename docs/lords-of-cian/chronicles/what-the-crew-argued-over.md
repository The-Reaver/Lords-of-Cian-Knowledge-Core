# What the Crew Argued Over

*Locked canon, Batch 210, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-913`). Sovereign Ghost of the Great Sea Alias Chronicle XLVII, wave 16. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The argument had been building since the settlement burned, and it finally broke open on *The Receipt*'s
deck one evening when a young crewman — one of the settlement's own sons, taken aboard as a hand three
months prior because he'd had nowhere left to go — asked, flatly, why the next Trust prisoner they took
would get a boat and a promise instead of a knife.

"Because that's not who we are," Corren Halst said, the answer coming fast enough that it sounded more
like reflex than argument.

"It's who we were before six people I grew up with got killed by someone we let go." The crewman didn't
raise his voice. That was, somehow, worse than if he had. "I'm not asking you to feel what I feel. I'm
asking why the rule doesn't change when the rule gets people killed."

Efa Gol, who had buried a partner of her own at the Black Trench and understood exactly what it cost to
keep functioning through a loss the doctrine hadn't prevented, was the one who didn't try to answer him
with logic. "It should feel wrong to you that it doesn't change. If it stopped feeling wrong, that'd be
the actual problem."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's the truth instead of one." She sat down across from him rather than standing over him. "I
don't know if the rule is right. I know that every version of us that's tried the other rule — kill
what threatens you, trust nothing, assume the worst of every prisoner — ends up a fleet that looks
exactly like the ones we're fighting. I've seen those fleets. You haven't, yet, and I'd rather you
never have to."

Kanja arrived partway through, and didn't interrupt the argument to settle it — he let it run its course
first, listening the way he'd listened at the gathering at anchor months before, before finally saying
the one thing that wasn't an attempt to win the crewman over.

"I'm not going to tell you your anger is wrong. It isn't. Six people are dead who might not be if I'd
made a different call, and you're allowed to want a different call made next time." He met the
crewman's eyes directly. "What I can tell you is that I've already sat with exactly that question, alone,
longer than you've had to sit with it tonight — and I made the call I made anyway, not because it was
easy, but because the version of this fleet that stops trusting anyone the moment trust costs something
is a fleet I don't think is worth being a ghost for anymore."

The crewman didn't answer that night, and didn't come around to it cleanly in the weeks that followed —
he stayed aboard, did his work without complaint, but the argument was never fully resolved between them,
a genuine, unclosed disagreement the crew simply learned to carry rather than settle, the way they
carried every other cost the doctrine asked of them.
