# Lauris Chronicle LVII: Read It Right the First Time

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1674`). Fifty-seventh entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, Strand W (Witness / present-day, quiet register). A stakes-free entry pairing
Lauris with Efa Gol, whose established blunt, precise assessment style (`CC-130`) provides a
direct-speaking counterpart to Lauris's own plainness -- a mutual-recognition register rather than
a mystery or a combat showcase. No new named characters. Written under Abad's blanket
authorization: "lock it, continue uninterrupted, test and push to main."*

---

*Archive fragment, present-day: "Efa Gol looked at a stack of crates for four seconds and told me
exactly how it would fail before it failed. I asked her to teach me the trick of it. She told me
there was no trick. I liked her better for saying so."*

Efa Gol reads a problem the way I have watched other people read a familiar sentence — not slowly,
not cautiously, simply correctly, on the first pass, because she has trained herself for years to
see what is actually in front of her rather than what she expects to find there. I have written
before, of her own war record, that the text pairs her assessment style directly against panic: she
reads a man surveying trouble correctly because she has done the same herself, under considerably
worse circumstances than the one I am about to describe.

The circumstance, this time, was nothing more dramatic than a dockside stack of cargo crates loaded,
by someone in a hurry, in a configuration Efa took one look at and pronounced unsound.

"That'll go in the night," she said, to no one in particular, as she and Lauris passed it. "Third
tier from the bottom's carrying weight it wasn't stacked to carry. Give it eight hours, maybe less
if the wind picks up."

Lauris stopped and looked at the stack for considerably longer than four seconds, turning her own
attention on it with the particular seriousness she brings to anything offered as a claim rather
than an opinion. "How do you know that without touching it?"

"I don't know it. I read it. There's a difference, if you want it explained, though I'll tell you
now the explanation's less interesting than you're hoping."

"I would still like it explained."

Efa obliged her, and what followed — I was present for the whole of it, having trailed the two of
them down to the dock on an errand of my own that I confess became secondary the moment I realized
what I was about to overhear — was, in essence, a masterclass delivered by a woman who does not
consider herself a teacher, to a student considerably more capable than her instructor in every
respect except this one specific, narrow skill. Efa Gol reads load distribution the way she once
read decoy formations and diversion routes: by pattern, by the small tells a stack gives off before
it fails, by having watched enough things go wrong to recognize, instantly, the shape of a thing
about to go wrong again.

"You could crush that whole stack with one hand," Efa said, not unkindly, "and it still wouldn't
teach you to see the third tier was wrong before it fell. That's not strength. That's just
paying attention long enough that you stop needing to guess."

Lauris considered this with what I can only describe as genuine, unguarded interest — the specific
attention she reserves for something she has correctly identified as a gap in her own competence,
rather than a challenge to it. "Show me the tell again."

Efa showed her. Twice. By the third pass, Lauris had it — not the years of accumulated instinct
behind it, which no single afternoon could teach, but the particular visual signature Efa had
learned to watch for, and I watched her apply it, unprompted, to two other stacks further down the
dock, correctly identifying one as sound and one as marginal, the latter earning a short nod of
approval from Efa that I do not believe Efa hands out often.

"You'll not read it as fast as me for a while yet," Efa said, with the same blunt honesty she
extends to everyone. "But you're not guessing anymore. That's most of the work."

"I would still like to be faster."

"Everyone would. Come back tomorrow and I'll show you the rest of it."

I record this exchange not because a stack of cargo crates carries any weight of its own in this
crew's larger story, but because I think it says something true and rarely shown about a woman most
of this account has spent describing at the height of her own competence. Lauris Letitia does not,
in my experience, often occupy the position of the person still learning something. She occupied it
gladly that afternoon, without a trace of the impatience I might have expected from someone who has
spent six thousand years being very good at almost everything she attempts. She simply wanted to see
the tell correctly, the way Efa saw it, and was pleased — genuinely, unqualifiedly pleased, in the
register I have come to recognize as entirely her own — to have been handed one more thing worth
being good at.
