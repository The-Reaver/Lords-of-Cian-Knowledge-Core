# Atunbi Chronicle I: What Takes Root

*Locked canon, Batch 85, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-348`). First entry in Atunbi's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Oluwole (`PH2-025`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`) through Aztlán Chronicle I (`MCD-347`). Narration is
close-third on Oluwole throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Continues Los Angeles's own run of
territory Chronicles. Slots into no existing mainline battle — original homage-era material set in
Atunbi itself.*

---

Oluwole had cleared the lot with his own hands before he'd ever asked anyone else's, the way he
cleared every lot: alone, first, so no one could ever say he'd asked for something he wasn't willing
to do himself. Rubble out, soil turned, the first rows planted in ground that had been ash a season
before. By the time the second planting came in, the ground itself had stopped behaving like
ordinary ground. It held water longer. It gave less under a hard rain. It had, in some way Oluwole
never claimed to fully understand and never once tried to explain to anyone who hadn't watched it
happen themselves, started remembering that someone had stayed.

The stranger had been helping turn soil three mornings running by the time the trouble came, saying
little, asking nothing, the kind of steady presence Oluwole had learned not to question too closely
— a lot this size needed every set of hands it could get, and questions were a luxury for lots that
weren't racing a season.

The trouble arrived at dawn, before the crew, before anyone but Oluwole and the stranger were
awake to see it: three men with a demolition order and a truck, sent to clear the lot for a
development neither of them had ever heard proposed to anyone in the neighborhood. Fast. In and
out before the block woke up enough to organize a single body to stand in front of it.

"You've got no papers posted," Oluwole said, standing between the truck and the first row of
seedlings, not moving, not raising his voice.

"We've got the order. That's what we've got." The lead man didn't look at the garden, only at the
clock he was clearly racing. "You've got ten minutes to clear whatever you want off this lot before
we clear all of it."

Oluwole didn't argue the order's legitimacy — there'd be time for that fight later, in a room with
people who could actually rule on it. He knew, standing there, exactly what the ground under his
feet could and couldn't do for him in this specific minute, and he didn't waste the minute pretending
otherwise. "This ground held one bad year already. It'll hold whatever's coming next too, given
time. It just doesn't have ten minutes' worth of that kind of time. Nothing I've ever built here does,
against something that moves this fast."

The truck took the first third of the garden before the block woke up enough to matter, before
enough bodies arrived to make the fast thing slow down and become, suddenly, a much harder
argument to finish. It stopped there — not because Oluwole's gift had reached out and stopped it,
but because thirty neighbors standing in a road do the same job a slow reclamation can't, when
speed is the only weapon on the other side.

"You could have lost all of it," the stranger said afterward, surveying the third that hadn't
survived the morning.

"I did lose a third of it. I'm not going to pretend the ground protected what it couldn't protect."
Oluwole knelt at the torn edge where the truck had turned, already sketching in his head where the
next planting would start. "Whatever this is, whatever's happened to the dirt from staying here long
enough, it isn't fast. It was never going to be fast. Fast is the one thing I can't give it, no
matter how many seasons I put into a lot." He looked at what remained — two-thirds, still standing,
still holding water the way ordinary dirt didn't. "Which means the answer isn't to make the ground
faster. It's to make sure there are always more hands than they can outrun before it matters."

"So you plant again."

"I plant again. Same lot, this time with thirty more people who now know exactly what's worth
standing in a road for at dawn." He was already turning soil by the time he finished the sentence,
the way he answered everything that tried to take something from him. "That's the whole of the
method. Don't move. Improve. Even the parts that get taken back, eventually, if you keep doing it
long enough."

The stranger was gone by the time the second planting went in that afternoon, the way he always
seemed to be. He left nothing carved, no phrase repeated hand to hand — only, the following
morning, a fourth of new seedlings already standing in the torn ground where the truck had turned,
planted by no one anyone in the crew could account for.
