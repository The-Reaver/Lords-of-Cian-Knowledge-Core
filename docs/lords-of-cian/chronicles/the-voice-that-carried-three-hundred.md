# The Voice That Carried Three Hundred

*Locked canon, Batch 123, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-418`). Crow King Alias Chronicle VI, closing the
second wave. Rebellion era, from the perspective of one of the crew's Hymn-Engine singers. Not a
territory Chronicle. New standalone material. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named
characters.*

---

She had ruined her voice twice in six weeks of rehearsal before the Braid's three-part harmony
finally held steady across a full four-hour cycle, and she carried the rasp it left behind for the
rest of her life, a permanent low edge to her ordinary speaking voice that strangers sometimes asked
about and she rarely explained in full.

"People hear about the Hymn-Engine and think it's a trick," she told a younger recruit, years after
the war, teaching the technique to a new generation of singers who'd never lived through the
Rebellion itself. "A clever idea, executed once, admired from a safe distance. Nobody who wasn't in
the marsh understands what it actually costs to hold one frequency, precisely, for four straight
hours, knowing that if your voice cracks even once at the wrong moment, the whole false signature
collapses and three hundred people you love don't make it out."

"How did you learn to do it?"

"Badly, at first. Everyone did. I lost my voice entirely for eleven days after the first real
rehearsal — not from the Hymn-Engine's design, which is actually gentler on the throat than people
assume, but from my own fear making me sing too hard, too tense, fighting the note instead of
letting it live in me the way he kept telling us it needed to." She demonstrated, briefly, the
relaxed, sustained tone the Braid actually required, worlds apart from the effortful strain a
frightened new singer instinctively reached for. "He never once yelled at any of us for getting it
wrong those first weeks. Just kept saying the same thing: the marsh doesn't need you to sound
powerful. It needs you to sound consistent, for longer than feels possible, and consistency is a
harder skill than power. Took most of us the full six weeks to actually believe him."

The recruit asked, eventually, the question every new singer eventually worked up the nerve to ask.
"Was it worth it? What it cost your voice?"

"Every single time." She didn't hesitate. "I've got a rasp I'll carry the rest of my life, and
somewhere out there are people who are alive specifically because of what that rasp cost me to earn.
That's not a hard trade to make peace with, once you've actually made it. The hard part was never
deciding it was worth it. The hard part was surviving the six weeks of practice before you got good
enough to make the trade in the first place."
