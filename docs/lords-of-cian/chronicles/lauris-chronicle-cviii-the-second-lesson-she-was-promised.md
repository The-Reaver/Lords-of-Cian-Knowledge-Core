# Lauris Chronicle CVIII: The Second Lesson She Was Promised

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1725`). One hundred eighth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of the second 50-Chronicle wave (Chronicles LX-CIX), the thirteenth entry of
the "Strand W" (Witness / present-day, quiet-register) thread within this wave. A direct sequel to
`MCD-1674` ("Read It Right the First Time"), paying off Efa Gol's own closing line there ("Come back
tomorrow and I'll show you the rest of it") -- extends her established blunt, precise assessment
style (`CC-130`) from reading cargo stacks to reading people and formations, drawing on her Rebellion
command of decoy and diversion forces without restaging any of that operational history. No new named
characters. Written under Abad's blanket authorization: "go."*

---

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Efa Gol promised me the rest of it, some
while ago now. I did not forget. I do not think she expected me to remember, and I do not think she
minded, either way, that I did."*

I recorded, some time ago now, an afternoon in which Efa Gol read a dockside cargo stack's coming
failure from four seconds' observation and spent the better part of an hour teaching Lauris the tell
behind it. I closed that account with a promise neither of them seemed to consider especially binding
-- come back tomorrow, and I'll show you the rest of it -- and I confess I did not expect to be
writing a second account of the same promise being kept, since I have watched enough casual offers
made and quietly forgotten across the whole of this record to have assumed this one might join them.

It did not join them. Lauris returned, not the following day, as it happened, but some weeks later,
and I want to record that Efa Gol did not treat the delay as any kind of lapse on either of their
parts.

"You're later than I said," Efa observed, by way of greeting, with the same blunt economy she extends
to everyone.

"You did not say when. You said tomorrow, and I did not come tomorrow. I came when I had something
worth bringing you to look at instead."

Efa considered this with something close to approval. "That's a better reason than most people give me
for being late."

What Lauris had brought was not a cargo stack this time, but a question -- one she put to Efa plainly,
without preamble, the way she puts most questions she has decided are worth the asking. "You commanded
decoy forces. Diversion. You read a stack of crates the same way you must have read a battle line,
before it moved. I would like to understand the second thing, if the first taught you anything useful
toward it."

I want to note, watching this exchange, that I had not expected Efa Gol -- a woman I have described
elsewhere as blunt to the point of near-total verbal economy -- to answer a question of this size with
anything more than a single flat sentence. She surprised me. She sat down across from Lauris at the
same dockside table where their first lesson had begun, and for the better part of an afternoon she
did something I do not believe I had ever seen her do before: she taught, at length, and with real
patience for a student who was, by every other measure in this account, already the most capable
individual either of them was likely to meet.

"A stack fails because weight's carrying wrong and nobody's looked closely enough to see it," Efa
said. "A formation fails the same way. Somebody's carrying more than they're built to carry, and
everyone around them is too busy watching the front of the line to notice the third tier's about to
go. I didn't read enemy formations by counting spears. I read them by finding the man who was about to
break under weight nobody else had noticed he was carrying, and I hit that seam first, every time,
because a formation doesn't fail from the front. It fails from wherever the weight was wrong all
along."

Lauris took this in with the same close, unhurried attention I have watched her bring to Sephtis's
architecture notes and Dol Maren's hull assessments both -- the particular pleasure she takes, I have
come to understand across enough of these accounts now, in being handed a true thing correctly
explained by someone who has earned the right to explain it. "You read people the way you read
crates."

"People are crates that talk," Efa said, not unkindly. "Doesn't change the physics. Everybody's
carrying something. Most people hide it well enough that you'd never know to look, unless you've
trained yourself the way I trained myself -- by watching enough things fail that you stop needing to
guess where the failure's coming from."

"Show me the tell," Lauris said, which I recognized, having recorded their first lesson, as precisely
what she had asked the first time, in precisely the same register.

Efa showed her -- not on a formation, since none was present to demonstrate on, but on a crowd of
ordinary dockworkers some distance off, walking Lauris through the small, telltale signs of a man
carrying more worry than his bearing let on, a woman holding herself the specific careful way of
someone managing pain she did not want noticed. Lauris watched, and then, unprompted, identified a
third figure herself -- a young dockhand whose forced ease, she said, read to her now as the same
kind of wrongly-carried weight Efa had just described, though she could not yet say what the weight
actually was.

Efa looked where she was pointing, considered it for a long moment, and nodded, once, with the same
short approval I recorded her giving the first time this happened, over a stack of crates rather than
a person. "That's the tell. You'll not know what he's carrying without asking him plain, and I'd not
recommend guessing wrong at a man's private trouble. But you saw it before I pointed it out this time.
That's the whole of the second lesson, and you've already half-taught it to yourself."

"I would still like a third lesson."

"There's always a third lesson," Efa said, rising from the table with the same unhurried finality she
brings to any completed assessment. "Come back when you've got something worth bringing me to look at.
I've found you're better at waiting for the right question than most people I've taught."

I record this account not for the skill itself, which will likely never be the thing history remembers
Lauris Letitia for, but for the quieter fact underneath it: that a woman who has spent six thousand
years being the most formidable individual in nearly every room she enters continues, plainly and
without any visible impatience, to seek out the rooms where she is not, and to find in them not
frustration at her own gap, but the same unqualified, competent pleasure she has always carried into
every other room besides.
