# Lauris Chronicle LXVIII: The One Thing They Asked in Return

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1685`). Sixty-eighth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, the eleventh entry of Strand K. Set at age ~3,960, roughly 380 years after
Chronicle LXVII (`MCD-1684`) opened the Iron-Speaker deliberation, closing it exactly as `MCD-174`
already locks: the Council agreeing to support her departure and asking only that she preserve a
record of what she found -- the origin scene for the standing archive debt already locked at
`MCD-211`. Reuses Veska Karth-Ven. No new named characters. No contradictions.*

-----

*Archive fragment, Vask-of-Vasks, age ~3,960: "Three hundred and eighty years is a long time to wait
for one word. I find I do not mind that it was long. I mind, a little, that it took this long to ask
so small a thing of me in return."*

I do not intend to narrate the full three hundred and eighty years of deliberation this entry
finally closes, both because Lauris's own archive does not preserve it in continuous detail — she
attended, by her own count, only the hearings that required her direct testimony, some eleven of
them across the whole period, and otherwise continued her ordinary duties without apparent
disruption — and because I do not believe the substance changed meaningfully across most of that
span. What I have gathered, from Veska's far more complete correspondence and from the Vask-of-Vasks
minute-keepers who did attend every session, is a Council that spent the great majority of those
centuries not arguing the merits of her departure, which had largely settled within the first
sixty years, but working through the practical and ceremonial architecture of a thing that had never
happened before in the civilization's recorded history: releasing, by its own consent rather than by
loss, the last documented Karesian it would ever produce.

I want to note, because I think it matters to how this whole account should be understood, that this
was not obstruction. A people reduced to fourteen thousand does not deliberate for three hundred and
eighty years out of bureaucratic habit. It deliberates that long because it has almost nothing left
to lose carelessly, and because — I believe this is the truer reason, reading between the lines of
Veska's own correspondence — it wished, in some way none of the surviving records state outright, to
give the departure the full weight of everything Kares Prime still knew how to give: time, attention,
and the unhurried seriousness of a civilization that had learned, across its own long decline, that
haste had rarely served it well.

*

The final hearing, in the three-hundred-and-eightieth year, was by every account the shortest of the
eleven Lauris attended. Veska's own record of it is only a page, which I take as its own kind of
evidence — that by this point, there was little left to say that had not already been said many times
over. The senior Iron-Speaker who had first raised the strongest objection, three centuries and eighty
years earlier, is recorded as speaking first, and I have chosen to preserve her closing words here in
full, because I believe they are the truest summary of what this whole long deliberation was actually
about:

*We cannot verify what you have told us of the world below. We have decided that this does not
matter as much as we once believed it did. You have given this civilization seventeen hundred and
eighty years of service and asked nothing of it in return but permission to leave when you had
nothing further to learn here. We are not in a position, any longer, to refuse someone that little,
however much we may wish, privately, that circumstances allowed us to.*

What the Council asked of her in exchange — and I want to be precise here, because it is a smaller
request than I believe most readers will expect after three hundred and eighty years of
deliberation — was one thing only. Not a return. Not a mandated report at fixed intervals. Not even
a promise of continued service to whatever institution she found. Only this: that she preserve a
record of what she found beyond the homeworld, as the civilization's sole continuing testimony to a
wider world it would never itself see again.

*

Lauris's own recorded answer, given — her archive notes — without visible emotion, though I confess
I no longer trust that word to mean, for her, quite what it means for anyone else I have narrated:
*I have kept a record of everything I have done since I was old enough to hold a stylus. I do not
know how to promise you anything else. I can promise you that.*

I have thought, transcribing this exchange, about the archive I am drawing this entire chronicle
from — the same one she has kept faithfully across every subsequent age, aboard the Karkosa, through
forty Directorate contracts and a defection and a life built around Ezio Valcari's crew that none of
these Iron-Speakers could have imagined existing. I do not think any of them, on that final day of
deliberation, could have known how completely she would keep this single promise, or for how many
thousands of years, or how much of what I have transcribed in this whole series exists only because
a Council of women she would never see again once asked her, after three hundred and eighty years of
careful consideration, for nothing more than her word.

Her closing line for the entry, brief even by her own standard: *They have said yes. I do not yet
know what to do with the permission, now that I have it. I expect I will find out.*
