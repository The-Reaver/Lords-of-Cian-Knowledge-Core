# Lauris Chronicle LIV: The Weight He Asked Her to Carry Gently

*Archive fragment, present-day: "He asked me to hold something that would break if I held it
wrong. Not something that would break if I dropped it. Something that would break if I so much as
breathed on it incorrectly. I have never been asked to be gentle before. I found that I liked being
asked."*

Kanja does not, in my long experience of writing him from the outside of his own chapters, often
ask anyone for anything he could not more easily accomplish himself. It is one of the small,
persistent frictions of his own leadership, I think, though I have never once heard him name it as
such: the habit of a man who has spent centuries being the largest capacity in any room learning,
imperfectly and by increments, to hand something to someone else instead.

What he brought to her that afternoon was not large. It was, if anything, insultingly small for a
woman whose reputation across two continents rests on what she has done to walls, to hulls, to the
occasional fortified door. A clock — an old one, brought back from some errand I was not present
for and am not at liberty to detail, its inner mechanism a lattice of brass so fine that I could
not have identified, looking at it, which end was meant to face up. It had stopped keeping time
some years before whoever gave it up ever parted with it, and the parting, Kanja said, had cost that
person more than the clock itself was worth, which was reason enough, in his accounting, to see it
running again.

"I need someone to hold the housing open while the pieces are reseated," he said. "Without moving.
For as long as it takes."

She looked at it for a long moment before she answered, and I understood, watching her, that she
was doing something I do not think anyone watching from a greater distance would have recognized:
she was calculating, with real seriousness, exactly how little of her own capacity the task would
require, and whether she trusted herself to hold precisely that little and nothing more.

"How long is 'as long as it takes'?"

"I don't know yet."

"Then I'll find out with you."

What followed was, by any ordinary measure, an uneventful hour. She held the clock's brass housing
open along two hinge-seams with four fingers and a thumb, her whole visible attention narrowed to a
pressure I am told was measured in a fraction most instruments could not register, while Kanja
worked inside it with tools too small for me to see clearly from where I sat. Her hand did not move.
It did not so much as tremble the specific, telltale amount that even the steadiest human hand
tends to at the hour mark. I have watched this woman put her whole density behind a single blow and
end an engagement in it. I had never, before that afternoon, watched her spend an hour holding
almost nothing at all, and found I could not look away from either version of her.

"You could ask anyone for this," she said, midway through, not quite a complaint.

"I could ask anyone to hold it," he said. "I couldn't ask anyone to hold it and not think about
holding it. You stopped thinking about it four minutes in. I watched you do it."

She considered this with the same unhurried seriousness she brings to everything, including, I
have come to notice, compliments she did not expect to receive. "I like this," she said finally.
"Not the clock. Being trusted with something that would notice if I got it wrong. Most things I am
given do not notice. This would have."

He did not answer that immediately, and when he did, it was with the particular plainness I have
learned, across enough chapters of his own story, to recognize as the register he reserves for the
few things he means exactly as stated. "That's most of why I brought it to you instead of fixing it
alone."

The clock ran, eventually, a small mechanical sound neither of them made any particular ceremony
of noticing. She did not ask what became of it afterward, and I did not either, though I record here
that Kanja returned it to wherever it had come from with no further comment to me about the matter,
which is, in its own way, as much of an answer as either of them tends to give.

I offer this account for a narrower reason than most of what I have written of either of them. It is
easy, across a story this long, to let a woman capable of ending a battle in ninety seconds become,
in a reader's memory, only the sum of what she can break. I wanted, once, to set down what she does
with the same body when the task is not to break anything at all — when the entire measure of her
competence is how little of it she is willing to spend, and how plainly she enjoys the discipline of
spending exactly that little and no more.
