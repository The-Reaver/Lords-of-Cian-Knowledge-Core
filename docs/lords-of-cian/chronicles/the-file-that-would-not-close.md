# The File That Would Not Close

*Draft only — not yet locked, pending Abad's approval. Bane Alias Chronicle VI, closing Bane's
second three-Chronicle wave. Rebellion era, within the "Bane" window, told through the Directorate's
own internal classification process for the alias itself -- the origin of the formal threat file
`MCD-232` already references ("'Bane' is the Directorate's own classification for a threat that
destroys the force built to destroy it"). Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material.
Narrated in a documentary/report register distinct from the other Chronicles. No new named
characters.*

---

The analyst assigned to close the Bane classification file had been given a simple instruction:
compile the existing field reports, assign a standard threat-response protocol, and submit the file
for archival so operational commanders would have a single reference document instead of eleven
scattered incident reports written by eleven different officers who had each encountered him under
different circumstances and reached different, sometimes contradictory conclusions.

It should have taken a week. It took the analyst four months, three formal extensions, and one
increasingly irritated series of memos from a superior who did not understand why a routine
compilation had become the office's longest-running unfinished task.

The problem, the analyst eventually admitted in an internal memo that was itself nearly refused for
being unprofessionally candid, was that the standard threat-response taxonomy had no category that
fit. Every existing classification assumed a consistent operational signature — a preferred weapon,
a preferred terrain, a predictable escalation pattern an officer in the field could train against.
Bane's file, compiled honestly, showed a pattern only in its total absence of pattern: a man who
sealed himself into a ravine at the Black Trench and stood exposed on open salt-pan weeks later; who
fought with overwhelming force in one report and refused to fight at all in another written the same
season; who was, by every metric the taxonomy measured, simultaneously the most and least
predictable subject in the Directorate's entire active file.

"The name itself is the closest thing to an accurate classification we're going to get," the
analyst wrote, in the memo that nearly ended the assignment. "'A threat that destroys the force
built to destroy it' isn't a threat-response category. It's an admission that the category doesn't
exist yet, dressed up as one so the file could be closed on schedule. I can write you a document
that satisfies the format. I cannot write you a document that tells a field officer what to actually
expect, because the honest answer is: expect the specific thing your own preparation didn't account
for. That's not useful intelligence. It's also the only true thing in this entire file."

The file was archived eventually, format satisfied, superior placated, eleven incident reports
condensed into one official document that field officers would go on citing for years without ever
reading the analyst's own closing note, buried in an appendix nobody outside the archive department
ever opened: *This classification will require continuous revision. The subject is not becoming more
predictable with time. He is becoming more consistently unpredictable, which is itself the only
consistent thing about him.*
