# The Blade She Almost Didn't Sheathe

*Locked canon, Batch 235, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1043`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle LX, wave 20, closing
the wave. A quieter internal-doctrine entry: Kanja enforces the persona's non-lethal restraint from
within his own crew rather than against an outside threat. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose.*

---

The slaving captain had already struck his colors, already opened his own hold, already done every
single thing the reputation required of a man who wanted to walk away from a Scourge boarding with
his life. It should have ended there. It nearly didn't.

Sena had come up out of that same class of hold herself, nine years before, and something about the
particular way this captain flinched away from the boarding party — hands up, eyes down, the posture
of a man who'd learned exactly how much space to give people who could hurt him — put her back inside
a memory she hadn't chosen to have on deck that day. Her blade was at his throat before anyone,
Kanja included, had crossed half the distance to stop her.

He didn't run at her. Running at a woman holding a blade to a pinned man's throat was how you got the
blade to move. He stopped an arm's length short instead, close enough to speak low, far enough that
nothing about his approach could read as a threat to either of them.

"He's already surrendered," Kanja said. "You don't owe him anything. But I need you to hear the next
part before your hand decides for you — this isn't about what he deserves. It's about what happens to
the next hold full of people if the word gets out that surrendering to us doesn't actually save you."

"He'd have done it to someone else. He *has* done it to someone else."

"He has. And every captain who hears what you do to him next decides, in that same second, whether
fighting to the last man is worth trying instead of striking his colors. That's the whole trade we've
built, Sena. Every surrender we honor is a hundred people who never have to be rescued at all, because
the fight never happens. Kill him now and you haven't punished him for what he did to you. You've
made it more likely it happens to someone else, tomorrow, on a ship that decides fighting's the safer
bet."

She held the blade there long enough that he genuinely didn't know which way it would go. Then her
hand loosened, not because the anger had left her — it hadn't, he could see that plainly — but because
she'd chosen, with the anger still in her, to put the blade away anyway.

He didn't praise her for it in front of the crew, and didn't let anyone else make a lesson of it
either. Later, alone at the rail, he told her the part he didn't tell most people: that he'd carried
the same restraint through every one of these moments for decades, that it never got easier to want,
only more automatic to choose, and that the choosing was the only part of it that had ever actually
been his.
