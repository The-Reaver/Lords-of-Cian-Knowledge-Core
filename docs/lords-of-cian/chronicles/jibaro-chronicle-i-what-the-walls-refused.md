# Jibaro Chronicle I: What the Walls Refused

*Locked canon, Batch 81, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-344`). First entry in Jibaro's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Omoba (`PH2-042`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`), Umoja Chronicle I (`MCD-335`), Yara Chronicle I (`MCD-336`),
Areíto Chronicle I (`MCD-339`), Guanín Chronicle I (`MCD-340`), Borikén Chronicle I (`MCD-341`), Ide

Omoba had walked into the seminary on a Tuesday with eleven people and no plan beyond staying. By
Wednesday there were forty. By the following Tuesday, when the diocese's own lawyers finally arrived
with a writ they were certain would end it by lunch, something about the building itself had already
decided the matter without asking anyone's permission — least of all the men holding the paper.

He did not know, that first week, that the something was him. He learned it the way he learned
everything of consequence in his life: by watching what happened when someone tried to take it back.

The city sent men first, the kind whose whole authority lived in a uniform rather than anything
underneath it, expecting a door and a crowd of frightened squatters to scatter at the sight of them.
The door held. Not locked — Omoba had never once locked it, on principle, since a sanctuary that
needed a lock wasn't proving the point he meant it to prove — simply, inexplicably, unmovable in a
way that made the lead officer's shoulder bounce off it twice before he stopped trying a third time
and looked, for the first time, honestly frightened of a piece of wood.

"It's just a door," he said, to no one in particular.

"It was," Omoba said, from the stairwell above, not moving to stop him, not needing to. "A week ago
you'd have been right through it in under a minute. I don't know what it is now any more than you
do. I only know it happened because we stayed, and I don't intend to test what happens if we leave."

The stranger had been inside three days by then, carrying crates of donated bread up from the
kitchen without being asked twice, the way he did everything. He'd said little, asked less, and
Omoba had stopped cataloguing him the way he cataloged everyone new the moment he understood the
man wasn't measuring the room for anything — not a threat, not a recruit, not a story. Just a pair
of hands that happened to be useful.

The diocese's lawyers came back the next morning with a different argument entirely — not force this
time, paper, a claim that the seminary's grounds included an adjoining half-acre lot, empty, unused,
that the daycare program had been quietly eyeing for a fenced play yard. Legally separate from the
building itself. Omoba's people had never actually held it, not for a single full day, let alone the
week the seminary itself had absorbed.

They took it back inside an hour. No door bounced anyone off it. No wall refused a boot. It was, by
any measure, simply ground, the same as any other ground in the city, and it yielded the same way
any ordinary thing yields to someone determined enough to take it.

"You could still hold that lot," the stranger said afterward, watching the fence go up around it
from the seminary's own steps. "Same as the building. Why didn't it work the same way?"

"I've been asking myself that for an hour." Omoba turned it over slowly, the way he turned over
everything since the reinvention, refusing to let a hard question go unanswered just because the
answer was inconvenient. "The building was never really the diocese's to lose. It was always half
already given — a place people came to be told they mattered, run by people who'd have been ashamed,
publicly, to admit they cared more about the deed than the children inside it. The lot was never
that. Just dirt with a fence around a fence they hadn't built yet. Nobody was ashamed of taking that
back, because nobody had ever pretended it was for anyone but themselves."

"So it's not the walls."

"It was never the walls. It's whether the people who'd have to take it back can live with what
taking it back would say about them." Omoba looked at the seminary doors, still standing open the
way they had every day since the first Tuesday. "This building forgives them nothing if they break
it down. That lot never asked anyone to be ashamed of anything, so there was nothing here to stop
them."

He did not try to reclaim the half-acre. He built the clinic inside the seminary instead, one floor
down from where it was meant to go, and let the fenced lot sit empty as a plain, visible reminder of
exactly where his own reach actually ended — a boundary he chose to leave standing rather than argue
with, because arguing with it would have meant pretending the gift worked everywhere, and he had
decided, somewhere in that first honest hour, that a leader who lied to his own people about the
shape of his own power was worse than one with less power and no illusions about it.

The stranger left before the clinic's first patients came through the door, the way he always
seemed to. He left nothing carved, no phrase repeated hand to hand — only, weeks later, a second
bread crate delivery that no one in the kitchen could trace to any supplier they'd actually ordered
from.
