# Two Different Kinds of Command

series, Chronicle LXIII — the Aethelgard strand's third wave, fourth entry, set strictly pre-Book-1,

Of all the things the General told me about his parents, this was the one he reached for whenever
someone asked him, carelessly, whether his mother and father had actually gotten along — a question he
said people asked more often than they should have, as though a War-Order tactician and a Wetlands lord
made for an unlikely enough pairing that strangers assumed the marriage must have been arranged out of
necessity rather than lived in with any real warmth. He always told them this one. It answered the
question better than any explanation of his own could have.

The matter itself was small enough on paper — river bandits, a handful of them, working the grain
barges along one of the quieter channels that fed the House's own stores, three raids in as many
months, no one killed yet but property gone and one bargeman badly hurt in the last of them. It reached
Aethelgard's desk the ordinary way, through a steward's report, and it was in the room where he and Val
Mirel disagreed about what to do with it that the General said he learned, properly, that his parents
governed from two entirely different places and had built an entire marriage out of neither one ever
winning outright.

His mother wanted it ended cleanly, and fast. The General said she read the pattern of the three raids
the way she read everything — the spacing, the routes, the fact that each hit had been slightly bolder
than the last — and told his father plainly that a problem left unanswered for three raids running
would not stay at the scale of three raids running. Send a disciplined party to the most likely fourth
target, take the raiders whole, end it before someone besides a bargeman's shoulder got hurt. Clean,
decisive, over.

His father wanted to know who they were first. Not out of softness, the General was careful to say —
Aethelgard didn't dispute that the raids had to stop. What he wanted was an answer to the question his
wife's read of the pattern didn't ask: why three raids, now, on that particular channel, by people
willing to risk House retaliation for grain barges rather than easier targets elsewhere. He said a
threat you end without understanding is a threat you've only postponed, because whatever pushed those
people toward the river in the first place would still be there to push the next ones after them were
gone.

The General said the argument that followed wasn't unkind, exactly, but it wasn't gentle either — his
mother telling his father that not every problem needed its causes understood before it could be
stopped, that sometimes the House simply needed a wall put up quickly, and his father telling her that a
wall put up without knowing what it was keeping out tended to need rebuilding within the year. Neither
backed down. The General remembered his mother, at one point, telling his father dryly that his kind of
patience was a virtue and hers was simply boredom with waiting for men to reason their way to the
obvious, and his father laughing — actually laughing, in the middle of an argument neither of them had
lost yet — and telling her that was, without question, the most Kareth thing he'd ever heard her say.

What they settled on wasn't a compromise so much as two separate answers running at once. Val Mirel took
a small, disciplined party to intercept the next raid directly — quick, precise, exactly as decisive as
she'd wanted, and it worked; the raiders were taken whole inside a week, no one further hurt. Aethelgard,
meanwhile, rode out along the same stretch of river on his own, the way he rode out to anything that
didn't sit right with him, and found what his wife's read of the pattern hadn't needed to find: a
fishing village two channels over that had lost its entire catch to an unrelated blight that spring,
turning desperate men toward barges they'd never have touched in an ordinary year. He arranged relief
before the next lean season could produce a fourth raid from people his wife's soldiers hadn't yet met.

The General told me he asked his mother, once, years later, whether it had bothered her that his
father's slower answer had turned out to matter as much as her faster one. She told him, he said, that
it had never once occurred to her to see it that way — that a household with only one kind of command
in it was a household waiting to be wrong about something the other kind would have caught. He said it
was the plainest description of his parents' marriage he ever heard either one of them give, and that
neither of them, as far as he knew, ever gave a better one.
