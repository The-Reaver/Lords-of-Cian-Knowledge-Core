# The Guild That Wanted to Own the Sound

*Locked canon, Batch 248, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1082`). Iron Bastard Alias Chronicle LXIII, wave 21, closing the wave. An economic/institutional entry: a merchant consortium offers to buy exclusive licensing rights to the resonance doctrine. Not a territory Chronicle.*

---

The offer came through proper channels, which was itself unusual — a sealed letter, a named factor, a
meeting requested rather than an ambush laid. The Ferrowright Consortium controlled inspection contracts
across half the Trust's shipping lanes, and its delegation sat across from Kanja in a warehouse office
with a proposal already drafted: exclusive rights to teach, certify, and license the diagnostic-listening
method, a share of every future inspection fee paid to structures cleared by it, and a standing retainer
larger than anything the rebellion's own treasury held.

Garren Hask read the contract twice before Kanja did, the way he read every number that crossed the
crew's ledger, and set it down without comment on the figures. "It's a fair offer," he said, which from
Hask carried no praise in it, only accuracy. "Fair money for something that isn't yours to sell."

"It's not mine to sell," Kanja agreed. "It was never mine to keep, either. I didn't invent the doctrine
so much as I finally listened to what iron had been saying the whole time. A man doesn't get to own a
thing like that any more than he owns the weather."

The factor pressed once, framing exclusivity as protection — a way to keep the method from falling into
careless or dangerous hands, a licensing board to certify who could be trusted with it. Kanja heard the
argument out fully before he answered, because it wasn't a bad argument on its face; the doctrine had
already produced one fatal misdiagnosis and an apprentice who'd nearly turned it on a target out of
resentment. But a licensing board answerable to a consortium's shareholders wasn't the same thing as the
honesty the doctrine had been built on — the same honesty that had gotten a Trust scholar's report
shelved once already for being inconvenient rather than wrong.

"I already have a board," Kanja said. "It's smaller than yours. It's a man who trained the first student,
and a student who's since trained a third generation of his own, and every one of them answerable to
whether the read was true, not to who profits from it." He slid the contract back across the table,
unsigned. "Teach anyone who'll take the time and tell the truth with it. Charge nothing for the teaching.
That's the only license I'll put my name to."

The consortium withdrew the offer and, within the season, quietly began hiring engineers trained by the
doctrine's own free lineage anyway — at market wages, same as any other skilled hire, with no exclusivity
clause attached. Hask noted the irony in the ledger without editorializing on it, the way he noted
everything: paid the going rate in the end, same as it would have cost him to lease it.

"You could have taken the money and still taught it free on the side," Hask said afterward, not quite a
question.

"I could have," Kanja said. "But the day the doctrine has a price on it, somebody starts asking what it's
worth to keep quiet instead. I've buried people over smaller silences than that."
