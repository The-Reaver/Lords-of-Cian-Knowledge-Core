# Lauris Chronicle XLV: Testimony for No One Left to Hear It

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1662`). Forty-fifth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, the seventh entry of Strand L. Advances the standing testimony debt to the
Iron-Speakers of Kares Prime, locked at her departure (`MCD-174`, "asking only that she preserve a
record of what she found") and named among her active operational debts at `MCD-211` ("its ongoing
existence is... the debt's discharge already in progress rather than a future obligation"). Deepens
rather than resolves: confronts, for the first time, the tension between an ongoing debt of testimony
and a dwindling audience to receive it, since her cohort (Velith, `MCD-1553`, among the 47
parthenogenically-conceived children who will produce no descendants of their own, `MCD-1559`) has no
future generation to inherit it. No new named characters; Velith and Selene already locked. Abad's
blanket authorization: "lock it, continue uninterrupted, test and push to main."*

-----

*Archive fragment, present-day: "I recorded something worth sending home tonight. I do not know who
home is anymore. I am recording that uncertainty as well, because I think it belongs in the account,
not beside it."*

I have transcribed, across the years of keeping this record, a great many entries Lauris makes without
apparent difficulty — combat accounts, operational summaries, the terse arithmetic she applies to
nearly everything she witnesses. This is not one of those. I watched her sit with a half-finished page
for the better part of an hour before setting down what follows, and I want to be honest that I do not
think the difficulty was the writing itself.

The occasion was small, by any external measure. She had spent the day cross-referencing a resonance
signature with Sephtis — not the Iron-Spire question that occupied them once before, but a newer
finding, something genuinely worth recording in the archive the Iron-Speakers asked her, at her
departure, to keep as her civilization's sole continuing testimony to the world beyond Kares Prime. She
has honored that request faithfully for six thousand years. I have never once heard her question the
obligation itself. What she questioned this evening, for the first time in my presence, was not whether
to keep the record, but who, precisely, the record was still for.

*

The arithmetic is simple enough to state and, I have come to understand, considerably harder to sit
with than its simplicity suggests. The Iron-Speakers who asked for this testimony are centuries dead.
The civilization that commissioned it exists now only in the twelve surviving Vasks and the population
within them, declining still, by the same slow attrition that produced her own synthesis in the first
place. And her own cohort — the forty-seven children raised alongside her at Threnarr specifically to
obscure her true nature, of whom Velith was her closest friend — will, by the collapse of the
Sister-Hold's own parthenogenic initiation requirement, live out their tens of thousands of remaining
years without producing children of their own. They are, in the civilization's own quiet understanding,
though never told so directly, its functional final generation.

Which means the testimony she has kept faithfully across six millennia — this evening's finding
included — is being addressed, with each new entry, to an audience that does not merely age or dwindle
the way any living readership eventually does. It is an audience that will, within some finite and
already largely determined span, simply cease to exist at all. Not through any new catastrophe. Through
the ordinary, already-locked-in arithmetic of a civilization that made its last children a very long
time ago.

"I have kept this archive as a promise," she told me, when I asked, carefully, what had made this
particular evening different from the countless others she has spent adding to it without apparent
difficulty. "I did not examine, until tonight, whether a promise made to people who will not be there
to receive it is still the same promise. I do not think it stops being one. I think I have simply never
before had to say so plainly, to myself, what keeping it now actually means."

*

I asked whether she intended to stop. She did not answer quickly — which, as I have noted before in
this series, I have learned to read as the honest answer taking longer to arrive at than a quick one
would have. "No," she said, eventually. "I intend to keep it exactly as I have kept it. I think the
testimony matters regardless of whether Velith's grandchildren ever read it, because there will not be
Velith's grandchildren to read it, and the record should exist anyway — for whoever on Cian eventually
wants to know what Kares Prime was, if no one on Kares Prime remains to want it known themselves." She
paused. "I am aware that this reasoning would have satisfied me completely a hundred years ago and only
mostly satisfies me tonight. I record the difference rather than pretend it away."

She finished the entry before she slept. I do not know what precisely it said — she keeps the substance
of these pages closer than most of her operational archive, and I have not asked to see it, judging
that some part of this particular ledger is not mine to transcribe whole. What I record instead is only
the shape of the evening: a debt she has never once considered abandoning, examined honestly, for the
first time in my presence, against the quiet and now unavoidable fact that its intended recipients are
running out before the debt itself ever could.

Her own closing note, the only line of the evening's entry she shared with me directly: *I do not know
who home is anymore. I am still sending the letter.*
