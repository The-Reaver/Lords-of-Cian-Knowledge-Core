# The Fourth Reading, Unwitnessed

*Locked canon, Batch 263, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1344`). Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle LXXI, wave 24. The fourth-generation apprentice makes his first supervised reading, and the entry details the specific sensory technique Sephtis's craft actually teaches, on the page in full for the first time.*

---

Nobody had ever written down, in one place, exactly what a reading was made of. The ledger recorded the calls -- times, pressures, outcomes, honest misses circled in the caller's own hand -- but not the method itself, the actual sequence of things a reader's body learned to notice before the mind caught up and gave them a number.

The student decided that gap had gone on long enough, and she used the boy's first supervised reading to close it.

"Barometric feel first," she told him, guiding his hand to the instrument the way Sephtis had once guided hers, "but not the number on the dial. The change in the number, against what your own joints already told you an hour ago, before you looked. Your body reads pressure before the instrument confirms it. Most people never learn to trust that half. Sephtis did. I do. You will."

Second was the water's color at the horizon line, a shift too subtle to name in ordinary language and too consistent to be coincidence, that every reader in three generations had learned to see only by being shown it, never by being told it existed. Third was the specific behavior of gulls -- not their presence or absence, which any sailor already watched for, but the particular tightness of their circling, a detail the northern pilot's own tradition had sharpened for the school's use only a season earlier.

"And the fourth thing," the student said, "is the hardest one, and it's not a sense at all. It's knowing when you don't have enough of the first three to call anything, and saying so, out loud, in front of people who want an answer. That's not a skill you can practice on calm days. You'll only really learn it the first time you're wrong."

The boy's reading that day was small stakes deliberately -- a coastal fishing run, not a fleet movement, nothing that would cost lives if he missed it entirely. He called it correctly, a light squall arriving within the hour he'd named, and said so with none of the confidence the student remembered feeling herself at that age, more relief than certainty.

"You did it right," she told him. "Not because you were right. Because you were honestly unsure right up until you weren't, and you said the unsure part out loud too."

Kanja, watching from the harbor wall, understood he was witnessing something the school's own written record had never quite managed before -- not a call, but the anatomy of one, laid out plainly enough that a fourth generation could learn it as a method rather than inherit it as a mystery.
