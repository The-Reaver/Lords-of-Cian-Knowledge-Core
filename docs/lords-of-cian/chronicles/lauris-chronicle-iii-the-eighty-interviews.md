# Lauris Chronicle III: The Eighty Interviews

*Locked canon, Batch 293, 2026-09-17 (`MCD-1563`). Third entry in Lauris Letitia's own Chronicle
series, the first of the "Strand D" (Sealbound Directorate years) thread. Gives full-scene
treatment to Operation 19, the Long Pursuit (already summarized at `MCD-1540`), dramatizing the
23-month, 80-interview investigative method and the institutional-blindness realization the rule
only compresses. Kaerith Vossen already locked, zero new proper nouns. Full text below. Abad's

*Archive fragment, Established Hunter designation LL-001-S-019: "Twenty-three months. Eighty
conversations. No blade drawn until the eighty-first. I did not track a man. I built the man who
would have to exist for his choices to make sense, and waited for the real one to arrive at the
same place."*

Operation 19 is the one Lauris least enjoys discussing, and the one I have pressed her hardest to
let me record properly, because I believe — and she has, with some reluctance, come to agree — that
it is the true hinge of her Directorate career, more than any single battle.

Kaerith Vossen had been a senior contractor of the Directorate's own external pool before his
defection, which meant he understood exactly what a Directorate pursuit looked like and had spent
three years making certain his own did not look like anything at all. Five acquisition teams had
failed to find him. The contract came to Lauris with a note attached, unusual in her experience: the
Directorate's assessment that conventional tracking had been exhausted, and that her deployment
was, in effect, a request to invent a new method.

She did not descend on Vossen's last known associates with threats. She spent twenty-three months
conducting interviews — current contractors, former ones, officers who had signed his old
requisitions, a woman who had once shared a safehouse with him for four days and remembered only
that he preferred his tea unsweetened. Eighty conversations in total, each one logged,
cross-referenced, weighed. She was not building a map of where Vossen was. She was building a model
of the man who would make the decisions Vossen had been making — patient, risk-averse, contemptuous
of anyone who mistook caution for fear — and then asking, of that model, where he would be standing
in six days.

The interviews were tactical in purpose. I want to be honest that they did not stay tactical in
effect. Eighty people, across twenty-three months, answered her questions about an institution they
all believed they understood. What she heard, again and again, in language too consistent to be
coincidence, was that the Directorate's containment doctrine — its lock sequences, its maintenance
protocols, the particular grammar of its oldest facilities — was simply "the old ways." Nobody she
interviewed could say who had designed the old ways. Nobody found the question worth asking. One
retired archivist, three decades into his own Directorate service, told her plainly that he had
never once wondered why the earliest vaults used a keying architecture no living engineer at the
Directorate could explain, because it had simply always been there, the way a mountain is simply
there.

She recorded that exchange word for word. I have read the entry more than once. It is the first
place in her archive where I can find her using the word *inherited* about the Directorate's own
methods rather than the engineering tradition's — the first place where the two things she had been
separately noticing since Operation 3 begin, on the page, to become one thing.

Vossen himself was almost an afterthought by the end. She intercepted him at a safehouse six days
ahead of his own arrival, exactly as her model predicted, and the acquisition itself — calm,
procedurally clean, no resistance offered by a man who recognized when a hunt had genuinely
out-thought him — occupies barely a paragraph of her account. What occupies the rest of it is the
eighty conversations that came before, and what they told her about an institution that did not
know what it was standing on.

Her closing line for Operation 19, written I believe some considerable time after the contract
itself closed: *I was hired to find one man. I found, instead, that the people who hired me do not
know who built the house they work in. I do not think this was the assignment. I think it became
the assignment the moment I understood it.*

I have asked her whether Operation 19 is where her Disillusionment truly began. She has told me,
more than once, that she does not trust single origins for anything that took years to become true.
But she has also told me that this is the operation she would choose, if forced to choose one, as
the place a door she had not noticed closing behind her finally clicked shut.
