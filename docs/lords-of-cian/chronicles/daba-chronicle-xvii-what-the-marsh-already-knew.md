# Daba Chronicle XVII: What the Marsh Already Knew

Sorin read ground the way other men read faces, and Daba had learned, across years of watching him
work, that the skill could not be taught by talking about it. It had to be walked. So on the morning a
Trust tax patrol was reported moving toward the causeway road that cut the northern marsh in half,
Daba took Kanja out ahead of it, and brought Sorin along not to fight but to narrate.

They lay on a rise above the causeway an hour before the patrol was due, and Sorin talked the whole
time in a low, unbothered voice, the way a man might talk about weather.

"That road looks solid," he said, nodding at the raised track below. "It isn't, past the second
marker. Peat under gravel, packed thin, built by people who never had to walk it in a wet season.
A cart crosses fine. Six carts crossing together, one behind the other, and the third one starts
sinking before the first one's clear the far side -- because the weight doesn't sit still under peat,
it travels, and by the time you've felt your own wheels sink you've already loaded the ground under
the man behind you."

Kanja was watching the causeway with the particular focus Daba had come to recognize as the boy
actually listening, rather than waiting to speak. "So the road's not weak everywhere. It's weak
*there*, at that stretch, because of what's already crossed it."

"Ground remembers weight," Sorin said. "Most men only look at what's standing on it right now."

Daba let them talk. This was the part of the lesson he could not deliver himself, not the way Sorin
could -- Sorin had grown up on ground like this, had learned to read a marsh's moods before he had
learned to read anything written down, and Daba's own gift ran toward people and formations rather than
soil. He had built 1804 on the principle of borrowing exactly this kind of specific knowledge rather
than pretending to possess all of it himself, and it pleased him, watching Kanja absorb the same
lesson without having to be told it directly: that a commander's job was not to know everything the
ground could do, but to know who did, and to listen when they spoke.

The patrol came an hour later, nine soldiers and two supply carts, moving at the unhurried pace of men
who had never once been made to hurry on this road. Sorin narrated the crossing in the same flat
voice. The lead cart passed the second marker clean. The soldiers spread loosely along the causeway's
length, unbothered, the way soldiers moved through ground they trusted. The second cart began, faintly,
to list.

"There," Sorin said, and nothing more.

They did not intervene. There was no reason to -- this was reconnaissance, not an operation, and the
cart crossed, straining, without incident, the patrol none the wiser that the ground under it had
nearly told a different story. But Daba watched Kanja track the whole crossing with an attention that
had shifted register somewhere in the last hour, from watching soldiers to watching the road itself,
as though the causeway had become, in the space of a single patrol, a participant in the scene rather
than a stage it happened on.

"You could have collapsed that stretch," Kanja said, once the patrol had passed out of earshot. Not a
question. An observation, testing itself against what he had just been shown.

"I could have," Daba agreed. "I didn't need to today. But notice what you just said -- *collapsed*, not
*attacked*. You're already thinking about the ground as something you could use against them instead
of something they happen to be standing on."

Kanja didn't answer right away. He was looking back at the causeway, at the ordinary raised track that
had, for one crossing, nearly swallowed a cart under nine unsuspecting soldiers' own indifferent
weight, and Daba recognized the particular quality of his silence -- not confusion, but the sound of a
mind rearranging something it had assumed was fixed.

"Terrain isn't where a fight happens," Daba said, before the boy could ask. "It's one more thing on
the field with an opinion about who wins. You just have to be patient enough to let it speak first."

They walked back into the marsh before dusk, and Sorin kept talking the whole way, pointing out ground
that would drown a man in boots and ground that wouldn't, water that carried sound across it flat and
clear for half a mile and water that swallowed sound within twenty paces, the whole quiet architecture
of a landscape that had, until that morning, been for Kanja simply a place things happened to occur in.
Daba said little on the walk back. He had learned that the lessons that mattered least needed the most
defending, and this was not one of those.
