# What Pell Ostra Set Down

*Locked canon, Batch 281, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1472`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle XCVII, wave 33, first
entry. Age 275, V4 gear. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

She'd measured the same kind of charge a thousand times: a shared wall, a sleeping room on the other
side of it, a lead crack and a follow-through timed a half-second behind it. Her hands had never once
needed to be told what four hours of listening had already worked out. This time, setting the
follow-through charge against a warehouse wall with an infirmary sharing its far side, her fingers
hesitated over the timing pin for a fraction of a second longer than they should have — a fraction she
caught herself, corrected, and set exactly right before it mattered to anyone but her.

She said nothing about it until the raid was finished and the ninety-one freed were clear. Then she
told Kanja directly, the way she told her materials things rather than asked them: her hands had
slowed, not much, not yet dangerously, but enough that she'd felt the gap between what she meant to do
and what her fingers did about it for the first time in two hundred years of setting charges for a
living. She wasn't asking permission to keep going. She was telling him she'd decided not to.

He didn't argue with her the way he might have with someone younger, someone whose judgment about
their own hands he trusted less than she'd earned. What he asked instead was what she wanted the work
to look like now, and she already had an answer ready — she wanted to teach it, not carry it, the
half-second timing and the habit of addressing a wall like it had something to say back, to whoever
came after her the way no one had ever formally come after Efa Gol's decoy line until there was a
successor already proven in the field. She'd already chosen someone: quiet, careful, patient with
materials in the specific way charges required and people rarely did.

Efa Gol, hearing it secondhand that same week, told her plainly that stepping back on her own timing
rather than someone else's failure was the harder thing to do and the better one — she'd know, having
done it herself a hundred and twenty-five years before. Pell Ostra didn't disappear from the crew's
work; Garren Hask's ledger would go on crediting her name to a reading lens here, a measurement caught
early there, for decades yet. But the charges themselves, from that season on, were someone else's
hands to set — and hers to watch, and correct, and finally trust.
