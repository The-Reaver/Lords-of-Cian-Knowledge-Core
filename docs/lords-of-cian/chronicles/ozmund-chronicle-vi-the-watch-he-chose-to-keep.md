# The Watch He Chose to Keep

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1735`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle VI, closing the Draconis strand opened in Chronicle I. Set some time after Chronicle
V, still strictly pre-Book-1 — Aethelgard Verehimu alive, the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) still
in the future. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. Closes the strand on

The last of these stories, the General told me, wasn't really a story at all. Nothing happened in it,
by his own telling — no ambush, no poison, no beam come loose from a rotten joist. He told it to me
anyway, more than once, which told me more about what it meant to him than the telling itself ever
did.

It was late in that same stretch of years, a autumn storm that came up off the coast faster than the
household's own watchers had read it, the kind that took down two chimney-stacks and half the orchard
before it was through and left the roads impassable for the better part of a week. Aethelgard took a
fever in the middle of it — nothing the household physicians called dangerous, in the end, but no one
knew that yet on the second night, when his breathing had gone ragged enough that his own steward sat
up with a lamp outside his door and the whole house moved through its halls the way houses move when
they're afraid of what the next hour might bring.

Draconis's watch that night ended, by the roster, at the second bell. The General remembered the
number precisely, telling it to me — he'd checked it himself afterward, curious, and found nothing
irregular in it at all, which was exactly the point. There was no order standing that required more
than the ordinary rotation even with a storm outside and a lord unwell within; the House had guards
enough for that, and Draconis's own relief came promptly, competent, entirely sufficient to the duty
as written. Draconis didn't leave. He told the man relieving him to take the outer door instead, and
stayed where he'd been standing all along, outside the General's own chamber, through a night that by
any measure asked nothing further of him at all.

The General lay awake and listened to him do it. He told me it was, of everything Draconis had ever
done for him, the hardest to sit still and simply receive, because every other test the man had passed
had at least had the decency to look like danger — a knife, a wrong seal, a beam he could have stepped
under and hadn't. This had no shape at all. No one on that floor needed guarding that badly, least of
all him; the storm could have taken the roof entirely and left him standing in the open air unmarked,
and Aethelgard's fever, frightening as it looked from outside a sickroom door, was never once a threat
that a blade at the threshold could have answered. Draconis had to know some part of that, even
without knowing why. He stayed anyway.

Near dawn the General rose, unable to keep lying there listening to the storm and the pacing both, and
opened his door to find Draconis exactly where the sound had placed him — soaked through from an earlier
circuit of the grounds, jaw set the particular way it set when he had decided a thing and meant to see
it through regardless of what anyone told him about it. The General asked him, plainly, why he hadn't
gone to his own bed hours ago the way the roster allowed. Draconis told him, just as plainly, that a
house with its lord fevered and its roof half torn off wasn't a house he could sleep well away from,
roster or no roster, and that he'd rather stand wet through until sunrise than lie warm somewhere
wondering if he ought to be standing here instead.

The General told me he had nothing to say back to that. Not because he lacked the words, but because
for once there was nothing underneath the moment that needed managing — no truth to withhold, no
instinct to suppress, no careful stillness required to let a lesser act stand in for a greater one he
could have performed instead. Draconis simply wanted to be there, and was, and the General let himself,
just that once, be nothing more complicated than a young man grateful for it. He told me that was the
version of Draconis he thought of first, whenever the man's name came up in later years — not the
soldier who'd stopped three knives on a road, or read a wrong seal across a crowded hall, but the one
soaked through outside a door at dawn for no better reason than that he'd chosen to be.

Aethelgard's fever broke before midday. The roof was mended within the month. Draconis never spoke of
that night again, as far as the General ever knew, and never once asked to be thanked for it. The
General said he didn't need to. Some kinds of loyalty, he told me, are worth more precisely because
they cost the man giving them nothing to explain — no danger to name, no reason required beyond the
plain fact of having decided to stay.
