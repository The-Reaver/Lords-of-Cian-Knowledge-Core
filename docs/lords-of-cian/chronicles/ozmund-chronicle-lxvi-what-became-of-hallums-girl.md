He told me this one as a kind of afterword to the story about Petrin Hallum, the way he sometimes did
with the ones that mattered most to him — not as a separate telling, but folded into the end of the
first, a quiet addition years after I'd already sworn never to repeat the name. I've kept that promise
here too, as far as it can still apply to a daughter I never met and have no way of tracing.

Petrin Hallum died the way most men of that generation eventually did, the General said — not
dramatically, just old, his ruined arm long since stopped aching in the way it once had because there
was so little strength left in it to ache. He'd outlived the House's public memory of what he'd done for
it by decades, exactly the way his own pride had once insisted on. The quiet payments Aethelgard had
kept sending for years, through the same factor neither man ever met face to face, had by then become
less a rescue than simply a habit both of them had settled into without ceremony.

What the General hadn't known, before this — hadn't been told even in the first telling, years earlier
— was that Hallum had a daughter, grown by the time her father died, a woman named Fenna who'd stayed
close to the river village her whole life doing the kind of unglamorous work that keeps a household fed
without ever making anyone rich. Aethelgard learned of Hallum's death the ordinary way, through the same
factor who'd carried the payments all those years, and the General said his father's first response
wasn't grief exactly, though there was some of that in it too. It was a question. What happens to the
arrangement now.

He could have let it lapse. The General was plain about that — nobody, least of all Fenna Hallum
herself, who apparently knew nothing of where four small sums a year had ever come from, would have
noticed or questioned the money simply stopping along with the man it had once been meant to help. That
was, in fact, the General said, closer to what the House's own custom would have expected: a debt owed
to a guardsman ends, reasonably enough, with the guardsman.

His father didn't see it that way. He redirected the arrangement to Fenna directly, through the same
factor, the same quiet channel, no explanation attached and none ever given. And for the first time, the
General said, his father brought him along on the errand — not to reveal anything to her, not even to be
seen by her at all, just to stand at a distance on the far side of the village square while his father
confirmed with the factor that the first payment under the new arrangement had gone through cleanly, and
then to ride home again without either of them ever having spoken a single word to the woman it was
for.

The General asked him, on the ride back, why bring him this time when he'd asked for nothing but
silence the first time the story was ever told. His father was quiet a while, the way he tended to be
before the answers that cost him something, and then he said that he wanted his son to see, once, what
it actually looked like for a debt like this one to keep being paid past the point where anyone living
remembered why it had started — not the story of it, which the boy already had, but the plain,
unremarkable fact of it continuing, year after year, to a woman who would likely never know her father's
courage had bought her anything at all.

"It doesn't end when the man it's owed to does," Aethelgard told him, in words the General repeated
carefully, decades later, the way he repeated very little else. "I used to think it would, when I
started this. I know better now. A debt like this one doesn't have an ending built into it. It only
ever has whoever's still willing to keep paying it."

The General told me he thought of that afternoon often, much later, once he had begun quietly paying
debts of his own that the people receiving them never knew the shape of — that he'd learned, standing at
the edge of a village square watching his father settle an account with a stranger who'd never know his
name, that the actual inheritance his father meant to leave him wasn't the House, or the Scar, or even
the coin. It was this. A willingness to keep something going long after anyone was left to thank you for
it.
