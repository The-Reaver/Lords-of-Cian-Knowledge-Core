# Daba Chronicle III: The Word They Used Instead

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1573`). Entry 3 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes the
Sovereign Trust's official "fire of undetermined origin" record (`MCD-1566`) reaching the Rookery's
survivors days later, and their collective refusal of that framing. Introduces Sana and Torvin, two
new minor named characters (fellow survivor-caregivers), collision-checked clean. Abad's approval:

The crier came on the fourth day, which Daba would remember afterward as its own kind of insult — that it had taken four days for the Trust to decide what the Rookery was allowed to have been. He read from a board nailed up at the crossing where the tannery road met the old rent office, a young man in Trust grey who did not look at the crowd gathering around him and did not slow his pace through the words, the way a man reads something he has been told to read rather than something he believes.

*By order of the Sovereign Trust Domain administration: the fire at the settlement known as the Rookery has been investigated and its origin determined to be undetermined. Structural failure in aging construction, compounded by inadequate firebreaks, remains the leading working theory. No further inquiry is presently warranted.*

Sana laughed. It was the wrong sound for the moment and she knew it and made it anyway, sharp and short, the laugh of someone who has run out of other places to put the feeling. She was younger than Daba, had lost a brother on the fourth floor, and had spent the last three days organizing what food the tannery had into rations with a precision that Daba had already noticed and admired without yet having a word for it.

"Undetermined," she repeated, tasting it. "Like fire just — happens. Like it struck itself with flint for the sport of it and walked in through a door somebody left standing open on purpose. They collapsed the stair, Daba. I watched them do it. I watched the beam come down from a rope that didn't attach itself."

"I know," Daba said.

"Then why does it feel like agreeing with a lie just by listening to it read out loud?"

He didn't have an answer for that, not one that wouldn't have sounded like a comfort he didn't feel entitled to give her. It was Torvin who answered instead — an old caretaker who'd run the Rookery's water cistern for longer than Daba had been alive, one of the handful of grown men who'd made it out, his left arm bound tight against his ribs where a falling lath had caught him on the way down the laundry chute. He'd heard the crier through the crowd from where he sat against the tannery wall, and he spoke without opening his eyes, like a man too tired to spend effort on where he was looking.

"Because they don't need you to believe it," Torvin said. "They need the *paper* to say it. A hundred years from now somebody pulls the Trust ledger and reads undetermined origin, structural failure, and that's the only version that costs them anything to keep. Your version costs them nothing to ignore. That's the whole trick of it. It's not a lie built to convince the living. It's a lie built to outlast us."

Daba turned that over for a long moment, watching the crier fold his board under his arm and start back down the crossing toward whatever the next posting was, already moving on to whichever settlement's grief needed managing next. Something in the shape of Torvin's sentence stuck to him differently than the rest of the outrage moving through the crowd around him — not the anger, which he shared and which would keep, but the practical, almost administrative coldness of it. *A lie built to outlast us.* Which meant the answer to it, if there was one, would have to be something built to outlast the lie in turn. Not a shout in a crossing. Something with a longer memory than a Trust ledger and no paper for anyone to burn.

He didn't say any of that aloud. It wasn't a thought yet, only the first cold edge of one, and he was not a man — not yet, not in any way he recognized in himself — who spoke a thing before he knew what shape it was going to take. He only nodded once, to Torvin, and went back to find Mika and the others, and let the crossing empty out behind him without him in it.
