# The Book Fenmoor Never Finished

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1829`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle C, Wider Verehimu Household/Guard strand, wave 2. Set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony
(`MCD-025`), in Ozmund's adolescence — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund still House heir. Narrated by Red
Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, a second visit to Master Alric Fenmoor in his
retirement, this time asking after the tutor rather than the student. A generational/legacy register:
Fenmoor's own unfinished scholarly ambition, and how it shaped the particular rigor he demanded of a
lord's son who had never once had to earn anything the hard way before him. No new named characters.*

---

I went back to Master Alric Fenmoor a second time, some months after the first, because a detail from
our earlier conversation had stayed with me — the three unfinished translations buried under his own
writing desk, which he had mentioned only in passing and I had been too eager, that first visit, to
follow up on. He laughed when I told him why I'd come back. He said no one in thirty years of retirement
had ever once asked him about his own work, only about whichever student of his had gone on to become
someone worth asking about, and that he found the question almost indecently flattering.

The largest of the three translations, he told me, was a full accounting of the Sovereign Trust's
earliest Scrip correspondence — the actual letters, invoices, and ledger disputes from the system's
founding years, rather than the tidied official history every later scholar had simply copied from one
another without checking. He had begun it as a young man, certain it would be the work that made his
name, and had spent the better part of a decade gathering source material before a senior scholar whose
approval he'd needed for publication dismissed the entire project in a single afternoon — not, Fenmoor
was careful to tell me, because the work was wrong, but because it was inconvenient to several
reputations built on the tidied version. He said he'd been young enough then to mistake that dismissal
for proof he'd failed, rather than proof he'd been right about something someone powerful needed left
alone.

He told me it had taken him the better part of his own middle years to stop thinking of the project as
a failure and start thinking of it as simply unfinished — a distinction, he said, he hadn't been capable
of making at the time, and one he only arrived at by watching, of all people, his own student wrestle
with the very same confusion a generation later.

He returned, without my needing to ask, to the story he'd told me before — the boy failed on the spot
over a Scrip working that was correct in its conclusion and wrong in its method, made to redo the whole
of it twice in front of no one. He said what he hadn't told me the first time was how closely he'd
recognized himself in the boy's reaction, those first months, the quiet controlled frustration of
someone convinced that a wrong method dressed as a right answer should still count for something. He
said he'd been unusually hard on that particular failure precisely because he remembered, with total
clarity, how many years it had cost him to learn the same lesson himself, and he'd decided, watching
Ozmund struggle with it at ten years old, that he wasn't going to let the boy take thirty years to
learn what Fenmoor had only learned by having his life's real work quietly buried.

He said the strangest part, looking back, was that he'd never once told the boy any of this directly —
never explained that the standard he was holding him to had cost the tutor something real himself. He
considered that a deliberate choice rather than an oversight. He said a student needed to learn that a
wrong method dressed as a right answer was worth correcting on its own terms, not because his tutor had
a wound attached to the lesson. The wound was his to carry, not the boy's to be burdened with.

I asked him, near the end, whether he had ever finished the Scrip translation, in all the years since.
He told me no — that he had, at his age, made a kind of peace with unfinished work being simply a
different category from failed work, and that he no longer felt the need to close every account he'd
ever opened. He said he supposed that was its own kind of lesson, arrived at very late, and that he
occasionally wondered whether the boy — grown now into whatever the General had become — had managed to
learn it a good deal sooner than his tutor had.
