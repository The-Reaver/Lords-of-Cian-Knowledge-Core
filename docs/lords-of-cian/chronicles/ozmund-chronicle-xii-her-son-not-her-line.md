# Her Son, Not Her Line

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1741`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XII — opens "the Val Mirel strand," five entries giving Val Mirel Kareth
(`MCD-101`/`CC-004`/`CC-016`) real, dramatized presence in Ozmund's life for the first time. Set
strictly pre-Book-1, years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is
alive and present in this entry. Ozmund is roughly nine. Dramatizes Val Mirel's Kareth War-Order
discipline set directly against House Verehimu's political register, and gives Ozmund his first
on-page moment of holding both inheritances at once. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per
`VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years later, matching Chronicle
I's convention. No new named characters.*

---

I never met Val Mirel Kareth. I want that said plainly, before anything else, because it matters to
how I tell this: everything I know of her comes from her son, told to me the way a man tells you
about weather he lived through once and never entirely trusted the sky again after. He did not talk
about his mother easily. When he did, it was never in the middle of some larger story. It came
sideways, unprompted, usually years apart, as though each telling used up a store of something he
only replenished slowly.

She did not announce herself. That was the first thing he ever told me about her, and it stayed the
truest. A woman who had commanded a Wing of the Kareth War-Order across centuries the House
Verehimu's oldest ledgers didn't reach back far enough to count did not send word ahead of a visit to
her own son. She simply appeared — in his case, at the foot of his bed, before the sky outside his
window had decided whether it meant to be dawn, with a hand on his shoulder light enough that he
never woke frightened, only awake, the way he said he was never quite sure how to be around anyone
else his whole life after.

He was nine. He had not seen her in — he was honestly uncertain, telling it to me decades on, whether
it had been one year or three, which tells you something about what those absences did to a boy's
sense of time. She took him to the training yard before the house had begun to move, before his
tutors would have had him at his letters and his bearing and the twelve forms of address a Verehimu
heir was expected to know before breakfast, and she taught him something she called, in a tongue he
only half understood then and never fully learned, a word that came into his own speech years later
simply as the Stone Count.

It was not a fighting form, not at first. She had him stand. That was the whole of it — stand, spine
straight, weight even, and count his own breath in a rhythm she set with two fingers tapped once
against his collarbone, slower each time he thought he'd found the pace. She corrected nothing about
his stance. She corrected only his hurry — the small, constant, court-bred urge to be seen doing
something, to fill a silence, to perform stillness rather than hold it. "A wall does not announce that
it is standing," she told him, the only full sentence of that first morning he ever repeated to me
word for word. "It is only ever asked to still be there when the weight arrives."

He told me his father found them an hour later, from the colonnade above the yard, and did not come
down. Aethelgard Verehimu believed — he'd told his son as much, more than once, in the very different
language of his own house — that stillness read as weakness at court, that a lord who let a silence
sit too long was a lord who'd be filled in by someone with sharper opinions. He watched his wife teach
their son the opposite lesson without a word of objection, and when Ozmund asked him about it that
evening, half-expecting correction, his father only said that a man who could hold both kinds of
stillness — the kind that commanded a room and the kind that outlasted one — would never need to
choose between his mother's world and his own. It was, the General told me, the only time in his
boyhood either parent spoke of the other's teaching as anything but a separate country he was
expected to carry a passport for.

He kept at the Stone Count for three days, which was all the time she had. On the fourth morning she
was gone again, no farewell beyond a hand on the same shoulder, and it would be — he never told me
exactly how long, only that it was long enough that he'd nearly convinced himself he'd dreamed the
whole of it — before she came back to test whether he'd kept the discipline in her absence. He had.
He told me that much with something close to pride, decades later, a boy's private triumph still
warm enough at the edges to hear in an old man's voice.

I only understood what she'd actually given him much later, watching him hold a command post through
three days of General Baryon's own doctrine breaking against it without once raising his voice above
what the moment required. He never called it the Stone Count in front of the Legion. He didn't need
to. I'd heard the phrase exactly once, years before I ever saw what it looked like grown into a man
who could stand at the center of a battle the way a wall stands at the center of weather, and I knew
it the instant I saw it, because he had already told me, without meaning to, exactly where he'd
learned to be still.
