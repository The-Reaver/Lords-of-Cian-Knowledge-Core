# The Night His Judgment Slipped

*Locked canon, Batch 241, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1059`). Bane Alias Chronicle LXI, wave 21. The first
entry to test Bane's own biological/mental limits under sustained exhaustion rather than a tactical,
intelligence, or gear failure. Not a territory Chronicle.*

---

Eleven days without a full night's sleep was nothing new for the column — Bane had run longer
stretches before, on less — but eleven days of it while also holding three separate operations open
at once was new, and by the ninth night Corren Halst had started watching him the way he watched a
rope under a load it wasn't rated for.

The plan Bane laid out that night was, on its face, sound: a night crossing of the Amitane flats to
relieve a besieged relay post before its water ran out, cutting two hours off the safe route by
routing the column directly through a grain village still technically inside the contested line. He'd
run the numbers twice. He was certain of them. What he hadn't done, and didn't notice he hadn't done,
was ask whether the village's own harvest crews would still be out in the fields past dark bringing in
the last of a threatened crop — something he would have asked on any other night, reflexively, before
the numbers ever got that far.

"You didn't check the fields," Danne Sok said, not as an accusation, reading the plan over his
shoulder. "You always check the fields."

Bane looked at the map a second time and found nothing wrong with it. That was the part that
frightened him once Halst said it plainly a minute later — not that the route was wrong, but that he
couldn't currently tell that it might be, and hadn't been able to for at least two of the eleven days
he'd been awake for. Onyx's own senses, sharp enough to read stress in a support beam from across a
bridge, told him nothing about the blind spot sitting inside his own skull.

They sent a runner ahead instead, lost the two hours, and found the harvest crews exactly where Danne
Sok's habit of checking had assumed they'd be — thirty of them, still working a field the safe route
would have carried the whole column straight through in the dark, weapons drawn, at a dead run.

Bane slept nine hours that night for the first time in eleven days, on Halst's flat order rather than
his own judgment, and woke to find the column had run itself competently without him.

"I've told every one of you, more times than I can count, that tired judgment is how good people make
the mistake that gets someone killed," he said afterward, to Halst, Danne Sok, and Maret Vos together.
"I never once applied it to myself."

"You're not exempt from your own rule," Halst said. "You just never told anyone it applied to you
too."

It did now, by his own standing order: no one, including Bane, ran an operational plan past the fourth
sleepless night without a second set of eyes checking it who wasn't as tired as he was.
