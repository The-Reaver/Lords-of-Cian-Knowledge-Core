# The Books That Were Built to Pass

*Locked canon, Batch 255, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1126`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LXIX, wave 23,
closing the wave. Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. The tally-verification method's first real defeat
by fraud sophisticated enough to survive cross-witnessing.*

---

The tannery's books passed every check Garren Hask knew how to run. Two independent workers'
accounts matched the owner's ledger to the coin. The figures were internally consistent across
eleven months of entries, written in three different hands on three different kinds of paper stock,
exactly the sort of small inconsistency a real, honestly-kept ledger accumulates over time and a
forged one almost never bothers to fake. Hask checked it twice more and found nothing wrong with it,
because there was nothing wrong with it that his method had ever been built to find.

"It's too clean," Efa Gol said, the first time she read through it, before Hask had finished his
second pass. She wasn't looking at the numbers. She'd spent her working life reading rooms and
routes, not ledgers, and what she was reading in the tannery owner's manner — a fraction too
relieved, a fraction too quick to offer the books up before anyone had even asked — didn't match a
man with nothing to hide.

"The figures are sound," Hask said.

"I'm not telling you the figures are wrong. I'm telling you the man's lying, and he's good enough at
it that the figures don't show it." Gol had commanded enough decoy operations by then to recognize
the same instinct running the other direction — a story built specifically to survive the check it
expected, rather than a story that simply happened to be true. "Somebody taught him what cross-
witnessing looks for. That's not luck. That's preparation."

It took three more days, and no new figures at all, to find what her instinct had already told her
was there: a second, older set of workers, quietly let go months before Hask's method reached that
district, replaced by two new hires trained specifically to corroborate a ledger built for their
benefit rather than to independently verify it. The workers Hask had cross-witnessed against weren't
lying about the numbers. The numbers themselves had simply never been real in the first place —
constructed backward from a target the owner wanted the audit to find, rather than forward from what
had actually happened.

"The method assumes the people checking each other don't already agree on the story," Kanja said,
once it was laid out plainly. "That's the whole method. Two honest accounts that can't have been
coordinated."

"So somebody coordinates them first," Hask said, "and my whole check becomes useless."

They didn't discard the method. They patched it — a new rule added to every verification from that
week forward, requiring at least one witness with no prior relationship to the owner being checked,
someone the owner couldn't have reached before the audit began. It cost more time. It found the
tannery's real, unpaid workers within a week of the patch, three districts over, exactly where the
"too clean" instinct had suggested they'd gone.
