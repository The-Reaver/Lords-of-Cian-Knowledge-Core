Ilyse had kept the dye-shop for nine years before anyone in the Trust's regional accounting office
noticed that its ledgers didn't balance the way an honest dye-shop's ledgers should.

Nine years is a long time to be someone. Daba understood that better than most of the people under
him, because he had built the practice himself, in the earliest days, out of nothing but the
conviction that a cover held too briefly was worse than no cover at all -- that a person who arrived,
did the work, and vanished within a season taught a watching Trust exactly the shape of what to look
for next time. So he had asked, of the people who could stand it, something harder: stay. Marry the
cover to a real life, if a real life presented itself. Let the seams close over until even you have to
think, some mornings, about which version of yourself woke up first.

Ilyse had married the cover to a real life without quite meaning to. She had a business partner named
Mattis Kastel who thought she was exactly what her ledgers said she was, a dye-merchant's widow rebuilding
a family trade, and after nine years she had stopped being entirely certain he was wrong. She liked
him. She liked the shop, the particular smell of madder root and iron mordant that never quite left
her hands, the two apprentices she'd trained who knew nothing and never would. None of it was supposed
to matter to the work. All of it, eventually, did.

The accounting clerk who caught the discrepancy was not clever, exactly -- he was thorough, which is a
different and in some ways more dangerous thing. A shipment of dyer's madder that should have taken
eleven days from the coast had taken four. He filed a note. The note sat for three weeks before a
junior investigator, working down a list of nothing in particular, pulled the shop's full five-year
record and found six more shipments that moved faster than any honest cart should move.

Daba heard about the note eleven days after it was filed, from a source inside the regional office who
had no idea what she was actually protecting when she flagged it for him. Eleven days was not much of
a lead. It was, against a thorough clerk instead of a suspicious one, enough.

He did not send anyone to intervene at the shop. He did not need to, and intervening would have been
its own kind of confession. What he did was simpler and colder: he had the fast supply route through
Ilyse's shop shut down that same night, rerouted through a slower path that would never again touch her
ledgers, and he sent word, through the one channel that could reach her without being watched, that she
had perhaps three weeks before the investigator's attention turned from her shipping records to her.

She had three weeks to become someone else again.

She used them to do it properly, which was its own quiet act of discipline -- she did not vanish
overnight, which would have confirmed every suspicion the moment it was noticed. She sold the shop, at
a fair price, to Mattis Kastel, who was glad of the opportunity and sorry to see her go, and told him she
was going north to be with a sister who didn't exist. She said goodbye to her two apprentices. She let
herself be sad about it where he could see, because a woman leaving a business she loved is supposed to
look sad, and looking exactly as sad as the story required was itself part of the work.

Daba met her twelve days later at a waystation two provinces east, and she was, for the first time in
nine years, simply a courier again -- no shop, no ledgers, no smell of madder root, no Mattis Kastel
asking after her in a letter that would never reach anyone because there was no longer an address for
it to reach.

"It worked," he told her. It had. The investigator's file closed within a month for lack of anything
further to chase, and nothing about the closure ever touched another cell.

"I know it worked," she said. She did not sound like a woman celebrating a doctrine vindicated. She
sounded like a woman who had spent nine years becoming a person and then, correctly, on schedule, exactly
as she'd agreed to when she first took the cover, had to put that person down.

Daba did not tell her it would get easier. He had learned, across enough of these, that it generally
did not, and that promising otherwise cost him more trust than it ever bought.
