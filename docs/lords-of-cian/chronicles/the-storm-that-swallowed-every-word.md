# The Storm That Swallowed Every Word

*Locked canon, Batch 221, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-987`). The Crow King Alias Chronicle XLIX, wave 17. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The rain had not stopped in three days, and by the second night it had stopped being weather and
become a wall. Sound didn't travel through it so much as drown in it — a voice pitched to carry
across a hundred yards in ordinary air made it perhaps twenty before the downpour ate the rest, and
the wind that came with it tore whatever survived into pieces no listener, friendly or hostile,
could have reassembled into a pattern.

The apprentice tried the opening bars anyway, on the first night, out of habit more than hope, and
heard her own voice vanish four strides from her own mouth.

"It's not a wind shift," she said, when she found Kanja. "Wind shift, you adjust the timing, you
compensate. This isn't adjustable. There's no version of this pattern that survives this much water
in the air."

"Then it isn't a vocal pattern tonight." He was already working through what was left. Sixty people
needed to move through a stretch of open ground that would have taken minutes on a clear night and
would now take the better part of an hour, and no false signal built on sound would reach far enough
to matter.

What they built instead had never been the primary method before — always a backup, a way to check
a partner's progress without breaking voice, taps against armor plate or a staff against stone.
Tonight it became the entire operation: a relay of six people spaced at the limit of visibility
rather than hearing, each one passing a percussive pattern hand to hand, plate to plate, down the
line rather than voice to open air. No single link carried the message more than a few strides. The
storm that killed every voice couldn't touch a signal that never had to travel through it at all.

It was slower. It was uglier, by the craft's own standards — no interleaved falsehoods, no Braid, no
elegance, just a plain relay of true information moving a chain of six bodies at a time toward the
one place that mattered. It worked because the storm blinded the patrol as thoroughly as it deafened
everyone else; by the time visibility cleared enough for anyone to see the relay line at all, the
sixty were already past the open ground.

"We built this whole craft around what a voice can carry," the apprentice said afterward, soaked
through, hands still faintly stinging from three hours of signaling. "Tonight it worked because we
stopped needing one."

"It worked because we had six people willing to stand in a storm relaying something they couldn't
hear for themselves," Kanja said. "The voice was never the craft. It was just the part that happened
to be easiest to teach first."
