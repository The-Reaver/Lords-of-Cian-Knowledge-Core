He told me about Petrin Hallum only once, and made me swear — the one time in all our years together
he ever asked me to swear to anything — that I would never repeat the name to anyone who might still
be living to be embarrassed by it. I have no way now of knowing whether that's still true. I've kept
the rest of the promise anyway, which is why I've waited this long to set the story down at all, and
why I've changed nothing else about it.

He was young when he found the ledger. Not the House ledger — a second one, smaller, kept in a plain
strongbox in a drawer of his father's study that wasn't locked so much as simply never opened by
anyone who didn't already know to look for it. The General said he found it entirely by accident,
hunting for sealing wax on an evening his father was out, and that what stopped him wasn't the money —
the sums were modest, unremarkable, nothing a House that size would ever miss — but the pattern.
Four times a year, on no date that matched any House obligation he'd ever been taught, a small
disbursement, routed through a factor's name he didn't recognize, to an address in a river village two
days' ride from anywhere the family had land or business. No explanation. No record of what it was
for. Just the same amount, four times a year, going back further than the boy could count.

He asked his father about it directly, which he told me took more nerve than most of what he did later
in his life, because some instinct in him already understood he was asking about something meant to
stay unasked. Aethelgard didn't get angry. The General said that surprised him more than anything else
about the whole evening — he'd braced for anger, or at least for the kind of firm redirection a lord
gives a son who's found something not meant for him, and got neither. His father simply sat down,
which he did before anything he considered worth doing properly, and told him about a night before the
boy was born, when a man named Petrin Hallum had been standing a post no more dangerous than any other
post on any other ordinary evening, and had put himself between Aethelgard and a blade meant for the
lord and not the guardsman, the way the post required, and had lost the full use of his sword arm for
it.

The House had done what a House does in that circumstance. There'd been a public commendation, a
formal retirement with honors, the standard pension a maimed guardsman was owed by custom. Aethelgard
said it had all felt, at the time, like enough — until years later, by accident nearly as small as the
one that had just led his own son to a locked drawer, he'd learned what actually became of a man whose
only trade was a sword arm he no longer had, once the ceremony was over and the House's attention had
moved, as attention always does, on to the next thing. The pension covered less than anyone in that
room the day it was voted had bothered to calculate. Hallum had a daughter by then. He was, by the time
Aethelgard found out, three years behind on debts he'd never once written to the House about, because —
and this was the part his father said still cost him something to repeat — the one time Hallum had
been asked directly whether he needed anything further, he'd said no, out of the same pride that had
put him in front of that blade in the first place.

So Aethelgard had started sending money of his own, not the House's — his own purse, not the ledger any
steward or auditor or future rival could ever hold up as either generosity or guilt — through a factor
neither man ever met in person, four times a year, with no name attached that either of them would
ever have to acknowledge across a table. It had gone on that way for longer than the boy had been
alive to notice.

The General asked him, that night, why not simply fix it properly — restore the man publicly, give him
a title, tell the story at a feast the way it plainly deserved to be told. His father was quiet long
enough that the boy thought he'd overstepped again, and then he said that the House had already told
that story once, loudly, in front of everyone who mattered, and it had fed exactly no one. A hero's
name in a ledger of honors warms the House that grants it far more than it ever warms the man it's
granted to. A sum of money, sent quietly enough that a proud man can spend it without anyone watching
him do it, actually feeds his daughter. "He didn't want to be a story," Aethelgard told him. "He told
me so, the one time I asked. He wanted his arm to stop aching in the cold and his girl to eat well. I
can't give him the first. The second I can manage, and manage in a way that lets him keep the only
thing the House's gratitude very nearly cost him along with his arm — the right to need something
without being paraded for needing it."

The General told me he thought about that conversation for years afterward without ever quite being
able to say why it had lodged itself so deep, until long after he'd started building something of his
own out of people the world had used up and then thanked loudly instead of helping quietly. He said he
came, eventually, to understand it as the first time he'd learned that the actual cost of leading
people well is very often paid in a currency no one else is ever going to see spent — which is, he
said, rather the point of it.
