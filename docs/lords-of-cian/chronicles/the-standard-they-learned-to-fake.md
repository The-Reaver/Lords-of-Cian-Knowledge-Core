# The Standard They Learned to Fake

*Locked canon, Batch 249, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1084`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LXII, wave
21. Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241), some months after the
Open-Forge Standard's institutionalization (MCD-1052). A genuinely new register of limit for
"metabolizes punishment." Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The first hammer arrived wrapped in a rag, carried three days by a farmer's son whose hand had
split open along a seam that should never have existed. The head was stamped with a mark close
enough to the Open-Forge Standard's own guild seal to fool anyone who hadn't spent eighteen months
learning to read the true one by feel — close enough, in fact, that the boy had trusted the tool
completely, right up until it failed him.

"It's not one bad hammer," the senior smith's successor said, laying out four more on the terrace
table, each carrying some version of the same near-mark. "It's four settlements over three weeks,
all bought from the same traveling cart, all sold as the genuine thing. Whoever's making these
learned the stamp faster than they learned the method underneath it."

That was the whole shape of the problem, once Kanja turned it over. Every prior punishment the
floor had ever answered had a location — a burned district, a blockaded supply road, a
mine shaft rigged to fall — something with edges a rebuild could reach around. This had none. The
counterfeit tools were already scattered across however many hands had bought them in good faith,
already in daily use in fields and workshops the campaign had never surveyed and couldn't now
retrace, and there was no single cart, forge, or district left to march toward and correct.
"We can burn every counterfeit we find from here forward," he said, "and we still won't touch the
ones already out there doing their work wrong in somebody's hands right now."

He did what could be done. A new counter-mark, harder to copy — a die cut deep enough that a
casual forger's shallow stamp would never match its exact geometry — issued through the guild
council rather than the campaign itself, so its authority didn't depend on anyone ever meeting
him. Word sent ahead of the tour's own path warning settlements what to check before they bought.
Free replacement of any tool a buyer could prove they'd purchased believing it genuine, campaign
stores absorbing the cost the way they'd absorbed the farmer's ruined harvest months before.

None of it reached backward. The boy's hand would heal wrong if it healed carelessly, same as any
injury; the tools already sold in villages three tours behind them would keep failing on their
own schedule, unrecalled and unrecallable, for as long as they lasted. And within a season, a
second forger had already found a way to fake the harder mark too — cruder, slower to spread, but
real.

"The standard was supposed to be the thing that outlived the story," the successor said, turning
the new die over in her hands. "I didn't think about what it meant for a lie to be able to
outlive it right alongside it."

"That's the cost of a method anyone can use," Kanja said. "It was never going to only be the
honest ones who learned it."
