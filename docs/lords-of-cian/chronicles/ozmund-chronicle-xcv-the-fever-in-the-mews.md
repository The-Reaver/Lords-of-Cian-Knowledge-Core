# The Fever in the Mews

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1824`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCV, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2. Set strictly pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's mid
adolescence (roughly age fourteen), years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard
alive, Ozmund still the House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from accounts given by falconer Annis Fairweather (Chronicle XLII), stable boy Cobb
(via the General's own account, extending Chronicle XLIV), and Master Alric Fenmoor (Chronicle XLIII)
— a genuine illness entry showing three of the strand's established figures covering for one another
outside their own domains. No new named characters.*

---

Annis Fairweather told me this one reluctantly, and only after I'd asked her, more than once, whether
there had ever been a season she nearly lost the mews entirely. She said there had been exactly one,
and that it had nothing to do with any bird.

She'd taken a fever in early autumn, the bad kind that goes around a household in waves and lays low
whoever it catches hardest first, and it laid her low for the better part of three weeks — bad enough,
by her own account, that she remembered very little of the first several days beyond the ceiling of her
own quarters and the sound of birds she couldn't get to. Eleven hunting birds, kept at working weight,
answering to routines only she fully held in her head, and no one else in the household with the
standing knowledge to keep them at that weight without her.

What she remembered clearest, once the fever broke enough to let her track time properly again, was
waking to find her own written feeding charts — pinned to the mews wall for exactly this kind of
emergency, though she'd never expected to need them — being followed to the letter by a stable boy who,
by his own later admission to the General, knew nothing whatsoever about hawks and had never previously
set foot in that mews without her standing beside him. Cobb had simply been told, by Fenmoor, that the
charts existed and that someone competent with animals generally needed to keep them running until
Fairweather could stand again, and Cobb had decided, without waiting to be asked twice, that a stable
boy's hands were close enough to the job to try.

Fenmoor's own role, by his account, was less hands-on and more organizational — he'd taken it upon
himself to visit her quarters daily, not as a physician, which he readily admitted he wasn't, but to
read her the mews charts aloud when she was too weak to hold the pages herself and confirm, point by
point, that Cobb's reports of what he'd done that day matched what the charts required. He told me he'd
found it strange, at first, to be spending his evenings on birds rather than books, and stranger still to
find he minded it less than he expected. He said it was, in its own quiet way, the same discipline he'd
spent years trying to teach in a very different room — checking the actual work against the actual
standard, refusing to accept "it seemed fine" as a substitute for confirmation.

The General was fourteen that autumn and, by all three accounts, present most days without ever quite
being asked to be — carrying messages between the mews and Fenmoor's study, sitting with Fairweather in
the evenings when the fever left her too weak for company but too restless for solitude, once even
standing in for Cobb an entire morning when a guard duty Kade needed covered pulled the stable boy away
unexpectedly. Fairweather told me she remembered very little of those weeks with any clarity, fever
doing what fever does to memory, but she remembered that specific morning distinctly — a boy she'd once
watched fall off his feet laughing in a pile of creels, years younger, standing in her mews reading her
own handwriting back to her weight by weight, getting every single figure right.

No bird was lost that autumn. Fairweather said she'd expected, realistically, to lose at least one or
two, given how far outside their competence both her helpers had been operating — and that she'd come
to understand, once she was well enough to properly thank them, that what had actually saved the mews
wasn't any hidden talent for falconry in either of the two boys who'd covered for her. It was that
neither of them had trusted his own judgment enough to improvise past what her own charts told him to
do, and had simply done, exactly and without embellishment, what a woman too sick to speak for herself
had already written down.

She told me she never again doubted, after that autumn, that the mews could survive her absence if it
ever truly had to. She said that was worth more to her, in the years that followed, than any single
bird could have been.
