# What the House Remembered of Him

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1769`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XL — fifth and closing entry in the second House-politics/broader-life wave. Set
strictly pre-Book-1, told with a retrospective quality spanning Ozmund's adolescence through his
mid-twenties — Aethelgard alive throughout the events recalled, the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`)
still years away at the time of anything described. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per
`VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, synthesizing his own accumulated retellings — Bevin's account (`MCD-1747`),
Marta's (`MCD-1748`), Ysbel's (`MCD-1749`), Garrow's (`MCD-1768`) — into a single closing reflection,
plus two brief, deliberately unnamed vignettes standing in for the many smaller accounts he never
individually recorded. Closes this wave on its theme without resolving anything about Ozmund's future.
No new named characters.*

---

I've written down, by now, a fair number of these — Bevin at his bread stall, Marta in the linen
stores, old Ysbel's hands in the dark, Garrow's slow last days in the kennels he'd kept for sixty
years. Each one came to me separately, from a different mouth, at a different time, and I set each
down as its own story because that's what it was to the person telling it. But somewhere in the
gathering of them — and there are more than these four, though not all of them held together well
enough, or clearly enough, to earn their own telling — I noticed they were also, without anyone
intending it that way, one story. Not a story about anything the General did. A story about what a
household, over enough years, quietly comes to expect of a man without ever quite deciding to.

I asked him about this once, directly — whether he'd known, at the time, that these small things were
accumulating into anything at all, or whether each one had simply felt, to him, like the ordinary
weather of a single morning or a single evening, unconnected to whatever had come before it. He
thought about the question longer than I expected before he answered. He said he hadn't known. He said
a man rebuilding a kitchen fire at two in the morning doesn't think of himself as laying the first
stone of a pattern; he thinks of himself as a man standing in a cold kitchen with a frightened boy
watching him work. It was only much later, he said — long after he'd left that House behind in every
way that mattered — that the shape of it became visible to him at all, and even then, mostly because
other people kept handing him pieces of it he hadn't known were still being kept.

There were smaller pieces than the ones I've already set down, fragments too slight to carry a whole
Chronicle on their own but that kept surfacing anyway, in the years I spent gathering what I could of
him. A stable groom, long past his working years by the time I found him, who remembered a night a
mare went into a difficult foaling and the young heir stayed past midnight holding a lantern steady
for a hand that wasn't his own, because the groom's hands, by then, had started to shake with age and
he'd been too proud to say so until someone noticed without being told. A laundress, decades after
Marta's own time in that House, who'd heard the story of the linen shortfall so many times from women
who'd heard it from women before them that she could recite Ozmund's exact words to the Seneschal
almost verbatim, though she herself had been a child in a different holding entirely when it happened,
and had no way of knowing whether the words she'd inherited were the true ones or simply the ones the
story had worn smooth enough to keep repeating.

That, I came to think, was the real shape of what the House remembered of him — not any single act,
weighed and judged on its own merits, but the way each one had been kept and passed on by people who
had no reason to lie about a man who'd never asked them to remember him at all. A servant doesn't
usually bother preserving, across sixty years and several changes of household, the small kindness of
an employer who never made the kindness cost her anything. She preserves it because it was rare enough,
and quiet enough, and asked for nothing back, and those three things together are rarer in most great
Houses than any single one of them alone.

I don't know what he would have made of hearing it put together this way, all the fragments laid end
to end into something that looks, from a distance, almost like a design. I suspect he'd have resisted
the word. He never built any of it toward anything, so far as I could ever tell him doing — he simply
kept doing it, one kitchen fire and one buried petition and one dying kennel-master at a time, because
each one, in its own moment, seemed to him like the only decent thing available to do, and he did
decent things the way some men breathe, without expecting anyone to notice the rhythm of it or keep
count.

What the House remembered of him, in the end, wasn't a Chronicle. It was smaller than that, and
stranger, and it outlasted him leaving in ways I don't think even he anticipated — a scattering of
small true stories, kept by people with nothing to gain from keeping them, waiting patiently across
however many years for someone to finally come asking. I only had to ask.
