# A Small Mercy

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1734`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle V. Set in the same general pre-ceremony span as Chronicles III-IV, still strictly
before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu alive. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn
Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. A deliberately warmer, lower-stakes entry giving Colonel Viktor
Draconis (`CC-085`) real personal interiority for the first time in the series — a hometown, a

Draconis almost never spoke about himself, the General told me, and when he finally did it wasn't
because anyone had asked. It was a slow winter posting, the household quiet, the two of them alone on
a stretch of corridor duty that had gone on long enough to stop feeling like duty, and Draconis had a
letter in his coat that he kept touching without opening, the way a man does with news he already
knows and isn't ready to have confirmed twice.

It was from Duskmere, he said eventually — a river town three weeks south by the low roads, the kind
of place, the General told me, that never once appears in any account of House Verehimu's holdings
because it had never needed to. Draconis had grown up there, one of a fisherman's five children, and
had a younger sister still living on the river, Halyn, who had stayed when the rest of the family
scattered and had, that winter, written to say the thaw had come early and taken the lower bank
houses with it — hers among them, her husband three years dead of a bad chest, two children too young
to be much help with anything but carrying.

He hadn't asked for anything. That was the detail the General kept returning to, telling me the story
—Draconis hadn't written back asking Aethelgard for coin, or asked leave to go south himself, because
in his own accounting a House Guard colonel's grief over a flooded bank house in a town no one at
court had heard of wasn't the kind of thing you brought to a lord's door. He'd simply carried the
letter and touched it through his coat and said nothing at all, until the General, walking beside him
in a corridor too long and too quiet for either of them to keep pretending otherwise, asked him
outright what was in it.

He told him everything, once he started — Duskmere's narrow streets and its smell of river reed, his
mother's grave there, the four siblings the current had taken in four different directions over the
years, and Halyn, who by his account had always been the steadiest of them and now had nothing steady
left under her at all. He said it plainly, without asking for anything even in the telling, the way a
man confesses a thing he expects will change nothing because it has never once occurred to him that it
could.

The General told me he sat with it a long moment before he answered, because he understood, even as
young as he was, that what Draconis needed wasn't what he was capable of giving. He could have had the
whole of that riverbank rebuilt in an afternoon and never broken a sweat doing it — could have gone
south himself and set new stone in place with his own hands faster than any crew of masons Duskmere
could hire in a season — and he told me the wanting to do exactly that was almost impossible to sit
still against. But a gift of that size, arriving in a fisherman's town with no explanation anyone could
credit, would have asked questions no lie was built to survive, and worse than that, he said, it would
have cost Draconis something to receive it that no repaired house was worth costing him — the last
piece of his life that had nothing to do with what he served.

So he did the smaller thing instead. Within the week, a modest sum went south from the House's general
almsfund, the kind Aethelgard's stewards disbursed to flood-hit tenants and townsfolk up and down the
river every few years without any particular fuss, addressed to no one in Duskmere by name and drawing
on no line of the ledger that could be traced back to any single request. Halyn's bank house got new
stone same as a dozen others that season, unremarkable among them, filed and forgotten by the clerk who
processed it before the week was out. Draconis never learned where the request had actually
originated. He thanked providence, the General told me, and let it go at that, the way a man lets go of
a mercy he has no reason to distrust.

The General told me he never once considered telling him. Not because the secret cost him anything to
keep — this one, unlike the larger one, cost him nothing at all — but because he understood, turning it
over afterward, that the gift Draconis actually needed that winter wasn't the stone. It was the simple,
ordinary fact of a House that looked after its own without being asked twice, the kind of quiet care he
had spent his whole career giving and had never once, in all those years, been given back. The General
said he'd rather Draconis believe in that House than know the truth of the hand behind it. It was, he
told me, the only lie he'd ever told him that he never once regretted.
