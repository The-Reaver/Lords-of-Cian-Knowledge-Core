# The Widow Who Asked for Blood

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle III, wave 7,
closing the wave. Rebellion era (age 19-21), unarmed and non-combat throughout, matching the alias's

---

Her husband had died three weeks before Kanja's crew reached the quarry district, crushed under a
lift cage the overseer had known was frayed and had run anyway rather than lose a day's output, and
she did not want a number written in a book. She wanted the overseer's name and an hour alone with
him.

"I've heard what you do," she told Kanja, in the yard outside the ledger tent, her voice flat in the
particular way of someone who had already cried past the point of tears helping. "You write things
down and men in offices feel bad and pay a little and everyone calls it justice. My husband is dead.
There's no number that buys him back. I don't want your book. I want that man to understand, once,
what it feels like to be the one who's afraid."

"I understand why you want that." Kanja didn't reach for the ledger, didn't try to redirect her
toward it the way he had with every other testimony that week. "I'm not going to give it to you.
Not because you don't deserve to be heard — you do, more than most people I've sat across from — but
because if I start deciding which men deserve to be hurt and which don't, I stop being someone whose
word means anything to the next person who's afraid of exactly that kind of judgment."

"Then what good are you to me at all?"

"I can make sure what happened to him is written in a place that can't be quietly erased. Not a wage
figure — he wasn't cheated of wages, he was killed by a cut corner someone chose on purpose — but a
named cause, permanent, cross-checked, put in front of every administrator above that overseer until
one of them has to answer for what he was allowed to keep doing. I can't give you an hour with him. I
can make sure his negligence outlives his career."

It was not what she had come for, and she told him so, plainly, before she left the tent without
giving him a figure to write. But the entry went in anyway — the lift cage's known condition, the
overseer's prior warnings ignored, the district's own maintenance log contradicting his later claim
that the failure was unforeseeable — and within the month, under pressure from administrators who
could no longer plausibly claim ignorance, the overseer was removed from his post.

She never came back to the ledger tent to be told. Kanja never sought her out to tell her. The entry
stood on its own, doing the only thing it had ever promised to do, whether or not it was the thing
she'd actually wanted.
