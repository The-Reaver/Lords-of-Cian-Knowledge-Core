# Lauris Chronicle XXIX: The City Was Big. She Was Small.

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1646`). Twenty-ninth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, part of the "Strand D" (Sealbound Directorate years) thread, opening this wave's
run through the Specialist and Disillusionment sub-periods (Operations 21-39). Full-scene treatment
of Operation 22 (the Velaris outbreak, previously only summarized at `MCD-1538`), dramatizing the
47-hour discrete-strike clearance of roughly 600 afflicted subjects and the origin of the "Petite
Catastrophe" alias (`MCD-1539`), quoting the already-locked perimeter-watch enforcer's after-action
line directly. Also dramatizes `MCD-1541`'s escalating institutional trust: by this operation the
Directorate had begun issuing her only contracts no other contractor in its pool could plausibly
complete. No new named characters. Zero proper-noun collisions.*

-----

*Archive fragment, Specialist designation LL-001-S-022: "Six hundred, across forty-seven hours. I
did not lose the count once, and I enjoyed none of it. I record that plainly, because the name they
gave me afterward reads as though I did."*

Operation 22 reached her already marked, in the Directorate's own internal language, as a contract
issued to an asset rather than a contractor — a distinction that mattered, she has told me since,
considerably more to the institution than it ever did to her. Velaris was a coastal manufacturing
city of some forty thousand residents, and in the span of a single night roughly six hundred of them
had begun exhibiting a density-anomaly presentation the Directorate's own medical assessors had never
before catalogued at that scale: sudden, violent, and — the detail that made the contract hers rather
than any other contractor's — spreading by proximity rather than by any traceable vector, which meant
every hour of delay multiplied the eventual cost.

She did not approach it as she had approached Brokenwall roughly eighteen months prior, the operation her own file
treats as precedent. At Brokenwall she had climbed a single sustained combat-progression curve
through the afflicted population as one continuous engagement, the pattern her career had by then
made familiar to her own body. At Velaris she chose differently, and her archive records the
reasoning without sentiment: six hundred afflicted subjects moving through a living city of forty
thousand unafflicted residents was not, in her assessment, a single battle. It was six hundred
separate small mercies, each one requiring enough precision that the person standing beside the
afflicted subject never fully understood what had happened.

She worked the city in discrete strikes across forty-seven continuous hours, sleeping — by her own
later admission to me, volunteered rather than asked for, which she does rarely about her own limits
— not at all. Each engagement was isolated to a single block, a single alley, a single afflicted
subject wherever she found them, resolved before the next residents on the street had time to
register that anything unusual had occurred at all. The Directorate's own after-action assessment,
compiled from Velaris's municipal watch reports rather than from her contract file, records that
fewer than forty residents witnessed any part of the clearance directly, out of a city of forty
thousand.

I want to record the line that followed, because it outlasted the operation itself and has followed
her, in one form or another, through every year since. A perimeter-watch enforcer, filing his own
account for the Directorate's internal contractor briefings rather than for any official record,
wrote: *She was a five-foot girl walking through the streets of Velaris terminating six hundred
people, and the city never noticed. The city was big. She was small. The catastrophe was the same
shape.* Copied into subsequent contractor briefings, the line attached itself to her permanently. She
has told me she did not choose the name, does not particularly like it, and has stopped correcting
those who use it, on the grounds that a name someone else insists on fitting you tells you more about
what they needed to believe than about what you actually are.

She traced the outbreak's cause afterward, as she had begun tracing every outbreak by then — not for
the contract's sake, which the Directorate considered closed the moment the last afflicted subject
was terminated, but for her own private accounting. What she found, buried beneath the central
plaza's public fountain, standing by municipal record over a century, was a small resonance node of
the same Ionic-Rite-derived architecture she had by then catalogued at a dozen sites without ever
once locating who was placing them. She destroyed the node outright rather than rebury it, a
departure from her Settlement K-447 practice that she notes without elaborating — I believe, though
she has never confirmed this to me directly, that years of a widening private archive of unanswered
questions had by then taught her that reburial answers nothing a determined actor cannot simply undo
a second time.

She filed the contract as closed. She did not file the node's existence, and this, I have come to
understand, is where the true seam in her career should properly be located — not one dramatic break
with the institution she served, but this: a woman who had once reported everything she found,
quietly beginning, contract by contract, to decide for herself what an institution that did not
understand its own foundations was entitled to be told.

Her closing archive note for Operation 22, terse even by her own standard: *Six hundred saved from
becoming a headline. One more question left for me alone to carry. I am beginning to notice how many
of those I am carrying now.*
