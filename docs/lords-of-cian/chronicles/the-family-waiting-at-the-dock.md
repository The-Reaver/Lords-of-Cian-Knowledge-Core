# The Family Waiting at the Dock

prose. Features already-locked crew member Danne Sok; no new named characters (Danne Sok's spouse

The Audit put in for six days of hull repair, and for the first time since he'd signed on, Danne
Sok didn't spend the layover drinking with the crew or sleeping through it. He spent it walking
inland, to a fishing settlement two hours from the docks, and came back the next morning with a
request instead of an excuse.

"My wife wants to meet you," he said. "So does my daughter, though she mostly wants to see if
you're as tall as I said."

Kanja looked up from the plating he was helping resurface. "You've never asked that before."

"Because I never used to have anything worth bringing you home to. I do now." Danne Sok didn't
elaborate further, and didn't need to — the whole crew knew what the settlement meant to him, the
thing he'd been quietly building on every layover for two years while everyone assumed he was just
disappearing to drink alone.

He came. He left Mafesto's plating with the repair crew and walked in wearing nothing that marked
him as anything, and spent the afternoon sitting on a low stool in a one-room house being watched
very seriously by a six-year-old who kept asking questions no officer or enemy had ever thought to
ask him — whether he was afraid of the dark, whether he had a favorite meal, whether he'd ever lost
a fight and cried about it.

"Once," he told her, honestly, when the others expected him to deflect. "A long time ago. It's
allowed."

Danne Sok's wife watched the exchange without saying much until Kanja rose to leave, and then she
said the only thing that seemed to matter to her: "He talks about all of you like you're the only
reason he's still whole enough to come home to us at all. I wanted to see for myself what kind of
man does that to people."

"I don't do it to them," Kanja said. "They do it to each other. I just try not to be the reason it
stops."

Walking back to the docks, Danne Sok didn't say much either, but the silence had a different weight
to it than his usual quiet — the kind of silence a man carries when something he'd kept separate his
whole life has finally been allowed to touch itself.

"Thank you," he said finally, at the gangplank. "For being someone I wasn't ashamed to bring her to
meet."

"Thank you for trusting me enough to ask."
