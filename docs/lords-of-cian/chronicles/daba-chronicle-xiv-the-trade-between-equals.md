# Daba Chronicle XIV: The Trade Between Equals

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1584`). Entry 14 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes the
explicit negotiation `MCD-1568` establishes -- the young Kanja proposing to teach Daba forging, drawn
from his own Rexmar tradition (`MCD-294` through `MCD-312`), in exchange for Daba's guerrilla
doctrine, both of them insisting the exchange be understood as a genuine trade between equals rather
than charity in either direction. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as many
agents as needed to make it efficient."*

---

It was Riasa who forced the conversation, though she never knew it, and Daba told the boy so plainly
enough weeks later that it became something the two of them could laugh about, carefully, the way
they had learned to laugh about most things.

She had come back from a supply run with a report Daba had heard some version of a hundred times
before and never stopped hating: two of the cell's blades had failed in the same week, both at the
same stress point along the same seam, both forged by the same half-trained smith working scavenged
stock because 1804 could not risk the visibility of a proper foundry contract. Nobody had died. It had
been close enough that Daba spent the night afterward doing the arithmetic he always did after a close
call, the arithmetic that traced every near-loss back to the Rookery and asked, without mercy, whether
this was the failure that would finally cost what that day had already cost once.

The boy was there when Riasa made her report -- Daba had stopped sending him away for cell business
weeks ago, a decision he'd made almost without noticing he was making it -- and he picked up the
failed blade afterward, turning it the way Daba had seen him turn the broken scaffold beam on the
night they met, running a thumb along the fracture with an attention that had nothing to do with
curiosity and everything to do with recognition.

"This wasn't bad steel," the boy said. "It was folded wrong. Whoever made this didn't listen to it
while they worked it. They just hit it until it looked finished."

"You know forging?" Daba asked, and heard, in his own voice, something close to surprise, which was
rare enough by then that he noted it.

"My family does. Did." The boy set the blade down carefully, the way a man sets down something he's
decided not to say more about yet. "It's not something anyone taught me either. It's just there, the
same way the other thing is there. I've never had metal to practice on that mattered."

Daba understood, watching him, that they had arrived without planning to at the conversation he had
been quietly circling for a week -- not sure how to raise it, because Daba had spent his whole
adulthood building 1804 on the principle that nothing was owed for free, that even trust had to be
paid for in something concrete or it curdled into dependency, and he did not know how to ask a boy
who owned nothing but his own uninstructed genius to pay for a debt he hadn't yet realized he was
accruing.

"I've been teaching you for three weeks," Daba said, "and taking nothing from you but your time. I
don't like the shape of that."

"Neither do I," the boy said, immediately, with a flatness that told Daba he'd been carrying the same
discomfort from the other direction. "I don't want you teaching me because you feel sorry for a boy
who fought off three overseers. I want you teaching me because it's worth something to you."

"It is," Daba said. "What you did with that scaffold, refined -- that's worth more to this cell than
you understand yet. But I won't build this on one side giving and the other side taking. Not even for
that."

The boy nodded slowly, and then, in the same tone he'd used to describe the ravine's arithmetic two
weeks earlier -- as though laying out a plan rather than making an offer -- said: "Then teach me the
rest of what you know, and I'll teach you and whoever you trust to forge properly. Not scavenged
stock hammered until it looks finished. Real work. My family's way, listening to the metal instead of
fighting it. Your people stop losing blades at the seam. I stop being the only thing I know how to be."

Daba considered it with the same care he gave every decision that would outlast the conversation that
produced it. It was not a small trade. Teaching a boy to fight with real doctrine, rather than raw
gift, meant putting a weapon into the world that Daba could not fully predict the ceiling of. Teaching
1804's own people to forge properly, in turn, meant a capability that could reshape what the smallest
faction in every ledger Daba had ever seen was actually capable of holding.

"This isn't a kindness either of us is doing the other," Daba said, making it a statement rather than
a question, because he needed it said aloud and understood the same way on both sides before either
of them went further.

"No," the boy agreed. "It's a trade. I want to be good at what you do. You need to be better armed
than you are. That's the whole of it."

"Then we have an agreement," Daba said, and put his hand out -- not a gesture he made often, not one
he made lightly -- and the boy took it, and neither of them let go for a beat longer than the
handshake strictly required, as though both of them understood, without saying so, exactly how rare a
debt this clean was going to be for the rest of either of their lives.
