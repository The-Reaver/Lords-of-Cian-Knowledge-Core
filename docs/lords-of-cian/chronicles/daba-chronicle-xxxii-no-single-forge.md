# Daba Chronicle XXXII: No Single Forge

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1602`). Entry 32 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes `MCD-1570`'s
"not mass-produced from a central armory" clause directly and in concrete practice -- Daba refusing a
proposal to consolidate 1804's forging into one efficient, hidden foundry, and stating the doctrine's
reasoning aloud against a real, well-argued case for the opposite. Introduces Marrek, a new named
1804 member. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make
it efficient."*

---

Marrek made his case the way Daba had trained him to make every case that mattered: on a slate, with numbers, standing up. He had run the figures three times before he brought them, which Daba respected even before he heard the conclusion, because a man who checked his own arithmetic three times was a man who did not want to be wrong more than he wanted to be listened to.

"Nine cells forging independently," Marrek said, tapping the slate. "Nine sets of tools, nine sets of scrap stock, nine people relearning the same mistakes because nobody's mistakes get shared. If we put four of the strongest forgers under one roof, somewhere defensible, somewhere we control the approaches to — we could double the output on half the material and cut the training time for the next generation by a third. I've walked the numbers every way I know how to walk them. They don't come out the other way."

"They don't," Daba agreed. "You're not wrong about the arithmetic."

Marrek waited, the way a man waits when he has learned that Daba agreeing with the first half of a sentence usually means the second half is coming for the throat of it.

"You're wrong about what we're building," Daba said. "You're building me a foundry. I'm not building a foundry. I'm building a network that doesn't have a foundry, because a foundry is a place, and a place is a thing that can be found." He set the slate down without correcting a single figure on it. "Walk it forward with me. Say we do this. Say it works exactly the way your numbers say it works — better tools, faster teaching, twice the output. And say, eighteen months from now, one man talks. Under a knife, under a promise, under nothing more than being tired and scared and twenty-two years old in a room with no windows. It only takes one. It has only ever taken one, in every network that has ever fallen, and I did not build 1804 small so that I could hand an enemy a single address that ends it."

"The cells that don't forge would still stand."

"The cells that don't forge would still stand unarmed, the exact day we needed them armed, because we'd taught ourselves to depend on the four strongest hands instead of teaching every hand something. That's not resilience, Marrek. That's a slower version of the same mistake the Rookery's own guards made, trusting one gate to hold because it was the strongest gate. I watched what happens when the strongest point in a system is also the only point." He did not raise his voice saying it, and did not need to; Marrek had heard enough of Daba's history secondhand to know exactly which gate he meant, and did not ask him to say the word aloud.

"So we stay slow."

"We stay small. Slow is your word for it because you're measuring against a foundry that doesn't exist and isn't going to. Measure it a different way. In eighteen months, under your plan, we have four forgers and a target. Under mine, we have nine forgers, each of them a dead end if anyone reaches them, and eighteen months after that, closer to twenty, because every one of the nine is already choosing who to teach next. Your output curve is a line. Mine is a doubling. It's slower to start and it never has a roof you can burn to end it."

Marrek looked at the slate a while longer, not because the arithmetic had changed but because he was doing, visibly, the harder arithmetic underneath it — the one that didn't reduce to a number, the one about what kind of thing he was actually part of. "I still think we could do both," he said finally. "A little consolidation, at the edges. Shared stock for the raw ore, at least, so we're not each of us hauling it separately."

"Shared stock is shared supply lines," Daba said, "and supply lines are the second thing that gets you found." He reached over and, for the first time, adjusted one figure on the slate — not the output projection, but the risk column beside it, which Marrek had left, tellingly, blank. "Fill that in honestly and bring it back to me. If the arithmetic still says I'm wrong once you've priced in what it costs us the day it's found, I'll hear you out properly. Until then, we stay nine forges nobody can walk to from any of the other eight."

Marrek filled it in that night, by his own account years later, and never brought the proposal back.
