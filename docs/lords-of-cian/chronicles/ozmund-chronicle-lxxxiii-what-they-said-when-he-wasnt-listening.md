# What They Said When He Wasn't Listening

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1812`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXIII, House politics strand wave 3, fourth entry.*

---

This one didn't come to me from the General at all, at least not directly, and I want to set that
down plainly before I tell it, because he never claimed otherwise. It came from Bevin, the same
kitchen scullion whose account of a cold oven and a sleepless night I've already set down elsewhere,
told to me on a second visit to his bread stall some years after the first, when I'd gone back to ask
whether he had anything more. He said he did, though it wasn't his own story this time. It was one
he'd carried secondhand himself, from a night he'd only heard about from the women who'd actually
been in the room.

The night before a regional feast House Verehimu was hosting — the largest the House gave that year,
by Bevin's account, with three neighboring Houses expected and the kind of preparation that kept the
kitchens working past midnight for the better part of a week — the household's cooking staff had
gathered in the scullery for what he called, without any irony I could detect, "the real feast," the
one that happened the night before the actual one, when the pressure of the coming day hadn't yet
arrived and the work of preparing for it had settled into something almost companionable. Bevin
wasn't senior enough by then to have been present himself — he'd have been perhaps thirty, running
his own small stretch of the ovens rather than sitting with the women who ran the kitchen entirely —
but he'd heard the story from three separate sources over the following years, closely enough matched
in the particulars that he'd come to trust it as something close to accurate.

The talk that night, as Bevin had it, had turned, as kitchen talk apparently often did in that House,
to the heir — not with any particular reverence, and not as gossip exactly either, but in the loose,
accumulating way people talk about someone whose presence has become part of the household's own
weather without anyone quite deciding to notice it. One woman, a senior cook whose name Bevin
couldn't recall with any confidence, told of finding a torn hem on her own apron mended, without
comment, sometime during a week when her hands had been too swollen with joint pain to manage a
needle herself, and no one in the kitchen willing to claim credit for it when she'd asked. Another
recalled a season when the House's fuel allowance had run unusually tight and firewood for the
servants' own quarters had been quietly supplemented from somewhere never explained, arriving each
week exactly enough to matter and never enough to draw questions. A third, older than the rest,
remembered — or believed she remembered, Bevin was careful to note the uncertainty in that particular
telling — a boy of perhaps twelve found sitting alone in the empty kitchens late one night, unable to
sleep, who had helped her knead a batch of dough gone wrong rather than wake anyone to fix it, and
said nothing to anyone the next morning about having been there at all.

Bevin said what struck him most, hearing the story secondhand and retelling it to me secondhand
again, wasn't any single kindness in it — each one, taken alone, was small enough to seem almost
unremarkable, the kind of thing a decent household absorbs without particular notice. What struck him
was that none of the women swapping these stories that night had known, until they started
comparing notes, how many of them were separately carrying one. Each had assumed her own small
unexplained kindness was a private, isolated thing, and it was only in the telling — in one woman's
story prompting another's, and that one a third's — that the pattern became visible to any of them at
all.

I asked Bevin, at the end, whether he thought the General had ever known the kitchen staff talked
about him this way. He considered the question longer than I expected before answering. He said he
doubted it — not because the General wasn't perceptive, which Bevin, by then, had every reason to
know he was, but because a man who goes to that much trouble to make sure none of his kindnesses can
be traced back to him has, by definition, made it very difficult for word of them to reach his own
ears. He said he thought that was rather the point, and that if the General ever did hear this story
told back to him, he suspected the man would be more embarrassed than pleased by it — which, Bevin
added, was itself about the surest confirmation anyone could ask for that the story was true.
