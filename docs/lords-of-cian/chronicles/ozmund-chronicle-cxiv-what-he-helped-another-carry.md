# What He Helped Another Carry

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1843`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CXIV, first entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave (part B, Chronicles
CXIV–CXX), set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. Ozmund
extends Val Mirel Kareth's Hollow Stand discipline (`MCD-1745`) outward for the first time — not
practicing it on his own grief, which he has none yet to speak of, but recognizing and helping carry
someone else's, understanding his own inheritance more fully by putting it to use in another man's
hands. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. Reuses Osric, the household's
senior steward (`MCD-1730`). No new named characters.*

---

There is a particular kind of learning, the General told me once, that never once feels like learning
while it is happening, and only reveals itself as such years afterward, turned over in memory until the
shape of the lesson finally comes clear. He gave me this story as an example of exactly that — a single
evening he did not understand, at the time, to be an education at all.

Osric had run the Verehimu granaries and stores since before Ozmund could remember being alive, and in
all those years the General had known him only as a man built entirely of steadiness — the sort of
steadiness that does not announce its own effort, that simply appears, every morning, indistinguishable
from ease. It did not occur to him, he said, that a man could carry that kind of constancy for decades
and still have something underneath it he had never once set down, until an evening late in autumn when
he came looking for Osric over some minor matter of the harvest ledgers and found the storeroom dark, the
lamps unlit, and Osric himself sitting alone among the grain sacks with a stillness in him that had
nothing steady about it at all.

He told me he almost left without speaking. That was, he said, the instinct discipline had trained into
him by then — that a man's private grief was his own room, and you did not open a door that had not been
offered to you. But something about the particular quality of that stillness stopped him at the
threshold, and he told me he thought, standing there, of his mother's own teaching: that grief carried
alone and grief carried through are not the same weight, even when the sorrow underneath them is
identical, and that the difference is not whether the weight is real but whether anyone stands close
enough to notice it's being carried at all.

So he did not leave. He sat down instead, on an overturned crate a few feet from where Osric sat, and
said nothing for a long while, because the Hollow Stand as his mother had taught it to him was never
about words — it was about presence held steady long enough that another person's grief did not have to
justify itself before it was allowed to exist in the room. He told me it was Osric, eventually, who
spoke first, and what he said was small and almost embarrassed: that it was the date, that it had always
been the date, the autumn evening some thirty years gone now on which his wife had died of a fever the
household's own healers had not been able to name in time, and that he had never once, in thirty years
of service, told a soul at Greyfen or the House seat that this particular evening cost him anything at
all, because a steward's usefulness, he'd always believed, lay in never once being the one who needed
carrying.

The General told me he did not try to fix it. He told me that was the second half of the lesson, the
part that took him longer to understand than the first — that his mother's discipline had never
promised an answer, only the willingness to sit inside an unanswered weight without flinching from it,
and that this was exactly as true of someone else's grief as it was of his own unresolved inheritance.
He did not tell Osric it would pass. He did not offer him the House's gratitude, or a day's rest, or any
of the small currencies a lord's son might reasonably have reached for. He simply stayed on the crate,
in the dark, until Osric's own breathing had settled into something that no longer needed watching, and
then walked back to the House in silence beside him, the ledger matter entirely forgotten by both of
them.

He told me — and this was the part he seemed to want me to understand most clearly, decades later —
that he had gone into that storeroom half-expecting to learn something about comforting another man, and
had come out instead having learned something about himself: that the discipline his mother had given
him for holding his own unanswerable weight was never actually built only for his own use. It was a tool
built to be lent. And a man does not fully understand the shape of a thing he was handed until the day he
finds himself handing it to someone else, and discovers it still holds.

Osric never spoke of that evening again, the General told me, and neither did he — not out of the old
habit of silence that had cost him Joren, he was careful to say, but out of something closer to respect,
the same instinct that had kept him at the threshold in the first place. Some rooms are not made less
private by having once been entered together. They are simply, afterward, a little less lonely to sit
in alone.
