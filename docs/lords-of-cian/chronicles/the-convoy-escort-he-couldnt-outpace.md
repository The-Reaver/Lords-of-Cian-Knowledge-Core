# The Convoy Escort He Couldn't Outpace

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Scourge Alias Chronicle, wave 13. Long Mask era,

---

The convoy's escort commander had built a reputation of his own, quieter than the Scourge's but no
less deliberate, on the principle that speed and dispersal beat armor and force every time the two
philosophies met — and across four days of pursuit through the Gale Straits' outer islands, he proved
the principle correct more often than not.

Every time the Forge-Coat's crew closed within striking range of the lead transport, the convoy split
into three scattered lines across pre-scouted channels, forcing a choice between pursuing the largest
prize or catching none of them at all. Every time Mafesto's stored charge built toward a decisive
strike, the escort commander's own scouts read the buildup and scattered his ships wider still,
trading formation discipline for pure unpredictability.

"He's not trying to win the fight," the successor observed on the third day, studying the pattern
from the chase ship's own deck. "He's trying to make sure there's never a fight to win. Every
engagement he lets us have is small enough to cost him one ship instead of the whole convoy."

"Then we stop chasing the convoy and start reading where it has to end up." He'd spent the third
night doing exactly that — not raw pursuit, but mapping every channel the scattered lines could
plausibly use before running out of open water entirely, the way Sovereign Eyes had once read a reef
no chart recorded, applied now to a wider, slower kind of puzzle.

By the fourth day's end, two of the convoy's three scattered lines had been run down and boarded,
freeing three hundred and eight captives across both engagements — a genuine, substantial victory,
delivered nowhere near as cleanly or quickly as a single decisive strike would have. The third line,
including the escort commander's own flagship, slipped past the mapped channels through a passage
narrow enough that pursuit would have cost more than the remaining prize was worth, and escaped clean.

"He beat us on that third line," the successor said, watching the flagship's sails vanish past the
strait's final headland.

"He didn't beat us. He made the cost of finishing the job higher than the job was worth, and I
decided not to pay it." He said it without frustration, the same flat assessment he'd have given any
honest partial outcome. "Three hundred and eight is real. It's not everything, and pretending
otherwise doesn't make the escort commander's own patience any less genuinely earned. Some fights end
in exactly this — most of what mattered, not all of it, and a rival who lives to try the same trick
again."
