# The Question Efa Gol Finally Asked

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Scourge Alias Chronicle, wave 15. Long Mask era,
age 180, V3 gear (Golden Terror era, near its close). Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

Efa Gol had stepped back from active command thirty years past, but she still traveled with the crew
when her own health allowed it, and on an evening with no engagement pending and nothing urgent
between them, she asked him a question she told him plainly she'd been holding since the night his
coat first finished burning, a hundred and fifty-eight years before.

"Do you regret it? Not any single raid. The whole of it. A hundred and fifty-eight years of being
this, instead of just being yourself."

He didn't answer quickly, which she seemed to have expected, watching him with the same patient
attention she'd once used to read a crowd's fear before it became a problem.

"I don't know how to separate the two anymore," he said finally. "That's not an answer. I know it's
not. But I've tried, more than once, to find the line between the man who existed before Ash-Wharf
and the one who's carried this since, and I can't find it clean. Too much of what I actually believe
about what a life is for got built while I was wearing this."

"That's not quite what I asked."

"I know." He allowed himself something rare, a small, honest laugh at his own evasion. "Regret asks
whether I'd undo it, given the choice. I don't think I would. Not because it hasn't cost something —
it's cost more than I let myself total up most nights. But undoing it undoes everyone the cost bought
freedom for, and I can't want that, even hypothetically, even for the version of myself that might
have gotten to just be a man instead of a legend for a hundred and fifty-eight years."

"So the mask became the man."

"Or the man grew into shapes the mask happened to be wearing first. I honestly don't know which of
those is true, Gol. Maybe neither. Maybe it's not a question with a clean answer, and I've just
learned to live inside the not-knowing instead of resolving it." He looked at her directly, something
unguarded in it that the coat, folded below in its sea-chest, had never once been present for. "You
asked me that a hundred and fifty-eight years too late to get a simple answer. I'm not sure there
ever was one."

She didn't press further, satisfied — not with the answer, which had answered nothing cleanly, but
with the honesty of a man who'd finally stopped pretending, even to himself, that the question had a
tidy resolution waiting somewhere at the end of it.
