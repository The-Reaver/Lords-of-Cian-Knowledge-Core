# Sankofa Chronicle VI: The Hand That Wrote the First Letter

*Locked canon, Batch 252, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1092`). Sixth Sankofa territory Chronicle, the fourth to
touch the forged-letter/pamphlet conspiracy from Chronicles II, IV, and V (`MCD-360`/`MCD-1023`/
`MCD-1025`) -- and its reveal. Protagonist Baálé (`PH2-021`), not a Kanja Chronicle.*

---

It took the better part of a year, and it was Yao, more than anyone, who made it possible — a man
who had once been paid to kill Baálé now spending patient months walking the same dead-drop chain
backward, cutout by cutout, until the coin trail thinned from a stranger at a dice game to a
courier who never showed his face to a rented room above a shuttered print shop that had changed
hands so many times no one remembered who'd first leased it.

Kra was the one who found the name on the lease, faded ink in a ledger nobody had thought to burn.
He said it out loud once, quietly, like testing whether it would still mean anything after this
long.

Baálé went still the way he had gone still over the fold, months before.

"I know that name," he said. "I knew that name before I knew Kra's."

They found him exactly where the lease said he'd be — an old man, alone, in a room with one window
and no weapon in reach, who did not run when the door opened and did not reach for anything when he
saw who was standing in it. He looked, Baálé thought, like a man who had been waiting for this
specific knock for longer than he could comfortably count.

"Babatunde," Baálé said. It wasn't a question.

"I wondered if you'd still know it." The old man's voice had none of the theater a decades-long
enemy deserved. It was just tired. "I ran your errands before Kra did. Before there was a Sankofa
worth the name. I knew where you slept, who you trusted, which handwriting would pass for whose."

"Which is how the fold worked," Kra said. "You wrote them yourself."

"I did." No denial, no softening. "They came to me first, before anyone thought to ask whether I
could be bought or only frightened. I told myself frightened wasn't bought. I told myself that for
longer than I should have."

Kojo, standing nearest the door, asked the only question that mattered. "And the last thirty years?
Nobody frightens a man that long without checking in."

"Nobody checked in," Babatunde said. "That's the part I don't think you'll believe, and I don't
blame you. The assignment was never closed. Not canceled, not finished — just left open, the way a
door left open stays open whether or not anyone's still standing on the other side of it. I kept it
open myself. I told myself someone would eventually come back and either release me from it or use
it, and until they did, stopping felt like admitting I'd never really been let go at all." He looked
at his own hands. "The letters were easy to keep telling myself were nothing. The boy on the roof
was harder. I sent him because I thought a threat that finally cost something might finally end it —
might finally force someone, anyone, to either close the door or walk through it themselves."

"It almost worked," Yao said, from the doorway, the first thing he'd said since they'd arrived. "I
nearly killed him."

"I know." Babatunde didn't look away from that either. "I've had a long time to imagine what I'd say
if it had."

Baálé crossed the small room and sat, unhurried, close enough that if this had been thirty years ago
and either of them had wanted it to end in blood, it could have ended in blood right there.
Babatunde didn't move against him. Didn't raise a hand, didn't reach for anything, didn't so much as
flinch away.

"The Turn" stayed exactly what it had always been — silent, waiting, useless here the same way it
had always been useless here, because there was nothing offered to bind. A conspiracy that never
showed its face had finally shown one, and it turned out the ability built to answer violence had
nothing at all to say to a man who had simply, finally, stopped.

"I'm not going to make you disappear," Baálé said. "That was never going to fix what thirty years of
an open door did. But I am going to tell people exactly whose hand wrote the first letter, and
exactly why it took this long for the door to close. Not for you. For everyone who spent thirty
years believing the thing that almost killed me was too big and too faceless to ever have a name."

Babatunde nodded, slowly, like a man setting something down he had carried so long he'd forgotten it
had weight.

"That's fair," he said. "That's more than fair."

Kra didn't say goodbye on the way out. Neither did Baálé. The door, this time, they left open on
purpose — for whoever might come looking, now that there was finally something true to find.
