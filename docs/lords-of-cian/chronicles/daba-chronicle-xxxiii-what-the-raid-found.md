The Trust patrol came to Fenmark on a tax pretext, the way they came to most places they didn't yet have real cause to search — a levy dispute over salt tonnage, a clerk's signature that didn't match the ledger, some small administrative wrongness manufactured or found to justify eleven men and a requisitions officer walking a settlement's storehouses with lanterns. Coen heard them three streets off, the same flat counted rhythm of boots he had never heard himself but had been told about often enough, by Daba, that his body recognized it before his mind finished naming it.

He had eleven minutes, by his own later count, between the first boot on Fenmark's cobbles and the requisitions officer's hand on his own door. He used four of them moving what mattered and seven of them making the room look like nothing had been moved at all.

What Coen kept in his shop, on any given week, was never the sum of what he had made. It was never more than what a single competent tinker plausibly needed on hand to explain his own trade: raw stock enough for two or three commissions, a handful of finished small work — clasps, hinge-pins, one modest reinforced strap-buckle he'd tell them, if asked, was bound for a merchant's harness — and nothing whatsoever that would read, to an eye that didn't already know what it was looking for, as anything but a competent tinker's honest inventory. The vambrace he'd finished the week before, the one Daba had checked the temper line on with his thumbnail, was three days gone already, carried out by a courier who did not know Coen's name and would not have recognized his face in a market crowd. Coen did not know where it had gone either. That was the point, and he had understood the point long before he had ever needed to live inside it.

The officer went through the shop with the particular thoroughness of a man who suspected something and could not say what. He turned over the strap-buckle twice, weighed it in his palm, asked what it was for and got the honest, boring answer, and set it back down. He asked about the raw stock and got an honest, boring answer about that too — salt-flat trade brought little enough coin that a tinker who could stretch a commission over three settlements instead of one kept himself fed better than a tinker who couldn't. He asked, eventually, the only question that mattered, though he didn't know it was the one that mattered: whether Coen had ever done work for anyone he couldn't name.

"Everyone I work for, I can name," Coen said, which was, in the narrowest and most literal sense available to him, entirely true.

They left with nothing — no ledger of buyers beyond the ordinary, no cache of finished armor, no second inventory hidden beneath a floorboard, because there was no second inventory to find; the whole of what Fenmark's cell had ever produced already lived scattered across a dozen hands in places the search would never think to walk. What the patrol filed, in the report that made its way eventually to a Trust archive nobody in 1804 would ever read, was a single line noting one tinker's shop, checked, nothing irregular, salt-flat trade as expected.

Daba heard the full account eleven days later, from a courier who had heard it from someone else, and did not go to Fenmark himself for another month after that, not wanting his own arrival to be the thread that finally connected anything. When he did go, he found Coen exactly where he had left him, working the same bellows at the same pace, and the only sign that anything at all had happened was that Coen had, on his own initiative and without being told, cut the number of finished pieces he kept on hand at any one time nearly in half.

"Overcautious?" Coen asked, watching Daba's face for the answer.

"No," Daba said. "Correct. You found the actual edge of what you needed to keep on hand, instead of the edge I'd guessed at from a distance. That's not caution. That's the doctrine teaching itself back to me, through you, better than I taught it to you in the first place." He picked up the strap-buckle the officer had weighed and set down, turned it once in the light, and put it back exactly where it had been. "That's what I was hoping this would eventually do. I just didn't expect the lesson this soon, or this cheaply."
