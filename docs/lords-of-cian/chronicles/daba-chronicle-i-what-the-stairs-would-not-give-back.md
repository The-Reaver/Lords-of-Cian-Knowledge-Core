The game was called Coalstep, and it required nothing but a floor and enough children to fill it, which the third-floor commons of the Rookery had never once lacked. You called a color, and the ones standing on it froze, and the ones who moved lost a turn sitting against the wall, laughing, waiting to be let back in. Daba had been calling colors for the better part of an hour, keeping nineteen of them occupied past their supper because their mothers were three floors down at the laundry-guild meeting and would want them tired when they came to collect them. He had just called *ash-grey*, which nobody liked because half the room was that color already, when the smell reached him before the sound did.

He knew smoke. Everyone in the Rookery knew smoke — it was a tenement block built of reclaimed timber and packed close enough that a cookfire gone wrong two buildings over could put ash on your own windowsill by morning. This was not that. This had an edge to it, oiled and deliberate, the smell of something meant to catch and meant to spread, and underneath it, arriving a half-breath later through the floorboards, boots. Not scattered. Counted. The particular flat rhythm of men walking in a line who did not expect to have to run.

He did not remember deciding anything. He remembered the door to the service stair being already in his hand, remembered saying *ash-grey wins, everybody to ash-grey, fastest color gets first pick of the good blankets tonight* in a voice that came out steadier than he felt it, and nineteen children who trusted him enough not to ask why the game had a new rule went to the wall in a scrambling, giggling line because he had made it a prize instead of an order.

Fennic met him at the stairhead with six more from the second floor, and between them they had the count they needed and a route they had drilled a dozen times for nothing worse than a kitchen fire: down the service stair, out through the laundry yard, over the low wall into the alley the Trust's own maps didn't bother to draw. Fennic took the lead line. Daba took the tail, counting heads the way you counted stitches, needing the number to come out the same at the bottom as it had at the top.

It did not, quite. Somewhere between the second landing and the yard he lost the sound of Fennic's voice ahead of him — not a cry, nothing so clean as that, just an absence where there had been a rhythm — and when he called forward there was no answer, only the growing wrongness of heat where heat should not yet have reached. He had eleven children behind him and a doorway ahead of him filling with smoke that moved with too much purpose to be accidental, and he made the choice the way he had made the game: without letting it look like a choice, without letting his voice change, turning them instead toward the laundry chute that dropped into the yard's rainwater cistern, cold and foul and three feet deep and survivable.

He went down it last, after the eleventh child, and came up in water that reached his chest with a girl no older than six locked around his neck so tightly he had to peel her fingers loose one at a time once they were both breathing air instead of smoke. Her name, she told him later, chattering, was Mika. He did not know yet what the stairwell above them had done, or how many had reached it before the timbers gave, or that Fennic's voice was one he would not hear again. He knew only the yard, and the ash starting to fall on it like slow grey rain, and eleven children plus one counted twice against a number he did not yet have and would spend the rest of his life carrying.

Behind him, the Rookery was still burning. He did not look back at it until every child in the yard had somewhere warmer to stand than beside him.
