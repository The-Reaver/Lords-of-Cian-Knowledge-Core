# Guanín Chronicle II: What He Chose to Print

*Locked canon, Batch 94, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-357`). Second entry in Guanín's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Eri Kotoko (`PH2-008`), not a
Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but granted no command, no intervention, and
no resolution credit, matching the established convention. Narration is close-third on Eri Kotoko
throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Directly dramatizes the friction `PH2-008` already locks and
Guanín Chronicle I (`MCD-340`) flags as "left undramatized, open for a future entry": the genuine,
unresolved alliance tension between Eri Kotoko and Kwame Ade (`PH2-004`) over strategy. No new named
characters -- the scene is carried by the two already-locked leaders plus unnamed criers and
distributors, matching the world's already-established pre-industrial crier/pamphlet media system
(`PH2-049`, `WC-012`/`WC-013`).*

---

The pamphlet reached Guanín three days before Eri Kotoko heard it read aloud in his own voice by a
crier who had no idea whose words they were carrying.

It was Kwame Ade's, unmistakably, printed on the same press that had set every broadside out of
Areíto for the better part of a decade, and it did not name Eri Kotoko once. It didn't need to. It
spoke of men who had made peace with earning their freedom one act of undeniable excellence at a
time, who had let themselves be conditioned into believing patience was itself a form of resistance,
and it called that patience, in the plainest words Eri Kotoko had ever seen him use, a kind of
collaboration dressed in the clothes of virtue.

Eri Kotoko stood in the crowd that had gathered to hear it, close enough to watch three separate
faces turn to find him once the crier reached the line about collaboration, and said nothing. He had
spent six years of his life becoming a man who did not answer in the moment, on a bargain that had
ended two years before this pamphlet ever reached him, and the habit had long since stopped being a
bargain's condition and become simply the shape of the man he'd made himself into.

"You're not going to say anything," the stranger said afterward, at the edge of the emptying square.
He had been there the whole reading, unremarked, the way he tended to be.

"Not today."

"He named you without naming you. Everyone in that crowd knew exactly who he meant."

"I know he did." Eri Kotoko watched the crier fold the last unsold copies under his arm and move on
toward the next square. "He's not wrong that I let myself be conditioned into a kind of quiet for six
years. He's wrong about what the quiet was for. But being wrong about that doesn't make what he said
worthless, and answering him today, in this square, with whatever's loudest in me right now, would
prove his point better than anything else I could do."

"So you'll let it stand."

"I'll let it stand until I've got something worth setting against it. That's the whole of what I
learned in those six years, if I learned anything at all. A blow thrown in the moment is just noise
looking for a place to land. A blow thrown once you've actually built the thing that answers it —
that's the only kind that ever changes a man's mind about anything."

Four months passed. Eri Kotoko said nothing publicly in all that time, though he was asked about the
pamphlet more than once, by allies who wanted him to answer and by rivals who wanted to watch him
fail to. He built instead — quietly, methodically, the same patient excellence the pamphlet had
called collaboration — a documented account, criers' testimony and counting-house ledgers both, of
every institution in Guanín that now stood because he had refused to burn anything down to build it:
schools that had opened without a single door kicked in to open them, a counting-house that paid
every worker in it the same wage regardless of what job they held, a granary that had never once
been seized because no one had ever given the seizing men a reason to come for it.

When his own pamphlet went to print, it did not call Kwame Ade wrong, either. It simply set the
account down beside his, unadorned, in the same plain language his rival had used, and let the two
sit next to each other in the same square for anyone to read without a single word of retaliation in
it — until its final line, which everyone who read it would remember long after they'd forgotten
everything printed above it: *I have never once had to ask a man to trust me with a fire he couldn't
put out himself. I don't know that the same can be said on the other side of this square.*

It was not an insult. It landed like one anyway, with a precision the four months of silence had
made possible and an angry answer four months earlier never could have.

The stranger was there again when the crier reached that final line, standing near the same spot
he'd stood in months before.

"That's the whole of it, isn't it," he said. "Not the anger. The waiting."

"The anger was never mine to spend. It was his, the day he wrote it, and it would have stayed his
forever if I'd handed it straight back to him in the square that day, angry answer for angry
insult." Eri Kotoko watched his own pamphlet change hands through the crowd, read and reread and
passed on to the next set of hands. "I don't know if he'll answer this one the way he answered the
first blow that ever actually reached him. I don't know if he even can, the way things stand for him
right now. That's not for me to know. I only had the one thing that was mine to give him, and I gave
it exactly once, exactly when it was finally worth giving."

The stranger said nothing to that, the way he rarely did when there was nothing worth adding to a
thing already said as plainly as it could be said. He was gone from the square before the last copy
changed hands, leaving the two men's dispute exactly where it had always genuinely stood: unresolved,
neither side conceded, both accounts still standing in the same square for anyone in either territory
to weigh for themselves.
