# The War-Sister's Warning

series, Chronicle XV — fourth entry in the Val Mirel strand. Set strictly pre-Book-1, years before the
Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, unmentioned in this entry. Ozmund is
roughly twenty-two. Dramatizes a piece of hard-won wisdom Val Mirel passes to her son that traces,
obliquely and without naming anyone, back to an unresolved debt from her own time in the war — a
forward hint for attentive readers, deliberately unconfirmed on the page. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn

The General told me this one plainly, without the weight some of the others carried, and I only
understood much later why it had stayed with him at all. At the time he told it to me, I took it for
a piece of motherly caution, the kind every soldier's mother hands down and every soldier half-forgets
until the moment it turns out to matter. It took me years — and a great deal I only learned long after
he was gone — to understand it had been something else entirely.

She had stayed six weeks that visit, the longest of any he could remember, and on the last night
before she rode out again she came to find him rather than waiting for him to find her, which he told
me was itself unusual enough that he knew before she'd said a word that this was not going to be an
ordinary parting. She found him on the same north terrace where, three years before, she'd told him
the truth about why she stayed away. This time she didn't sit. She stood at the rail with her back to
the dark beyond the walls, the way he said she always stood when she was about to say something she'd
turned over a long time before deciding to say it at all.

She told him she owed a debt. Not coin, not favor in the way House Verehimu understood the word — an
old debt, she said, carried from the war, to a woman she had fought beside longer than his father's
entire line had existed to count the years. She did not name her. The General told me, both times he
ever mentioned this story, that he had never once heard his mother speak that woman's name aloud, not
that night and not in any of the years after, and that some instinct in him — he couldn't say where it
came from — had understood even then that asking would get him silence rather than an answer.

What she told him was this, near enough to her exact words that I've never let myself smooth them:
that the debt had gone unpaid a very long time, not for lack of will but because the two of them had
been pulled apart by the war itself, into corners of it neither could fully see out of, and that some
debts outlive the chance to settle them cleanly. She said that if the debt was ever called — not by
her, she was careful to say, she expected to carry it herself to the end — but if it ever came due
through some line she couldn't predict or control, he would know it when he saw it, and he was not to
ask why before he paid it.

He asked her what it would look like, if it ever came. She told him she couldn't say precisely, only
what to watch for: a stranger who moved through a fight like a man who had already lost everything
once, kept going anyway, and never once let anyone see what that cost him. She told him not to test a
man like that to see whether he'd break. He said she told him, with something in her voice he'd never
heard from her before or since, that a man who has already broken and rebuilt himself once is not
offering you a weakness when he lets you close. He is offering you the only thing he has left that he
hasn't already spent, and you do not get to spend it for him a second time.

He told me he thought, at twenty-two, that she was speaking in general terms — the kind of thing a
tactician says to a son she wants prepared for a world full of exactly that sort of man, because the
Order had no shortage of them. It was only much later, long after he and I had both learned things
about his own family neither of us had asked to know, that he began to wonder whether she had been
speaking in general terms at all, or whether some part of her, standing on that terrace with her back
to the dark, had already known — the way she seemed to know most things a full turn of the world
before anyone else caught up to them — exactly whose debt she was describing, and exactly whose blood
would one day come looking for the woman who carried it.

He never asked her outright, not in all the years he had left to ask. And she never offered more than
what she gave him that one night: a warning shaped like caution, carrying, underneath it, something
that felt — he said this to me only once, and only near the very end — less like advice and more like
a door she'd left unlocked on purpose, for a stranger neither of them had met yet to eventually walk
through.
