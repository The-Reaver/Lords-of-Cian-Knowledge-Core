# The Boy Who Wouldn't Stay Down

*Locked canon, Batch 216, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-948`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LV, wave 19. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The boy was maybe fourteen, small for his age, and had taken to shadowing the crew at every site
within walking distance of Warehouse Twelve — not asking for anything, mostly just watching, until the
day a foreman's hired man knocked him flat for standing too close to a cart he didn't own and he got
back up anyway, three times, before anyone pulled him clear of it.

Kanja hadn't seen it happen. He heard about it from Efa Gol, who'd been the one to pull the boy clear,
and who mentioned it mostly in passing — one more small cruelty on a dredge site full of them — until
she added that the boy had come back the next morning wanting to know how to make sure it didn't
happen again.

"Not how to fight," she clarified. "He asked me how you stand so nobody wants to try it twice."

Kanja found him that afternoon, sitting apart from the other workers with a split lip he was pretending
not to favor, and didn't offer him anything grand. He didn't offer to fight his battles for him, and he
didn't offer to make him a fighter in any sense the boy seemed to be hoping for. What he offered was
six weeks of mornings before the shift started — footwork, balance, how to fall without breaking
something, how to read a man's shoulders before his hands moved. Nothing with a blade. Nothing that
looked, from a distance, like anything worth chalking a name onto a wall over.

The boy didn't join the crew. He wasn't recruited the way Hask or Ostra or Efa Gol herself had been,
folded into the tally network with a role and a purpose. He stayed exactly where he already was — a
dredge-site kid with a family that needed him working, not adventuring — and six weeks later he was
simply a dredge-site kid who no longer got knocked down twice by the same man.

"You could bring him in properly," Danne Sok said, watching one of the last sessions. "He's got the
build for it, given time."

"He doesn't want in," Kanja said. "He wants to stop being the smallest thing on a site full of people
looking for something to push around. That's not the same wish, and I'm not going to talk him into a
life he never asked for just because it'd be a better story."

The last lesson ended the way all of them had — no ceremony, no chalk mark, no name. The boy asked,
before Kanja left, whether he'd done enough to actually be safe now.

"No," Kanja told him honestly. "Six weeks doesn't make anyone safe from everything. It makes you
harder to knock down twice by the same kind of man, and it makes you someone other people learn not to
underestimate twice either. That's worth having. It's not a guarantee, and I'd rather tell you that
now than let you find out the hard way later."

He never saw the boy again after that season's dredge work moved to a different site. Efa Gol heard,
years later and secondhand, that he'd grown into an ordinary dockworker, unremarkable in every way
except that people who'd known him as a child sometimes mentioned, almost as an aside, that nobody had
ever managed to push him around twice.
