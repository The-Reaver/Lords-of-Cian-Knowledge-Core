The General told me this once. I have not heard it from anyone else. No one else was there.

Lowmere. A fen hamlet east of Greyfen. Eleven houses below the waterline. Forty-one people. One dike
of packed clay and timber between them and the fen. One sluice gate in the dike, on a chain.

He was a grown man by then. Old enough to ride the wetland circuit for his father. Colonel Draconis
was on the northern circuit with Lord Aethelgard. The General had a sergeant and two riders. He left
them at the inn at Greyfen and walked to Lowmere alone, at night, to hear a petition from the dike
warden. The warden had written three times. The third letter was in another man's hand. The warden's
own hand was broken.

The petition named Marek Draye. Branded once. Walked off a provincial circuit before his debt was
cleared. Burned the brand over with a smith's iron. I knew the type before the General finished
saying it. On the sand they are fighters who leave the circuit with the mass and without the rules.
They do not lose matches after that. They stop having them.

Draye carried roughly six hundred times a man's mass at rest. Lowmere had no one above ten. He had
put a toll on the dike road. Two seasons the hamlet paid it. The third season the warden said no.

Three days of rain before the General arrived. The fen stood a hand below the crown. Draye was on the
crown when the General came up the road. Iron bar through the gate chain. Leaning on it.

The General read the ground the way I would read a floor before the bell. Wet clay crown, three
strides wide. Fen on the left, high and moving. Hamlet on the right, dark, every lamp out. The gate
housing was old timber. The chain was at full strain. Two more pulls on the bar and the housing would
sunder. The dike would follow the housing. The water would do the rest. He counted the time it would
take. Less than a minute.

He said two words to Draye. "Step off."

Draye pulled.

I asked the General later what lines he had. He gave them to me plain, the way a fighter gives you a
match after.

Close and grapple: six hundred weight on a wet crown. Both men go into the sluice. The housing fails
anyway.

Strike the bar: the chain whips free. The housing fails anyway.

Wait: the second pull comes. The housing fails.

One line left. Draye.

The General closed three strides. Rain stopped falling straight over the crown. The air went heavy
and flat, the way it goes before a Maw floor cracks. Draye heard it and turned off the bar. Too late
to set his feet. The General put one open hand flat to the center of his chest.

Mass met mass. Draye's was smaller. Ribs failed in sequence, front to back. The spine after them.
Draye went off the crown on the fen side, away from the gate, and the fen closed over him. The bar
stayed in the chain. The chain held at the last link.

On the sand they would call that a clean finish. One exchange. No second bell.

The General pulled the bar out of the chain. Eased the chain off the strain a link at a time. Stood
on the crown in the rain until the fen dropped a hand. Did not go down to the houses. Did not knock
on a door. Walked back to Greyfen before light.

The warden came up the dike at dawn. Found the gate shut, the chain slack, the bar on the clay, the
toll-man gone. The fen did not give Draye back. It does not give anything back.

Lowmere tells it as the night the dike held itself. Greyfen tells it that way now too. I have heard
it in a tavern four days' ride from either place, told by a man who swore his uncle saw the water
stand up. The General never corrected any of them.

Draconis came back from the north eight days later. Heard the story at the inn. Called it bad
weather and a lucky dike. Doubled the guard on the wetland circuit that same week, at his own
expense. The General let him.

I asked one more question. Whether there had been another line he missed. He said no. I have stood
across from enough men to hear when a no has been checked. His had.

Two hundred years of living with it before the Ceremony. That was the one time. I have his word for it. His word is the
only ledger I keep on him.
