# Lauris Chronicle XXX: The Movement Built to Watch Her

*Locked canon, Batch 299, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1647`). Thirtieth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, continuing "Strand D" into the Specialist sub-period. Full-scene treatment of
Operation 26 (previously only summarized at `MCD-184`): the insurgent leader Veth Korr, found on
exit reconnaissance to have been founded and resourced by the same engineering-tradition apparatus
operating beneath the Directorate, engineered specifically to draw Lauris into a controlled,
observable engagement. Dramatizes the operation as the point from which Lauris understood the
apparatus's inherited protocols were adapting to and studying her own combat methodology across her
career, per `MCD-184`'s own framing (the automated apparatus responding to accumulating operational
data, not Anu Un Ra's own personal attention, consistent with his established dormancy). No new
named characters; Veth Korr already locked. Zero collisions.*

-----

*Archive fragment, Specialist designation LL-001-S-026: "I was sent to end an insurgency. I left
having learned the insurgency was never entirely its own. Something older built it, aimed it at me
specifically, and waited, with more patience than any human sponsor would have spent, to see what I
would do."*

Operation 26 arrived as a contract of an unremarkable shape: Veth Korr, an insurgent commander
holding a contested stretch of the Korren border territories against Directorate administration,
had, across eighteen months, inflicted losses on three separate Directorate garrison forces
disproportionate to the modest scale of his own reported following. The contract's own briefing
described him as an unusually capable local warlord. Lauris's archive records that she found this
description accurate as far as it went, and dangerously incomplete in every particular that mattered.

She engaged Korr's forward position at dawn, alone, and the engagement itself — she has told me,
and I have no cause to doubt her, that it occupies the least interesting portion of the account —
resolved within the hour. Korr fought competently, commanded well, and died the way most contracts
of his class died against her: outmatched by a margin his own experience had given him no honest way
to anticipate.

What she found afterward, in the two days of exit reconnaissance she conducted before filing her
report — time the contract did not strictly require of her, but which she had by then made a
private habit — was the detail that has since made Operation 26 the hinge she returns to more often
than any other in this stretch of her career. Korr's forces were resourced beyond what his own
territory's economy could plausibly sustain: weapons of a manufacture she recognized from the
Iron-Halls of Velkar's own malfunctioning instrumentation, supply lines that traced backward through
three deliberately obscured intermediaries to a source her own accumulating private archive had
already begun associating with the engineering tradition beneath the Directorate. Korr himself, she
concluded, likely never knew the true nature of his own patronage. He believed he was building an
independent insurgency. He was, by her reading, a controlled variable in someone else's experiment —
resourced specifically to the level of capability that would require the Directorate's single most
capable asset, and no other, to resolve it.

She wrote, in the archive entry she has since shown me in full, that the realization did not arrive
as a single moment of clarity. It arrived the way most of her realizations by then arrived: as one
more data point that refused to sit comfortably beside the data points that had come before it —
Operation 6's twins, symmetric past what an accident could plausibly produce; Operation 19's retired
archivist, who did not know who had built the house he had served his whole career; Operation 23's
untranslatable cooperative-era instrumentation, evidence of a hand that had reached Kares Prime
itself. Operation 26 added a new and specifically unsettling category to that accumulating account:
not merely evidence that the apparatus existed, but evidence that the apparatus was watching *her*,
specifically, closely enough to construct an opponent calibrated to require her personal deployment.

"I have spent six operations assuming I was investigating something," she wrote. "I am beginning to
suspect I am also, in some measure I cannot yet quantify, being investigated in return."

She filed the contract's tactical facts accurately and omitted her own conclusion about Korr's true
patronage, the same quiet discretion she had begun exercising since Velaris. What she did not omit,
because it was for her own archive alone and answerable to no one, was the operational premise she
adopted from that day forward and never again abandoned: that every subsequent contract's unusual
capability, unusual resourcing, or unusual behavior deserved the same scrutiny she had only just
learned to apply — because the apparatus that had built Veth Korr's insurgency to study her would,
she now understood, keep building things to study her, and the only honest defense available to her
was to notice faster than it could adapt.

Her closing note for Operation 26 is the shortest in this stretch of her archive, and I record it
exactly: *It was never his war. It was mine, offered to me disguised as his. I intend to notice the
next one sooner.*
