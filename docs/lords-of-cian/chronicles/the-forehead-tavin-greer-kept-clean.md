# The Forehead Tavin Greer Kept Clean

*Locked canon, Batch 118, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-403`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle VI, closing the
second wave. Years after the Dredge-Line Ambush (`CC-118`), from Compliance Officer Tavin Greer's
own perspective -- already a locked named character. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone
material. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters beyond the already-locked
Tavin Greer.*

---

Tavin Greer shaved every morning for the rest of his career with a care most men never brought to
the task, checking his own forehead in the mirror each time with the particular attentiveness of a
man who had once had three chalked words scrubbed off it by his own commanding officer in front of
the entire garrison.

He left Directorate service eleven years after the Dredge-Line Ambush, and told the story, when
asked, without the bitterness most colleagues expected of him.

"Everyone assumes it ruined me," he told the young officer interviewing him for an internal history
project decades later, the chalk-marking long since become a minor, half-remembered footnote in a
much larger war. "It should have. A captured officer, marked and mocked, released back to his own
side wearing a rebel's chosen name across his face — that's supposed to be the end of a career, or
close to it. Mine kept going another eleven years. Got promoted twice after."

"How?"

"Because the man who wrote it never once treated me the way I expected to be treated by an enemy who'd
won. No cruelty in the capture itself. No violence past what the fight actually required. The
sergeant who did the actual chalking — Breck, I later learned his name was — didn't do it out of
hatred for me personally. He was thinking about his own daughter, I found out years afterward, not
about humiliating me specifically. I was just the surface closest to hand when he needed somewhere to
put what he was feeling." Greer touched his own clean-shaven forehead, an old habit that had never
quite left him. "I spent years angry about it before I understood that. Once I did, the anger didn't
have anywhere honest left to live. You can't stay furious at a man for something that was never
really about you, once you actually understand what it was about instead."

He kept a single memento from that day — not the chalk, long since washed away, but the after-action
report he'd been required to file, which he'd deliberately drafted with more precision and less
editorializing than any comparable report he wrote before or after, describing exactly what happened
without embellishment or self-pity.

"I decided that report was going to be the one honest thing to come out of the whole humiliating
afternoon," he said. "If a rebel could write a name on my forehead and mean something true by it, even
by accident, I could at least write down what actually happened to me without lying about how it
felt. Strange kind of dignity, maybe. It's the one I found that day, and it's the one I kept."
