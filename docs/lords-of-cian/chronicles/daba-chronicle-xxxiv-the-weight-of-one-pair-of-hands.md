Wren Talsen had broken the same two fingers on her left hand three times before she ever came to Sabeth's forge, and each time she had set them herself, badly, because a proper healer meant a name in a Trust infirmary ledger and a name in a ledger meant a thread someone could eventually pull. The fingers had healed crooked and stiff, and stiff was, for a woman whose work in 1804 sometimes meant holding a rope under full weight for longer than a body was built to hold anything, a problem that no amount of will was going to solve on its own.

Sabeth measured the hand for the better part of an afternoon before she forged a single piece of it. She did not measure the way a tailor measures, taking numbers and sending them off to be worked from elsewhere; she made Wren open and close the hand a hundred times, holding a rope, holding a knife, holding nothing, watching exactly where the crooked fingers wanted to sit and where they refused to. She asked what the hand needed to do that it currently couldn't, and listened to the answer the way Daba had taught her to listen to anyone describing a problem they'd already half-solved themselves without knowing it: for the shape of the real need underneath the stated one.

"I don't need it straightened," Wren said. "I need it to stop lying to me about how long it'll hold before it gives."

What Sabeth built her, over eleven days, was not armor in the sense the word usually meant on Cian — no plate, no visible bulk, nothing that would read to a stranger's eye as anything but a worn leather glove reinforced at the knuckle the way any laborer's glove might be. Underneath the leather ran a lattice of Rexmar-forged steel no thicker than a hairline, patterned along the exact places Sabeth had watched the crooked fingers want to fold, so that the lattice did the holding the tendons no longer reliably could. It took the load off the joint and put it somewhere the steel could carry indefinitely instead. It was, in every particular that mattered, built for one hand and would have fit no other hand on Cian half so well.

There was no maker's mark on it, no stamp, nothing that would have told a second person who had made it or where — but there was, worked so small into the inside seam that only Wren herself would ever run a thumb across it and know it was there, a single line scored into the leather at the wrist, the same line Sabeth put into everything she finished, not as a signature anyone else could read but as a private accounting of her own hand's growing steadiness. Wren asked her once what it meant. Sabeth said only that it meant she'd made it, and that was answer enough for both of them.

When Wren wore it for the first time under real weight — a rope bridge rigging, three hundred feet of cable strung between two gorge walls in the dark, nothing about the work forgiving of a hand that lied — she held the full duration without the familiar creeping ache that had always, before, told her exactly how many minutes she had left before her grip started to go. She did not think, in the moment, about Kanja's Rexmar tradition, or Daba's doctrine, or the eleven days Sabeth had spent watching her hand instead of simply casting a shape from a chart. She thought only that the glove had stopped lying to her, which had been the entire of what she'd asked for, and did not know, until Daba told her so much later, that it had been asked for and answered exactly that precisely on purpose.

"That's the whole of it," Daba said, when Wren described the gauntlet to him at a gathering neither of them had expected the other to attend. "A central armory gives you the nearest size off a shelf. What we're building gives you the actual shape of what you need, made by someone who watched you fail first. It costs more time. It costs a name — hers, to yours, and back — that a shelf never has to know. But it never once hands an enemy a warehouse to burn, and it never once hands you a glove that doesn't know your own hand." He did not ask to see it up close, and Wren understood, without being told, that this too was doctrine: the fewer hands that touched a piece after its maker was done with it, the fewer threads there were to trace it home.
