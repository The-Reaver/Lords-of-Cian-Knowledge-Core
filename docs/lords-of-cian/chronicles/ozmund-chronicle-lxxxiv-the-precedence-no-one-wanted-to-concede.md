# The Precedence No One Wanted to Concede

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1813`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXIV, House politics strand wave 3, fifth entry.*

---

The General laughed once, telling me this one, and said it was the closest thing to genuine farce he
could offer me from those years — not because the stakes were imaginary, he was careful to add, but
because the form the dispute took was so thoroughly, absurdly small that it had taken him longer than
he liked to admit to realize how much actually rode on it.

House Renlow and House Ashmere held adjoining territories some distance north of the Verehimu lands,
allied to House Verehimu by old treaty and to each other by nothing more than geography and a long,
mutually inconvenient habit of disputing precedence whenever both Houses attended the same regional
gathering. The particular argument that reached House Verehimu's involvement — Ozmund was
twenty-four, sent as his father's representative to a border ceremony neither Renlow nor Ashmere
could resolve between themselves — concerned nothing weightier than seating order at the formal
supper marking the ceremony's close: which House's delegation would be seated nearer the host's own
table, a distinction that carried, by the region's old customs, real symbolic weight regarding whose
standing the assembled peerage was meant to recognize as senior.

The General said the actual history behind the dispute was, once he'd bothered to have it explained
to him in full, almost too tangled to summarize — competing claims running back three generations,
resting on a marriage that may or may not have technically elevated one House's rank, a disputed
reading of an old treaty's phrasing, and at least one incident, decades past, that both Houses
remembered with total confidence and in completely contradictory versions. Neither House was
willing to yield the point, not because either side particularly believed its own historical claim
beyond challenge, but because yielding, at that stage, had come to mean something larger than seating
— a public concession of lesser standing that neither House's own pride could absorb in front of the
assembled region.

He said his first instinct was to simply decline the whole matter as beneath a Verehimu
representative's proper business — House Verehimu held no formal authority to arbitrate a dispute
between two other Houses, and could plausibly have left them to settle it, or fail to settle it,
themselves. What stopped him, he told me, was recognizing that an unresolved seating dispute
threatened to overshadow the ceremony itself, which mattered rather more than either House's pride —
a border-renewal ceremony both Renlow and Ashmere genuinely needed to go well, for reasons that had
nothing to do with seating and everything to do with the peace it was meant to formalize.

What he proposed, once he'd studied both Houses' competing claims closely enough to understand that
neither was demonstrably wrong, wasn't a ruling at all, but a precedent borrowed, at some remove, from
an old Verehimu custom he'd encountered once in his own House's records — a rotating seniority,
applied not to this one supper but to the whole ongoing relationship between the two Houses, alternating
which delegation sat nearer the host's table at each successive joint gathering, the rotation itself
recorded formally enough that no future dispute over it could arise. Neither House, he pointed out to
both delegations in turn, would be conceding the other's historical claim by accepting it — the
rotation simply set the argument itself aside, permanently, in favor of an arrangement that treated
both Houses as equally deserving of the honor across time rather than forcing either to lose the
argument once and for all.

The General said the proposal wasn't received with any particular warmth by either side at first —
both delegations, he suspected, had arrived hoping for a ruling that vindicated their own claim
outright, and a compromise that handed neither House total victory satisfied neither House's pride in
the moment. What won it, in the end, wasn't the elegance of the solution but a simple practical
argument he made to both delegations privately, separately, before either could be seen backing down
in front of the other: that the alternative to accepting the rotation was continuing to relitigate the
same three-generation argument at every joint gathering for the next three generations, at real and
mounting cost to both Houses' patience and standing, over a question neither side could actually prove.
Both delegations accepted it within the hour, each privately, before either could be seen agreeing
first.

He told me the rotation held for as long as he had any way of tracking it afterward, and that what he
took from the whole episode wasn't pride in the solution itself, which he considered more clever than
wise, but a clearer sense of something he said he'd go on using for the rest of his political life:
that most disputes people insist are about principle are, underneath, disputes about being seen to
lose, and that a settlement letting both sides claim they hadn't lost holds far more often than a
correct one that makes someone.
