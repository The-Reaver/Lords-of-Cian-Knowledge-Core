# Two Kinds of Safekeeping

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1801`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXXII — third entry in the Val Mirel strand's third wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive and present
throughout. Ozmund is roughly fourteen, shortly after Chronicle LXXI. Dramatizes a genuine, nuanced
disagreement between Val Mirel and Aethelgard over how to raise their son in the aftermath of the
ambush — neither parent framed as wrong. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per
`VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years later. No new named
characters.*

---

This is one of the very few stories the General ever gave me in which his parents disagreed openly, in
front of him, about something that mattered — and he told me, more than once, that he'd nearly kept it
from me entirely, not because it reflected badly on either of them, but because he'd spent so many
years protecting the memory of them as a partnership that admitting they'd ever genuinely pulled in two
directions felt, to him, almost like a confession.

It happened perhaps three weeks after the road, once the House had quieted enough that the two of them
could speak of what to do about it rather than simply managing the fact that it had happened. He was
not meant to hear the conversation. He told me he'd wandered near enough to his father's study by
accident, on his way to somewhere else entirely, and had simply stopped in the corridor when he
recognized both their voices raised, carefully, in the particular register adults use when they are
trying very hard not to actually raise them.

His father's position, as near as the General could reconstruct it for me, was that the ambush proved
the House Guard alone could not be trusted to hold a threat like that off, and that Ozmund's protection
from here forward needed to be formalized — a permanent, visible escort, a curtailment of unsupervised
travel, and, eventually, the beginnings of the political marriages and alliances that would surround
an heir with enough interested parties that no single rival House could move against him without
answering to a dozen others. It was, the General told me, exactly the kind of solution his father's
whole world was built to produce: protection through visibility, through obligation, through making the
cost of the attempt higher than any single enemy could afford.

Val Mirel's position was the opposite, and the General told me he'd never heard her voice carry quite
that edge before, not in anger exactly, but in something close enough to it that it frightened him more
than the ambush itself had. Cage him in enough protection, she argued, and the House would raise a boy
who had never once been tested by the world without a dozen swords between him and it — a boy who
would reach manhood having never learned, in his own body, what he was actually capable of when no one
was there to catch him. She did not deny the danger. She told Aethelgard plainly that she understood
it better than he did, having spent centuries learning exactly what real danger looked like up close.
What she refused to accept was that the answer to danger was less freedom rather than more discipline —
that a son wrapped in enough protection to never be tested was not a son who had been kept safe. He was
a son who had been kept small, and small men did not survive the kind of world either of them actually
expected him to inherit.

The General told me the argument went on longer than he stayed to hear, and that what finally sent him
away from that corridor was not the content of it but the realization, dawning slowly, that both of
them were right, and that neither of them was going to concede it to the other in that room. He carried
that discomfort with him for days before either parent spoke to him about it directly.

What they settled on, he told me later, was neither position whole. There would be more House Guard
attention on his movements, yes, quietly rather than visibly, enough that Aethelgard could sleep. But
there would be no curtailment of the unsupervised travel Val Mirel had argued for, no marriage
politics begun before he was old enough to have any say in them, and instead — this was the part he
said he only understood the weight of years later — an acceleration of exactly the training his mother
had already been giving him, the Stone Count deepened, the Narrow Door's hard lessons returned to more
than once in the months after, on the theory, which both his parents came to share even if they'd
arrived at it from opposite directions, that a boy who could actually hold himself in danger was safer
than a boy who was merely surrounded by people paid to hold it for him.

He told me, decades on, that it was the first time he understood his parents as two people who loved
him enough to fight about how, rather than two halves of a single seamless authority he'd simply
accepted without examining. He said it frightened him a little, at fourteen, to learn they could
disagree that sharply and still, within the same week, build something together out of it that neither
of them had proposed alone. He told me it was also, looking back, the moment he stopped being afraid of
disagreement itself — the moment he understood that two people could hold two true things at once,
refuse to let either one win outright, and still end up somewhere neither had started.
