# What He Taught Without the Mask On

*Locked canon, Batch 200, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-827`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle XLII, wave 14 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The newest crew hand had joined only that season, recruited through the ordinary channels the crew
used to replace losses and retirements without ever revealing the whole shape of who they actually
served, and had spent three weeks aboard without once seeing the Forge-Coat or learning what name the
strait's rumors used for the ship's quiet, unassuming quartermaster.

He found the boy on deck one slow evening, struggling with a length of rigging rope in a knot that
kept slipping loose no matter how many times he retied it, and sat down beside him without any
ceremony, off-duty, unmasked, nothing about the moment suggesting anything but one sailor helping
another with an ordinary problem.

"You're crossing the working end under instead of over," he said, taking the rope gently. "Watch."
His hands moved through the knot slowly, twice, narrating each turn until the boy could follow it
without needing to watch the hands themselves. "It's not about strength. Rope doesn't care how hard
you pull it. It cares whether the shape's right."

The boy tried again, got it right on the fourth attempt, and grinned with the particular pride of
someone who'd just earned something rather than been handed it.

"You're good at teaching," the boy said. "Better than the bosun. He just gets frustrated and does it
himself."

"Doing it yourself is faster. Doesn't help anybody learn anything, though." He checked the boy's knot
once more, satisfied, and moved on to showing him how to read the wind's shift a few seconds before
the sails themselves would betray it. "Most of what's worth knowing on a ship like this, somebody
patient enough taught somebody else, one small thing at a time, without needing it to be dramatic to
matter."

They spent the rest of the evening on nothing more consequential than knots and wind and the honest,
unglamorous work of a deck hand's earliest education, and the boy went to his bunk that night having
learned three real, useful things from a quartermaster he still knew nothing else about.

Months later, deep into a season that would eventually show him exactly what coat that quiet man
sometimes wore, the boy would tell it to newer hands of his own as the first thing he really knew
about their captain — not any battle, not any rescue, just an evening of patient hands correcting a
knot, taught by someone who'd never once needed the mask on to be worth learning from.
