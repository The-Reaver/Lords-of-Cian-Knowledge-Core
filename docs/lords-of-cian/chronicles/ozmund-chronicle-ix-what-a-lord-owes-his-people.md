# What a Lord Owes His People

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1738`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle IX — the Aethelgard strand's third entry, set strictly pre-Book-1, years before the

He told me this one plainly, without the weight some of the others carried, as though it were simply
a fact about how his father had done business rather than a story that needed telling carefully. I've
come to think that was its own kind of tell. The stories that cost him something to say, he told slow
and low, like the one about Draconis. This one he told the way you'd describe a habit you'd inherited
without ever deciding to.

Twice a year Aethelgard rode the Wetlands circuit himself rather than send a steward, the same
instinct that had him take his son past the walls at all — a lord who governs from reports governs a
country that exists only on paper. Most years it was nothing worse than a flooded grain store or a
boundary dispute between fen-families who'd been arguing the same half-acre for three generations.
The year the General told me about, it was a levee at a settlement called Greyfen, low and reed-choked
even by Wetlands standards, that had been reported repaired eight months earlier and had, in fact,
never been touched.

The dike-warden who'd made the original report to the district steward was a man named Corwen Dask,
and he did not want to see Aethelgard when Aethelgard's column rode in unannounced a full season ahead
of the scheduled inspection. The General remembered that part specifically — how careful the man was,
how much of himself he was visibly holding back, the practiced flatness of someone who has learned
that complaining to power costs more than the flood it complains about. His family had been working
that levee for four generations. He told the lord, when pressed, in the fewest words he could manage,
that the repair funds had been logged as spent and the levee was exactly as unsound as it had been
the day he first reported it, and that he assumed, at this point, it would stay that way.

Aethelgard didn't send anyone down to check it. He climbed down off his own horse, in the clothes he'd
ridden in, and walked the levee himself — the General said his father came back with mud to the knee
and a torn coat and didn't seem to notice either — pressing his boot into the soft places, asking Dask
plain questions about where the water came fastest and how many nights of hard rain the structure had
actually held versus how many it had merely survived. It took the better part of an hour. Nobody in
the column, including the boy, had expected a lord to do that part himself rather than simply take a
report and issue an order.

What happened after was the part the General said he'd actually gone there to watch, though he hadn't
known it at the time. Aethelgard didn't summon the district steward for a private reckoning, out of
Dask's earshot, the way the boy had assumed a lord handles being lied to. He stood in the mud in front
of the whole settlement — everyone who'd gathered by then, drawn by the sight of the column — and said,
loud enough to carry, that the fault was his own: that he'd trusted a report he hadn't verified for
eight months while the people who'd have drowned for that trust kept living under a levee he'd been
told, falsely, was sound. He apologized to Corwen Dask by name, in front of his neighbors, and only
then gave the order — real funds, a mason he named on the spot, a return visit inside a season he set
himself rather than leaving to the steward's discretion.

On the ride back, the boy asked him why. Why the apology, specifically — the order alone would have
fixed the levee; the apology fixed nothing the order didn't already fix, cost the lord something in
front of people who couldn't have made him pay it any other way. Aethelgard was quiet long enough that
the General thought the question had annoyed him, and then he said something the General repeated to
me exactly, decades later, the way he repeated very little else word for word: "The order tells them
the levee will hold. The apology tells them that if it doesn't, next time, it'll be because I failed
them and said so — not because I hid behind a steward's report and let them believe it was somehow
their own fault for trusting me. A lord who can't be embarrassed in front of the people he's failed
will fail them again, and worse, because he'll have learned nothing costs him anything. I'd rather it
cost me something every single time."

The General told me he didn't understand the lesson fully until decades after his father was dead —
until he was building something of his own, out of freed men and women who'd spent their whole lives
being lied to by people with the power to fix what they broke, and found himself standing in front of
them admitting fault before he gave any order at all, without remembering, at first, where he'd
learned to do that.
