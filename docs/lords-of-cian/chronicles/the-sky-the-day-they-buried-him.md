# The Sky the Day They Buried Him

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle LIII, wave 18. Long Mask era, roughly a year after "The Reading He Could No Longer Make Alone." Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. Names Sephtis and his successor; no new named characters.*

---

Sephtis died in his sleep, on land, in the small house near the harbor the fleet had built for him
when his sailing years finally ended. There was no storm in it, no last dramatic reading, nothing that
made his death resemble the craft he'd spent his life practicing. He simply did not wake, on an
ordinary morning, at the end of an unremarkable night, at an age that made the fact unsurprising to
everyone but somehow no easier for it.

The funeral rites were to be held at sea, by the old custom, carried out from the deck of the ship
that had carried his voice longest. That meant a call had to be made — whether the water would hold
calm enough for the rites to be done properly, or whether the fleet would need to wait a day, or two,
for weather that would let the ceremony proceed without indignity.

It fell, without discussion, to his successor to make it.

She stood alone at the rail where the two of them had once argued a disagreement into a two-hour
delay, and read the sky the way he had taught her to read it across decades, and the way she had, for
years now, long since made entirely her own.

"Clear through tomorrow," she said. "Calm enough. He'd have called the same, and he'd have been
right."

Kanja, standing near her, said nothing for a long moment.

"You could have let someone else make this one," he said finally. "No one would have questioned it."

"No," she said. "This is the one call I couldn't hand to anyone else. Every other reading I've ever
made, I made because he taught me how. This one, I needed to make because of what he taught me it
means to be trusted with a sky that matters to people who are watching you and grieving at the same
time."

The rites were held the next day, under a sky exactly as calm as she had called it, and no one
present ever asked whether that calm was the craft working, or simply the ordinary kindness of an
unremarkable day arriving when it was needed most. She never asked herself, either. Some readings, she
had come to understand, were true whether or not anyone could prove they mattered.
