I met Bevin only once, an old man by then, running a bread stall near the Karkosa dockside markets
that did better trade than a man his age had any right to manage alone. He knew who I was before I'd
finished introducing myself — the General's reputation traveled ahead of him even into flour and
yeast — and when I told him I was gathering what I could of the years before I'd known his old
master, he set down his paddle, wiped his hands on his apron, and told me this without my having to
ask twice. He said he'd been waiting a long time for someone to want it.

He'd been eleven, new to the Verehimu kitchens, the kind of low that doesn't get looked at twice by
anyone above the pantry stair, when he made the mistake that should have cost him the position. He'd
fallen asleep against the flour sacks sometime past midnight, exhausted from a full day and a short
night before it, and woken to find the ovens gone cold two hours before the dawn baking was meant to
begin — bread that wouldn't rise in time, a kitchen staff that would answer for it, and a head cook
who Bevin said, flatly, would have had him out on the street by breakfast and thought it mercy not
to have him whipped first.

It was Ozmund who found him first, not the cook. Bevin never did work out exactly why the heir of
the House was awake and walking the kitchen passage at that hour — he'd wondered about it for sixty
years and never landed on an answer that satisfied him, though he'd come, by the time I met him, to
his own private theory, which was that the General simply never slept the way ordinary men did and
had taken to walking instead. Whatever the reason, he found an eleven-year-old boy in a genuine
panic over cold ovens and did not call for the cook, and did not wake the household, and did not so
much as raise his voice.

He built the fires himself. Bevin was very particular about this detail, the way a man is particular
about the one moment of his life he's replayed the most — not directed, not supervised, but on his
knees at the ovens in the clothes he'd been wearing to a formal supper six hours earlier, splitting
kindling with his own hands while a scullion boy fed him wood and forgot, for whole minutes at a
stretch, to be afraid. He asked Bevin's name. He asked where he was from, and whether the work suited
him, and listened to the answers the way Bevin said no one above the pantry stair had ever listened
to anything he'd said before or since. By the time the ovens were drawing properly there were perhaps
forty minutes left before the household would begin to stir, and the bread went in only slightly
behind its usual hour — late enough that the head cook noticed, not late enough that she thought to
ask why, because the answer she was given was that Bevin had simply worked faster than usual that
morning, and no one thought to doubt a boy taking credit for his own competence.

Bevin told me he never once heard the General mention it again, not that week, not in the eleven
years he went on working those kitchens before leaving to open his own stall. There was no favor
afterward, no promotion, no quiet word that marked him out as someone owed something. That, Bevin
said, was the part he'd turned over longest in sixty years of turning it over — that a man who could
have made the story about his own mercy instead simply let it be nothing, let it cost him nothing,
let Bevin keep the only thing worth keeping out of it, which was that he hadn't actually failed.

I asked him, at the end, why he'd never told anyone the true version while the General was near enough
for it to matter to him. Bevin thought about that for a while, working his paddle through the coals,
before he answered. He said he'd always suspected the General didn't want it told — not from shame,
and not from modesty exactly either, but because a man who'd spent his whole life being watched for
what he was worth had worked out early that the only gifts that meant anything were the ones nobody
saw him give.
