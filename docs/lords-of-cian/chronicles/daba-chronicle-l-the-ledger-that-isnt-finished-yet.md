Nothing was wrong that evening, which was itself worth noticing, because Daba had lived long enough
inside the particular discipline of his own life that an evening with nothing wrong in it registered
almost as strange as one gone badly. The cell had gathered in the low room they were currently using
for meals -- eleven of them, that night, a number he only realized he'd counted once he noticed himself
doing it, the old habit surfacing the way it always did in any room he sat in for longer than a few
minutes, unbidden, automatic, no longer anything he needed to fight. Eleven. He let the number settle
and did not go looking, the way he once would have, for who might be missing from it. Nobody was
missing. It was simply eleven people eating a plain meal on an unremarkable night, and the number came
out the same at the end of counting as it had at the start, which was, he had learned across a very
long life, the only kind of arithmetic that had ever actually mattered to him.

Wrenna was telling a story about the fish again, embellishing it further with each retelling until it
had acquired, by this point, something close to the structure of a proper legend -- a fish that had, in
her latest version, developed both a name and a grudge. Perrin was only half listening, his attention
on a supply map he'd unrolled across one corner of the table out of habit more than urgency, tracing a
route with one finger the way another man might worry a coin. Mika sat closest to Daba, saying little,
the small locked box that held her own private accounting of the cell's living and its dead left
untouched on its shelf across the room, exactly where it belonged on a night that asked nothing of it.

Someone asked, eventually, the question that got asked every so often in rooms like this one, usually
by whoever was newest and hadn't yet learned that it rarely got a satisfying answer. It was Wrenna, this
time, mid-story, who paused long enough to ask it plainly: what, exactly, was all of it *for*. The
drilling, the dispersal, the decades of staying small enough not to be seen. What was 1804 actually
waiting for.

The room went quiet in the particular way it went quiet whenever that question surfaced, not from
discomfort but from genuine attention, because it was a fair question and everyone present had asked
some version of it to themselves at least once.

Daba considered it honestly, the way he tried to consider everything Wrenna asked, precisely because
she asked so few things carelessly. "I don't know," he said, and meant it completely. "I've never known.
I built this on a lesson I learned in one terrible night and have spent every year since trying not to
need repeated -- that a thing large enough to be seen is large enough to be burned, and a thing small
enough to survive is worth keeping alive even when you can't yet say what it's being kept for. I taught
that to people who taught it to people who are teaching it to you now. None of us has ever needed to know
the reason in advance. We've only needed to still be here, whole and unburned, on the day the reason
finally arrives."

"And if it never does?"

"Then we will have spent a very long time keeping each other safe for no reason at all," Daba said,
"and I find, on nights like this one, that I could live with that outcome more easily than most people
would expect." He looked around the low room once more -- Wrenna already returning to her fish, Perrin
still tracing his unhurried route, Mika quiet and present beside him, the count still eleven, still
whole, still correct. "But I don't think that's the outcome. I think something is still coming, the way
weather is always still coming even on the clearest day, and I think none of us gets to choose when.
What I can tell you is that whenever it arrives, it will find this cell exactly as it's found every
trouble before it -- small, ready, and not remotely where anyone expected to look."

He did not say more than that, because there was not, honestly, more that he knew to say, and because
he had learned, across a life spent teaching people to trust an unfinished plan, that the truest thing
you could offer anyone waiting on an unwritten ending was simply your own steady presence in the room
while it stayed unwritten. The meal went on. The fish story acquired, before the night was through, a
second grudge and an entirely invented cousin. Somewhere beyond the walls of that ordinary, unremarkable
room, a world Daba had spent decades deliberately staying outside of kept turning without him, carrying
toward whatever it was carrying toward, and he let it turn, and did not reach for it, and counted his
people once more before he slept -- eleven, correct, the ledger, as it always was and always would be
for as long as this work required of him, not yet finished, and not, tonight, in any hurry to be.
