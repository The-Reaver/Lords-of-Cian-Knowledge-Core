# The Lecture Hall Didn't Know What to Do With Him

*Locked canon, Batch 154, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-487`). The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle XII, closing
the fourth wave. Rebellion era, age 20, the Sewer War of Killane. Not a territory Chronicle. New
standalone material. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

A Sovereign Trust engineering college, desperate enough for practical tunnel-reading knowledge that
academic pride finally lost to necessity, extended a formal invitation for the Blue-Collar Titan to
lecture its senior engineering class on structural failure prediction — an invitation nobody involved
in arranging it seemed entirely comfortable with.

The hall didn't know what to do with him. He arrived in work clothes rather than anything resembling
academic dress, declined the lectern in favor of standing among the students, and opened not with
theory but with a question: how many of them had ever actually put a hand against failing stone and
simply listened to it.

None had.

"Then we should start there, not with equations." He spent the following two hours teaching the same
tactile method the old Killane digger had once taught him — pressure, weight, the specific feel of
stone under real strain — translating hard-won trade knowledge into a formal lecture hall that had
never once been asked to sit with something so unglamorous as another man's calloused hands on raw
material.

"Your methods contradict several established structural models," one student said afterward, not
hostile, genuinely working through the tension between what he'd just learned and years of formal
training. "How do we reconcile the two?"

"You don't have to choose one over the other. The models tell you what should be true, in the
abstract. The hands tell you what's actually happening, in the specific tunnel you're standing in
right now." Kanja considered the question with real care, matching the student's own seriousness.
"I've watched formal training miss what an experienced digger's hands catch in seconds, and I've
watched an experienced digger miss what a model predicts before it's visible at all. The people who
actually keep tunnels standing use both. Don't let anyone convince you it has to be either-or."

The college's dean, watching from the hall's edge with visible discomfort at the whole arrangement,
admitted afterward it was the best-attended lecture the engineering program had held in years —
students staying long past the scheduled end, asking questions no formal curriculum had ever
prompted.

"I don't think we quite knew what to expect," the dean said. "I'm not certain we know what to do with
it now that we've had it."

"You don't have to know yet. Just don't let the discomfort talk you out of what actually worked
today." Kanja gathered his things, unbothered by the hall's own uncertainty about him. "That's been
true of most things people eventually found useful about this whole method."
