# Kanja Chronicle II: The Lesson He Carried Alone

*Locked canon, Batch 313 (`MCD-1867`). Second entry of the "Kanja version" Chronicle track,
written second but set chronologically *first* — before Chronicle I, before the Rebellion begins,
before Kanja's fifteenth year, matching the project's established write-order-vs-in-universe-order
precedent (Xaragua Chronicle II, Batch 66). Dramatizes the mutual mentorship between Kanja and
Daba (`MCD-1568`/`1570`) from Kanja's own side for the first time — Daba's own 50-Chronicle launch
wave (Batch 296) covers the same relationship from his POV; this entry is deliberately a quiet,
unspecified night rather than any single dated lesson, to avoid restaging a scene already told.
Set explicitly before Kanja bonds with Onyx of Oblivion at seventeen (`ARS-020`) — no blade, no
narrator presence, no coda of any kind. No new named characters. No child-safety issues.*

---

The lesson that night had not gone well, and Daba had not pretended otherwise.

They were on the high bank above a drainage cut two miles inland from the coast road, a stretch
of ground Daba had chosen because it looked, from a distance, like nothing worth defending — low
scrub, a collapsed footbridge, a scatter of boulders too irregular to be called a wall. Kanja had
been given a simple problem three hours earlier: hold the cut against six of Daba's people
approaching from any direction they chose, using only the ground itself.

He had lost. Not decisively — two of the six had gone down to a rockslide he'd rigged along the
western slope before they reached him — but the other four had come through the collapsed
footbridge in a formation he hadn't accounted for, and by the time he understood what had
happened, three practice blades were resting against three different parts of him at once.

"You held the ground," Daba said, when it was over and the six had melted back into the scrub the
way they always did, without ceremony, as if the exercise had simply stopped existing. "You didn't
hold the approach."

"I counted four ways in. There were five."

"There were five ways in and one way through. You fortified the ways in." Daba crouched beside
the collapsed footbridge, running a hand along the underside of a beam that had not, in fact,
collapsed on its own — Kanja could see the cut marks now, old ones, weathered enough to have
happened years before either of them stood here. "This bridge has been down for longer than you've
been alive. Everyone who's ever crossed this cut in the last decade has learned to go around it.
So when six people came at you tonight, how many of them did you expect to use it?"

"None. It's not a way in. It's an obstacle."

"It's a habit. A habit and an obstacle aren't the same thing, and the difference is the whole
lesson." Daba straightened, brushing dust from his knees with the unhurried economy of a man who
had done this exact motion in far worse places than a training exercise. "You fortified four roads
because four roads are what a map shows you. The fifth way in wasn't on your map because it wasn't
a road. It was a bridge nobody uses, which means nobody watches it, which means it's the one route
your enemy can walk across in full daylight and nobody — including you — will think to look."

Kanja said nothing for a while. He was still holding the rope he'd used to rig the rockslide,
coiling it without any particular attention to the coiling, the way a person's hands sometimes
keep working while the rest of them is somewhere else entirely.

"At the forge," he said eventually, "there's a rule about the fourth heat. You never trust a piece
that's only failed three times. The fourth failure is the one that tells you the truth, because by
then you've stopped expecting it, and whatever's actually wrong stops hiding behind the excuse of
bad luck."

"That's the same lesson."

"I know. I didn't apply it. I applied the forge rule to the metal and forgot it applies to
ground too."

Daba sat down on one of the irregular boulders, not because he was tired, but because sitting was
sometimes the fastest way to tell a boy he wasn't in trouble. "You want to know something Kanja,
Rex and Mar don't have anywhere in them?"

"What."

"Shame at being wrong in front of somebody. Every fighter I've trained who came up through a
formal house or a formal school flinches at that moment — the moment the mistake is visible to
someone else. You didn't flinch. You went straight to the forge and found the version of the
lesson you already owned." He was quiet a moment, watching the dark shape of the useless bridge.
"That's not nothing. Most people spend the humiliation instead of spending it on the lesson. You
skip a step everyone else has to pay for."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It's an observation. I don't know yet if it's a compliment or a warning. A person who never has
to sit with being wrong might also never learn to sit with someone else being wrong at him. We'll
see which one you turn out to be."

Kanja considered this the way he considered most things Daba said to him — turning it over slowly,
looking for the load-bearing part, the way Maren would one day teach him to look at a beam. "You
didn't correct the ground the way you corrected the forge rule. You just told me the bridge was a
habit and let me find the rest myself."

"I could've told you exactly what I told the six earlier tonight. Where to cross, when, how fast.
You'd have learned the bridge. You wouldn't have learned to distrust your own map." Daba pushed
himself up off the boulder, the motion of a man for whom standing and moving were the same
decision made once. "There'll be a hundred bridges in your life you don't know are bridges yet.
I can't walk you across every one of them in advance. I can only teach you to ask, every time
something looks too settled to matter: settled for whom, and since when."

They walked back toward the camp in the dark without speaking further, which was, between them,
its own kind of conversation — the particular silence of two people who had already said the part
that mattered and saw no reason to spend more words confirming it had landed. Somewhere behind
them, in a satchel at Kanja's own hip, sat an iron file and a half-finished hinge he'd been shaping
in whatever hours the mentorship left him, the trade running the other direction: tomorrow, or the
day after, he would put that file back in Daba's hands and start teaching him the difference
between iron that wanted to be a blade and iron that only wanted to be a tool, and neither of them
would call it a debt, because neither of them was counting.

He carried the failed exercise home the way he carried most things — without complaint, and
without setting it down. He would not fortify a road again without first asking what habit was
standing where a road should be. He did not know yet that this was the same question that would,
years and a war from now, put an entire Sovereign Trust column waist-deep in a flooded canal
before it understood the ground had ever been a weapon.

He only knew, that night, that he had lost cleanly, and that losing cleanly to someone worth
losing to was its own kind of forge — the kind that didn't leave a mark you could see, only one
you could use.
