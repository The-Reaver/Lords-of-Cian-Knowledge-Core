The lesson that night had not gone well, and Daba had not pretended otherwise.

They were on the high bank above a drainage cut two miles inland from the coast road, a stretch
of ground Daba had chosen because it looked, from a distance, like nothing worth defending — low
scrub, a collapsed footbridge, a scatter of boulders too irregular to be called a wall. Kanja had
been given a simple problem three hours earlier: hold the cut against six of Daba's people
approaching from any direction they chose, using only the ground itself.

He had lost. Not decisively — two of the six had gone down to a rockslide he'd rigged along the
western slope before they reached him — but the other four had come through the collapsed
footbridge in a formation he hadn't accounted for, and by the time he understood what had
happened, three practice blades were resting against three different parts of him at once.

"You held the ground," Daba said, when it was over and the six had melted back into the scrub the
way they always did, without ceremony, as if the exercise had simply stopped existing. "You didn't
hold the approach."

"I counted four ways in. There were five."

"There were five ways in and one way through. You fortified the ways in." Daba crouched beside
the collapsed footbridge, running a hand along the underside of a beam that had not, in fact,
collapsed on its own — Kanja could see the cut marks now, old ones, weathered enough to have
happened years before either of them stood here. "Down longer than you've lived. Everyone goes
around it. How many did you expect to cross it?"

"None. It's not a way in. It's an obstacle."

"It's a habit." Daba straightened, brushing dust from his knees with the unhurried economy of a
man who had done this exact motion in far worse places than a training exercise. "Maps show roads.
You fortified four. Nobody uses the bridge. Nobody watches it. Including you."

Kanja said nothing for a while. He was still holding the rope he'd used to rig the rockslide,
coiling it without any particular attention to the coiling, the way a person's hands sometimes
keep working while the rest of them is somewhere else entirely.

"At the forge," he said eventually, "there's the fourth-heat rule. Never trust a piece that's
failed only three times. The fourth tells the truth."

"That's the same lesson."

"I know. I kept the rule for metal. Forgot it covers ground."

Daba sat down on one of the irregular boulders. Sitting was sometimes the fastest way to tell a boy
he wasn't in trouble. "Rex and Mar lack one thing in
them, Kanja."

"What."

"Shame at being wrong in front of somebody. House-trained fighters flinch there. You went straight
to the forge." He was quiet a moment, watching the dark shape of the useless bridge. "That's worth
something. You skip a step everyone else pays for."

"Is that a compliment?"

"An observation. Maybe a warning. Never sitting with your own wrong can mean never sitting with
another's. We'll see."

Kanja considered this the way he considered most things Daba said to him — turning it over slowly,
looking for the load-bearing part. "You told me the bridge was a habit. The rest you left to
me."

"Told the six where to cross. When. How fast. That teaches one bridge. You need to distrust the
map." Daba pushed himself up off the boulder, the motion of a man for whom standing and moving were
the same decision made once. "There'll be a hundred more bridges. Anything too settled to matter,
ask: settled for whom, and since when."

They walked back toward the camp in the dark without speaking further, which was, between them,
its own kind of conversation — the particular silence of two people who had already said the part
that mattered and saw no reason to spend more words confirming it had landed. Somewhere behind
them, in a satchel at Kanja's own hip, sat an iron file and a half-finished hinge he'd been shaping
in whatever hours the mentorship left him. The trade ran the other direction too: at the forge,
Kanja put the file in Daba's hands and taught him the difference between iron that wanted to be a
blade and iron that only wanted to be a tool, and neither of them called it a debt, because neither
of them was counting.

He carried the failed exercise home the way he carried most things — without complaint, and
without setting it down. He meant never again to fortify a road without first asking what habit
was standing where a road should be.

He knew, that night, that he had lost cleanly, and that losing cleanly to someone worth
losing to was its own kind of forge — the kind that didn't leave a mark you could see, only one
you could use.
