# The Distance Between Two Evenings

He gave me this one last, out of order, on an evening near the very end of the long stretch of nights he
spent telling me who he had been before I knew him. I have chosen to close the account here, at his own
choosing, because he told me himself that this was where he would have ended it, had the telling been his
to arrange rather than mine.

He went back to the quarry below Aldenmoor once, he said, years after the nights he'd spent there as a
boy learning the floor of what he was rather than its ceiling. He did not go to practice. He told me that
was the whole point of the visit, in fact — that he went simply to stand at the edge of the black water
again, older now, and see what it felt like to look at the place without needing anything from it.

It was late in the day when he arrived, the light going long and gold across the broken stone rim the way
it never quite had on the cold winter nights he remembered best, and he told me he sat for a long while
on the same flat rock he'd once used to steady himself before stepping onto the ice, and simply let the
years catch up with him in whatever order they chose to arrive.

He thought first, he said, of Draconis — of a loyalty built years earlier on a misunderstanding he had
never once corrected, and had come, by then, to think of not as a debt owed but as a gift he was still
learning how to deserve. He thought of his father, whose patience he had once assumed he would eventually
have to choose against his mother's discipline, and of his mother herself, whose long, hard-won stillness
he had carried into a granary doorway on an autumn evening and found, to his own quiet surprise, that it
worked exactly as well in his hands as it ever had in hers. He thought of Osric, older now than he had
been in any of the stories that came before, and of Cobb, still laughing at nearly everything, and of a
boy named Joren whose face had gone soft at the edges with time the way memory does to the things it can
no longer verify, and of a second boy at a milling floor whose name he had let go of on purpose, because
some lessons are worth more than the details that taught them. He thought of Berrin Hollis's grudging
respect, and Annis Fairweather's ordinary laughter, and Alric Fenmoor's refusal to flatter him, and
Oswin Kade's plain, unglamorous account of what a household owes the family it serves — the whole wide,
unglamorous constellation of people who had shaped him without ever once being asked to, and without
ever fully understanding what, exactly, they were shaping.

He told me he did the arithmetic again that evening, the same arithmetic that had frightened him once,
and found it had not gotten any smaller in the years between, and had somehow stopped frightening him all
the same — that a man could hold the knowledge of every year he would likely outlive the people he loved
most and still choose, every single day, to love them at full measure anyway, and that the choosing had,
by then, become so practiced it barely felt like courage anymore. It simply felt like the way he'd decided
to live.

What he told me, sitting there, was that he had come to understand something about the whole shape of
those years that no single story inside them could have taught him on its own — that a life is not
actually made of its largest moments, the floods and the reckonings and the names given and withheld. It
is made, almost entirely, of the small ones: a race let go of on purpose, a silence held at a threshold
until it was no longer needed, a discipline handed from one set of hands to another and found, each time,
to hold. He said he did not know, sitting on that rock in the long gold light, what was still ahead of
him. He told me plainly that he did not spend that evening wondering. The evening was not for that. It
was for the simple, complete pleasure of looking backward across a distance he had actually traveled, and
finding, when he looked, that he recognized the man who had covered it.

He stood, eventually, and walked back up from the black water the way he had walked away from it a
hundred times before, unhurried, and told me that was the last time he ever went to that quarry for any
reason at all — not because the place had stopped meaning anything to him, but because he no longer
needed a room built only for himself to know the shape of who he was. He had found, somewhere in all the
evenings between the first night on the ice and this last quiet one beside it, that he could carry that
knowledge anywhere. He had built it, finally, all the way into himself.

That is where he left it, the night he told me. It is where I will leave it too.
