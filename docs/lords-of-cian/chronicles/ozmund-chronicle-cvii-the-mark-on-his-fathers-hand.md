# The Mark on His Father's Hand

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1836`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CVII, second entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave (part A), set strictly
pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. A private realization about his own
body and power that young Ozmund never spoke of to anyone until this telling — noticing, then finally
understanding, the true origin of a small permanent mark on his father's hand, evidence that the
Density Spike was already active and beyond his control in the earliest days of his infancy
(`MCD-024`/`CC-015`). Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. No new named
characters.*

---

There is a particular kind of story a man carries for years without ever quite deciding whether it is
a wound or simply a fact, and the General gave me this one late, after a great many easier ones, the
way you finally hand someone the thing you've been carrying only once you trust them not to drop it. I
have thought about it more than almost anything else he told me across our whole friendship, because it
is, of everything in this account, the story that most plainly shows what it must have cost him — and
everyone around him — simply to exist as what he was, from the very first days of his life, before he
had done anything at all to deserve either the burden or the fear of it.

He told me he noticed it the ordinary way a boy notices small permanent things about the people he
loves without thinking to ask what they mean — a faint white crook along the smallest finger of his
father's left hand, the bone healed slightly wrong, a mark he must have seen a thousand times across a
childhood of being held by that hand before he ever thought to wonder at it. He asked about it once,
he told me, the way children ask about scars, expecting a story with a horse in it, or a blade, or some
ordinary mishap of a busy man's life. Aethelgard had given him exactly that — an old mishap, nothing
worth troubling over, some door or gate or piece of ill luck long since forgotten — and the boy had
believed him completely, because there was no reason yet not to.

He told me the true understanding came years later, and came sideways, the way the truest things
sometimes do. He was watching, from some distance across a courtyard, a household woman settle a
newborn into the crook of her own arm for the first time, and saw the child's whole fist close blind
and reflexive around one of her fingers the instant it found something to hold — the ordinary, fierce,
mindless grip of an infant who does not yet know it is gripping anything at all, only that something is
there and must be held onto. He told me he had seen that grip a hundred times before and thought
nothing of it. What stopped him that day was a single, unbidden thought arriving whole and certain, the
way a fact arrives once you are finally standing close enough to see it plainly: that his own hand, at
that same age, had closed around his own father's finger in that same blind and mindless way, before
he had ever had a single conscious hour of control over what his blood already was — and that the
crook in Aethelgard's smallest finger was not the shape of an old mishap with a door. It was the exact
shape of a newborn's grip that had pressed, without meaning to, without any possibility of meaning
anything at all, harder than any newborn's grip should have been able to press.

He told me he never asked his father to confirm it. He did not need to. He said he understood, standing
in that courtyard with a stranger's infant gripping a stranger's hand twenty feet away from him, that
his father had carried that mark for two decades and more, through every year of teaching him to walk
soft-footed through a house full of breakable things, through every lesson in stillness and restraint
and the quiet arithmetic of what he was and was not permitted to touch carelessly — and had never once,
not a single time in all those years, let his son see it as anything but an old mishap with a door. He
told me that was the part that undid him a little, thinking it through afterward: not that his hand had
been capable of marking his own father before he could speak or walk or understand what he was, which
frightened him in its own quiet way, but that his father had looked at proof of exactly the danger
everyone around him would spend a lifetime being careful of, and had chosen, deliberately and without
complaint, to carry it as a private thing rather than a grievance. He said he never told anyone else
what he'd worked out that afternoon — not Osric, not Draconis once Draconis was old enough to be told
anything at all, not even his mother, who of everyone might have understood it best. He told it to me,
once, decades later, and asked me, when he was finished, not to make more of it in the telling than it
had been in the living. I have tried to honor that. But I do not think a man forgets, easily, the exact
moment he first understood that his own hands had already done something irreversible to the person who
loved him most, before he had ever had the chance to choose otherwise.
