# Where the Captives Went

Maret Vos hadn't thought about the slaver vessel's manifest in years — freed captives folded into
crews and settlements up and down the coast until the numbers blurred into the general accounting of a
war that had rescued more people than any single ledger could track — until a letter reached *The
Audit* addressed, improbably, to "whoever remembers the ship with the manifest written in chains."

It came from a coastal settlement neither Maret Vos nor anyone else aboard had visited since the night
of the liberation itself. Half the letter's signatories, it turned out, had been among the captives
pulled from that hold.

"They built a school," Maret Vos said, reading it aloud on deck, something unsteady in his voice that
hadn't been there since his own liberation years before. "Twenty-two children. Half of them born free
because their parents weren't in chains long enough to lose everything that mattered before someone
cut the chains."

Kanja said nothing, letting him finish.

"There's a name on here I remember. Girl who couldn't have been more than nine when we pulled her out
of that hold, shaking so hard she couldn't hold a cup of water without spilling it." Maret Vos's finger
stopped on a line near the bottom. "She's teaching now. Reading and figures, to children who'll never
know what a manifest written in chains actually looked like, because she's making sure they never have
to find out."

They sailed for the settlement themselves, a detour that cost three days neither of them regretted,
and found it exactly as described — modest, thriving in the specific way a community builds when it's
had to build everything from nothing, the school itself a converted warehouse with the settlement's
own name for it painted crudely but proudly above the door.

The young woman Maret Vos remembered met them without ceremony, recognizing neither of their faces
specifically but understanding immediately, from the ship anchored offshore, exactly who had come to
visit.

"I don't remember much from that night," she admitted. "I remember being afraid, and then I remember
not being afraid anymore, and I've spent every year since trying to build something that means these
children never have a night like the one I don't remember clearly enough to describe."

"You don't need to remember it clearly," Maret Vos told her, and meant something specific by it,
something only another freed captive would fully understand. "You just need to keep doing exactly
what you're doing. That's the whole point of what we did that night. Not the rescue. What came after
it."
