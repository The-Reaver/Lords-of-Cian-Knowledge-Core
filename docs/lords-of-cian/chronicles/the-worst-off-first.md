# The Worst-Off First

*Locked canon, Batch 108, 2026-09-10 (`MCD-371`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle I. Rebellion

---

The dye-works district had heard about the Furnace District by the time Kanja's crew reached its
gates, and the foremen had prepared for it the way foremen prepare for anything they've decided is
theater: they lined up their best-paid, best-treated workers at the front of the yard, ready to
answer the question everyone assumed was coming, expecting the visiting crew to hear a handful of
satisfied answers and move on.

Kanja walked past all of them without asking a single one anything.

He went instead to the drying sheds at the yard's far edge, where the dye-vat handlers worked —
the ones whose hands had gone permanently the color of whatever batch they'd mixed that week, the
ones nobody had thought to clean up or line up for anyone's benefit, because nobody visiting a
dye-works ever asked to see them. He crouched by the first one he found, an old man with hands
stained a blue so deep it looked like it had grown into the bone, and asked the only question he'd
come to ask.

"How much do they owe you?"

The old man looked at him for a long moment, the way a person looks at someone offering something
they've stopped believing exists. "Nobody's ever asked me that question first," he said. "Only ever
the ones out front."

"That's why I'm starting with you instead."

It took most of the afternoon. Kanja worked backward from the drying sheds — the handlers, then the
mixers, then the loaders, then finally the well-paid front-gate workers the foremen had staged, each
answer written down in order, unedited, uncorrected, in a ledger that would not distinguish a
dye-vat handler's decades of unpaid overtime from a foreman's favorite worker's comparatively minor
grievance. By the time he reached the front of the yard the foremen had prepared for, the story had
already changed shape without their permission — every worker in the yard had heard, by then, that
the visiting crew had gone to the worst-treated first, on purpose, before anyone had told them to.

"You could have started with us," one of the front-gate workers said, uneasy, watching the ledger
fill with entries that had nothing to do with him. "We would have given you a cleaner number. Faster."

"I didn't come for a clean number." Kanja closed the ledger over the day's tally. "I came for the
real one. The dye-vat handlers have been telling the truth about this yard for years and nobody's
written it down where it couldn't be argued with. Yours is easy to find. Theirs is the one that's
been sitting unrecorded the whole time. I go where the truth is hardest to get to, not where it's
easiest to hear."

The foremen never got the theater they'd prepared for. What they got instead, when the full ledger
reached the district administrators three days later, was a documented account that started with
the people they'd been most confident no one would ever ask, and worked its way, unbroken and
undeniable, all the way to the front gate.
