# What Annis Kept Beyond the Mews

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1830`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle CI, Wider Verehimu Household/Guard strand, wave 2. Set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony
(`MCD-025`), spanning Ozmund's boyhood and adolescence in reflection — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund still
House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, a second conversation
with falconer Annis Fairweather, this time about her own life beyond the House Verehimu mews. A
generational/legacy register: what she carried into that mews before Ozmund ever entered it, and why
a goshawk's fear read to her the way it did. No new named characters.*

---

Annis Fairweather told me, the second time I came to her, that she'd wondered after our first
conversation whether I would come back and ask the obvious question she'd left unanswered — not what
the boy had been like, which she'd already given me in full, but why a falconer had cared so much,
watching him, about what an animal's fear looked like from the outside. She said she'd been waiting for
someone to ask it for the better part of sixty years, and that she was almost relieved when I finally
did.

She had married young, she told me, to a huntsman two houses down from her own family's modest holding,
and had a son by him before either of them were much past twenty — a boy who, by every account she gave
me, had wanted nothing in the world so badly as to serve in a House Guard, any House Guard, the way boys
of that station sometimes fix on a single ambition early and never once waver from it. He had trained
for it from the time he could hold a practice blade. He had died of a fever before he ever reached the
age a House would take him, not yet fourteen, in the space of eleven days that Annis said she still
counted, some years, without quite meaning to.

She said she'd left falconry for a long while after that, unable to stand the sight of anything small
and frightened in her care, and had only come back to it — to House Verehimu's mews specifically —
because an old friend of her husband's had offered her the position when she had nowhere else obvious to
go and no particular will left to look for one. She hadn't expected to find any comfort in it. What she
found instead, she told me, was that a hawk's fear was the one kind of fear she could actually do
something about — patient, careful, entirely within her hands to answer, in a way her son's fever never
had been.

That was why, she said, she'd watched the boy heir so closely those mornings in the mews, more closely
than she'd ever let on to him. Not because she saw anything remarkable in a child sitting quietly with a
difficult bird, but because she recognized, in his particular stillness around a frightened animal,
something of the same instinct that had kept her coming back to that work herself — the specific,
unglamorous patience of someone who has decided that a creature's fear is worth taking seriously rather
than simply overriding. She never told him any of this while he was a boy. She told me she'd decided,
watching him laugh in that pile of overturned creels, that whatever he carried in him — and she said
plainly she had never known, not truly, what that was — it hadn't come from being told his fear didn't
matter. It had come from somewhere that had let him feel it and still be gentle with something smaller
than himself.

She said she thought of her son sometimes, still, when she thought of that mews, and that she had come
to a kind of understanding, late in her own life, that the two boys had nothing to do with one another
except in the shape of what she'd given each of them without either one asking for it — patience,
mostly, and the plain refusal to treat a frightened thing as something to be ashamed of. Her own boy
had never lived long enough to need that lesson tested against anything larger than a green horse or a
half-broke hawk. She said she liked to think, without any way of knowing it for certain, that some part
of what she'd taught a Verehimu heir in a mews thirty years later had traveled further than anything she
had ever managed to teach her own son in the short time she'd had him.

I asked her, at the end, whether she wished she had told Ozmund any of it while he was still a boy in
her care. She said no, without hesitation. She said a child that age didn't need to carry an old
woman's grief alongside his own quiet burdens, whatever they were. It had been enough, she said, simply
to give him the mews, and the birds, and an hour, now and then, of being allowed to be gentle without
anyone asking him why.
