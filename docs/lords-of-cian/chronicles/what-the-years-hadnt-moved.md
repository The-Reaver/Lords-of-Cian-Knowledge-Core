# What the Years Hadn't Moved

*Locked canon, Batch 233, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1037`). The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LX, wave 20,
closing the wave. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

He hadn't planned to go back down. The supply route through Killane only ever touched the surface
streets, and there was no operational reason to take the old stair into the tunnels he'd shored during
the siege, years behind him now. He went anyway, alone, after the caravan made camp for the night.

The western gallery was exactly where he remembered it and nothing like he remembered it. The timber
braces he'd set with a conscripted sixty-one-year-old engineer looking on had been replaced twice over
by crews who'd never met either of them, following specifications that had drifted a full generation
from what he'd actually built — a joint reinforced here where his original read hadn't called for it, a
support left thinner there than he'd have allowed, decisions made by people solving problems he'd never
had to solve because his own work had held long enough that theirs never needed to match it exactly.

He ran a hand along a reinforced seam near the old surface breach — the one place the tunnel still bore
a trace of the extraction crew's desperate forty minutes, a discoloration in the stone nobody had ever
bothered explaining to a new generation of diggers. Two decades of ordinary use, ordinary maintenance,
ordinary failures elsewhere in the network that this particular gallery had simply never contributed
to. Not because it was special. Because it had been read correctly once, by people who'd had every
reason to get it wrong in a hurry and hadn't.

A dredge-crew foreman found him there an hour later, unbothered by a stranger in the old tunnels at
that hour — Killane's tunnels had long since stopped being anyone's secret. "You're not local," she
said, more observation than challenge. "This gallery's the good one. Whoever built it the first time
knew what they were doing. Everything since has just been trying not to be the one who finally breaks
it."

"High compliment for something built in a hurry, under fire, by people who'd never worked together
before."

"Higher compliment than that, honestly. Most rushed work shows it eventually. This didn't." She studied
him a moment longer than the dim light strictly explained. "You know something about it?"

"I knew the man who read it the first time." It wasn't a lie, and it wasn't the whole truth either, and
both of those felt like the correct amount to give a stranger in a tunnel at night. "He'd tell you the
gallery isn't good because nothing's gone wrong with it. He'd tell you it's good because when something
did go wrong — twice, that he knew of — the read held anyway. That's a different kind of good than
lucky."

She didn't ask how he knew that, and he didn't offer more. He left before dawn, the way he'd arrived,
having confirmed nothing he needed for the caravan and everything he hadn't known he still wanted to
know — that a piece of work done once, honestly, under conditions that forgave nothing, could keep
holding for people who'd never have reason to thank the hands that first read it right.
