# The Yard Hollis Would Leave Behind

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1832`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle CIII, Wider Verehimu Household/Guard strand, wave 2. Set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony
(`MCD-025`), in Ozmund's later adolescence — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund still House heir. Narrated by
Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, a second visit to Armsmaster Berrin Hollis. A
generational/legacy register, deliberately mirroring Chronicle XCIX's Sergeant Kade entry: Hollis
training the man who will inherit his yard, and discovering in himself the same doubt he once carried
about Ozmund. No new named characters.*

---

Berrin Hollis laughed at me, the second time I asked him about the General, and said I was fishing for
the same story twice dressed in different clothes. I told him I wasn't — that I already had the story
of the boy who'd proven himself to him. What I wanted this time was the story of the man Hollis had
proven the same lesson to, years later, from the other side of it. He went quiet for a moment, the way
men his age sometimes do when a question lands somewhere they hadn't expected, and then he told me,
with something closer to reluctance than pride, that he supposed he did have that one after all.

He had taken on an assistant instructor in his later years on the yard, a decision Aethelgard's own
steward had pressed on him more than once as his hip worsened and the drills grew harder to run alone.
Hollis said he'd resisted it longer than he probably should have, out of the same stubbornness that had
kept him running that yard past the age most armsmasters retired, and had finally accepted a young man
recommended to him by another House's own training master — solid technique, disciplined, nothing
obviously wrong with him at all.

And that, Hollis told me, was precisely the trouble. He said he'd watched the man run drills for the
better part of two months and found himself doubting him for reasons he couldn't fully name — a
guardedness in the way the man held himself, a reluctance to correct a student too firmly, that Hollis
read, at first, as softness. He said he'd nearly gone back to the steward to say the arrangement wasn't
working, and had stopped himself only because something about his own certainty felt uncomfortably
familiar.

It took him the better part of a week of sitting with that discomfort, he said, before he placed it —
that he was doing to this man exactly what he'd once done to the General as a boy, reading caution as
weakness because he hadn't yet bothered to ask what the caution was actually protecting. So he asked. The
assistant told him, plainly, that he'd trained under an instructor years before who had broken a
student's wrist through carelessness during a drill gone wrong, and that he had never since been able to
correct a student at full force without first being certain, entirely certain, that the correction
wouldn't cost the boy something permanent. It wasn't softness. It was a man who had watched what
recklessness dressed as rigor could actually do, and had built his whole caution around never doing it
himself.

Hollis told me he'd felt something close to shame, hearing that, remembering how many months he'd spent
suspecting a boy of coasting on his name before he'd bothered to ask what was actually behind the boy's
own guardedness. He said he hadn't told the assistant any of that directly — some things, he told me, a
man works out better on his own time than being handed the comparison outright — but he had changed how
he trained him from that day forward, teaching him to hold the caution and still commit fully when the
drill called for it, the same lesson, in reverse, that he'd once taught a boy who'd been holding back
for entirely different reasons.

He told me the man runs the yard alone now, most days, while Hollis sits and watches with his bad hip
propped on the same overturned crate he'd used for thirty years, and that he still catches himself,
now and then, doubting a student's caution before he's asked what it's protecting. He said he supposed
that particular lesson was one he was destined to keep learning the same way, over and over, every time
a new face arrived on his yard looking like something he thought he already understood.

I asked him whether the General knew any of this — that the doubt Hollis had once carried about him had
gone on to shape how he trained every student after. Hollis said he doubted it. He said some debts get
paid forward rather than back, quietly, to people who never know they were the reason the payment was
made at all.
