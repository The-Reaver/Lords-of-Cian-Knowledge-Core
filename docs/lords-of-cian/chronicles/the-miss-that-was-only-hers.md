# The Miss That Was Only Hers

*Locked canon, Batch 285, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1505`). Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle XCIV, wave 32, first entry in the wave. Months after the fourth-generation apprentice was ceded full forecasting authority (`MCD-1419`), she makes her first genuine independent miscalculation while holding it alone -- an honest miss, not sabotage or a factual trap, testing what the authority actually costs now that no one stands behind her to check it.*

---

Nothing about the reading had felt wrong. That was the part she kept returning to, days after: not one gut-catch, not one hesitation she'd talked herself past. She had read the pressure the way she'd been taught to read it, cross-checked it against the horizon color the way she'd been taught, and called a six-hour window of building weather that would close the strait to any prudent captain.

The weather never came. The system broke apart against a ridge of high pressure nobody's tradition had a name for, and the forty-vessel convoy she'd held at anchor for a day and a half sailed two full days late into a market that had already cleared its urgent orders to someone else's hulls.

No one died. No ship was lost. It was, by any measure that mattered to the ledger, a small miss -- smaller than the storm that never came in `MCD-1346`, smaller by a wide margin than the loss at `MCD-503`. But it was the first one that belonged entirely to her, with no student standing at the chart's edge ready to take it back, no successor's steadying hand on a call that had already been made and only needed confirming. Just her name, alone, against a window that hadn't opened.

She wrote it into the ledger herself, in full, the way the tradition demanded -- the pressure figures, the horizon reading, the ridge she hadn't accounted for, the two days it had cost forty captains who'd trusted her word over their own judgment. She did not soften the language. She did not, as she later admitted to the student, feel the honesty as generous the way she'd always assumed it would feel from this side of it.

The student read it standing beside her, said nothing for a long moment, and then turned to the next blank line in the same volume rather than to her.

"You're not going to check it," the apprentice said. Not a question this time either, though it landed harder than the same words had landed from the other side of the table, wave after wave ago.

"There's nothing to check," the student said. "You read what was there. The ridge wasn't in anyone's book yet. It's in this one now." She tapped the fresh entry. "That's the whole of what changes when it's yours -- not that you stop being wrong. That you're the one who has to write it down."

Kanja, who had said little through the whole exchange, was the one who finally spoke to the part neither of them had. "Sephtis missed by two hours once," he said, "on a call with a fleet's whole night riding on it. He didn't stop reading the sky the next morning. He read it better." He looked at the young woman who'd held the coast's whole weather in her hands for six months now, wrong for the first time on her own. "You'll read this strait again in a month. You'll read it right. That's not forgiveness. That's just what happens next."

She nodded, closed the ledger on her own handwriting, and did exactly that -- three weeks later, on a system with a ridge she recognized this time before it had fully formed, and said nothing about it to anyone but the page.
