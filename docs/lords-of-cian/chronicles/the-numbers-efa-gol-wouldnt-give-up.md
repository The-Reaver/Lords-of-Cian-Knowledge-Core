# The Numbers Efa Gol Wouldn't Give Up

*Locked canon, Batch 264, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1373`). Captain Alias Chronicle LXXIII, wave 25. Efa Gol's own aging becomes the crew's concern for the first time; she refuses to slow down.*

---

It was Callum Breck, of all people, who noticed first — the same quiet attentiveness that had once caught a jealousy nobody else would name now caught something smaller and harder to raise: Efa Gol's hands taking a half-second longer over the ledger than they used to, a squint at figures she'd once read at a glance, a stiffness in the cold mornings she covered by moving faster rather than admitting to it. He said nothing to her directly. He said it to Kanja instead, the way he'd learned mattered — flag the thing quietly, let the person it concerned decide how to answer it.

Kanja raised it with her carefully, choosing his moment the way Sera had once chosen hers with Hask. Efa Gol didn't deny any of it.

"I know what my hands are doing," she said, not looking up from the count she was mid-way through. "I've known for two years. I'm not going to pretend otherwise to spare anyone a difficult conversation."

"Nobody's asking you to stop. We're asking if there's a way to slow down that doesn't cost you the thing that makes you *you*."

"The thing that makes me me is that the numbers are right. Not that I'm the one who gets there fastest." She set the pen down, finally, and looked at him directly. "You want to know why I've never once let this crew's ledger carry an error it didn't have to carry? Because after the Black Trench, counting was the only thing I had that grief couldn't touch. It didn't care that Tam wasn't coming back. It just needed to be right. I'm not giving that up because my hands are slower than they used to be."

What she agreed to, eventually, wasn't retirement. It was a second set of hands — the same deckhand Kanja had once taught to read, now grown and steady, brought in not to replace her count but to check it, quietly, the way she'd never needed checking before and now, occasionally, did.

"Two sets of hands catch what one pair misses," she admitted, the closest thing to acceptance anyone got from her that year. "I can live with being caught. I can't live with being wrong."
