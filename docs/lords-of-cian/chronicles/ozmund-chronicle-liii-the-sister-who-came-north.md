# The Sister Who Came North

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1782`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series, Chronicle LIII, Draconis strand wave 3.*

---

Of everything the General told me about the winter of the flooded bank house, the part he seemed to treasure most in the retelling was that it hadn't ended there. Years later, Halyn came north herself, and for once in the whole of these stories, the General told me, he was allowed simply to watch a happiness he hadn't had to arrange.

It took some persuading, by his account — a river woman with two children and a rebuilt house doesn't leave a working farm on a whim, and Draconis had apparently asked her north more than once over the years without success, always answered with some practical reason the crossing wasn't worth the trouble that season. What finally moved her, the General said, wasn't an invitation at all but a wedding two counties over, a cousin's daughter she couldn't in good conscience miss, close enough to the Verehimu seat that Draconis simply refused to let her pass within three days' ride without stopping. He arranged the leave himself, quietly, and for once spent it exactly the way every other man in that guardhouse assumed he ought to have been spending his leave all along — on nothing that concerned the household at all.

The General told me he met her only briefly, and by design rather than accident. Draconis had never once, in all the years the General had known him, brought his private life anywhere near the seat itself, and the General understood without being told that this was a boundary worth respecting rather than crossing simply because he could have arranged an introduction. He was presented to her, when the moment came, as a young officer of the household Draconis served with — no more than that, no title pressed forward, nothing in the meeting that would have told her the man shaking her hand outranked every soldier her brother had ever answered to. Halyn, for her part, treated him exactly the way the General said she treated everyone that week: with the unhurried, unimpressed warmth of a woman who had raised two children through a flood and a dead husband and had long since stopped being anxious about which men in a room mattered more than others.

What struck him, watching from the modest distance he kept for the two days she stayed near the seat, was how little of Draconis's guardhouse composure survived contact with her. The General told me he had never, before that week, seen the man laugh without first checking the room for who might be watching him do it. Halyn didn't allow it. She teased him, openly and without mercy, about a scar on his forearm he'd apparently claimed for years came from a training mishap and which she informed the General, cheerfully and at some length, had actually come from a fishing hook their own father had mishandled when Draconis was nine and too proud to admit whose fault the wound truly was. Draconis endured this the way the General said he endured very little else in his life — without argument, without correction, with the specific defeated fondness of a man who has long since given up defending a version of himself his family never believed in the first place.

The children were shyer, at first, of a household so much larger and stranger than any they'd known, and the General told me it was Draconis, not Halyn, who spent the better part of an afternoon coaxing the younger of the two — a boy of perhaps six, all elbows and caution — into the guardhouse yard to be shown, with enormous ceremony, exactly how a blunted practice blade was properly held. The General watched the whole of it from the gallery above, close enough to hear but careful to stay unseen, and told me he understood, watching Draconis kneel in the dust correcting a six-year-old's grip with the same patient exactness he brought to drilling grown soldiers, that he was looking at the version of the man that had existed before any of it — before the road, before the oath he'd made of himself, before a household that had never once asked what it cost him to belong to it so completely.

They left after two days, bound for the wedding and then home again to the river, and the General told me Draconis walked them to the gate himself and stood there long after the wagon had gone from sight, in a stillness the General had learned, over the years, meant the man was somewhere he couldn't be followed to. He didn't intrude on it. He told me he simply stood at a distance and let Draconis have the whole of that quiet, and thought, watching him, that whatever debt he still privately believed he owed the man for every knife and seal and beam across the years, none of it weighed anything at all against the plain, uncomplicated gladness of having, for two days, done nothing more than let him be somebody's brother.
