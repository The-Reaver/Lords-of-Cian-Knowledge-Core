# The Chain That Held Two Ships Together

---

Grappled hull to hull, the rebel vessel and the Trust patrol ship shared three separate iron
boarding chains, two thrown by the enemy crew to hold the rebel ship in place for their own boarders,
one thrown by the rebels themselves to keep the patrol ship from disengaging before the fight was
decided. All three ran taut between the same two decks. Severing the wrong one meant losing the
engagement's own anchor rather than the enemy's grip.

Kanja read all three before touching any of them.

The distinction wasn't in the metal — all three chains carried the same alloy, the same rough
tension — but in what each was actually doing at that specific moment, a diagnostic problem no prior
naval engagement had presented him. He traced the load transfer through each link in turn: which
chains were currently bearing the boarders' weight as they crossed, which was simply holding position
against the current.

"Their two, not ours," he said, the resonance already building toward a broadcast tuned specifically
to the enemy chains' current load signature rather than their base alloy. "Ours isn't carrying
anyone's weight right now. Theirs both are."

The two enemy chains parted within seconds of each other, boarders mid-crossing losing their footing
into open water between the hulls, while the rebellion's own anchor chain held the patrol ship fast
exactly as intended. Mafesto caught the handful of boarders who'd already reached the rebel deck
before the chains failed, Obsidian Malice's discharge finishing what the fall into open water hadn't
already settled.

"You read three identical chains and only touched two," the rebel captain said afterward, watching
the patrol ship's remaining crew realize their own boarding party was gone and their retreat cut off
by the one chain still holding. "I couldn't have told you which was which without cutting all three
and hoping."

"They looked the same. They weren't doing the same thing. That's true of almost every structure I've
ever read — the metal tells you what it's made of. It doesn't tell you what it's for. I had to ask
that question separately, every time, or I'd have cut us loose right alongside them."
