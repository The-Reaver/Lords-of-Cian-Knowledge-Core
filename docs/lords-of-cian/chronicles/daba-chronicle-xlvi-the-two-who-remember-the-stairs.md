The boot needed resoling and Daba was bad at it, which was one of perhaps four things in the world he
was openly, cheerfully bad at, and Mika had long ago stopped offering to do it for him because watching
him fail at something small and harmless was, she said, good for both of them. She sat across the low
table doing her own share of the mending, and for a while neither of them said anything worth writing
down, which was its own kind of comfortable.

"You still call me that," she said eventually, not looking up from the seam she was closing. "In your
head. I can tell. You get a particular look."

"Call you what."

"The girl from the cistern."

He set the awl down. It was true, and he had never decided whether it was a fault in him or simply the
truth of how a person gets fixed at the moment they first became real to you. "You were six," he said.
"You had your arms around my neck tight enough to leave a mark. I don't think a man forgets the exact
weight of the person he happened to keep."

"I was six for one night," she said. "I've been a great many other things since." She held up the boot,
inspected the stitch, set it down satisfied. "I'm not arguing you keep the picture. I'm asking what I
am to you now. Not what I was. What I am. Because 'the girl from the cistern' isn't a job, and I've had
a job in this cell for longer than some of our people have been alive."

It was, he thought, a fair question, and fair questions deserved better than the answer he usually
gave people, which was silence dressed up as thoughtfulness. He turned it over honestly instead. "My
second," he said. "The keeper of whatever we're allowed to remember out loud. The only person who can
walk into a room I'm in and know, without me saying it, whether I'm having a good day or a bad one." He
paused. "Family, if the word still means anything after everyone who first taught it to either of us
is gone."

"Odalys would have liked that," Mika said. "The last part."

"Odalys would have wanted it in the ledger."

"Odalys," Mika said, with the particular fondness she reserved for the dead, "wanted everything in the
ledger. It was the whole of her religion. Names, so nobody goes in as a number." She nodded at the small
locked box on the shelf behind her, which held nothing so foolish as an actual roster -- no names, no
faces, nothing a raid could turn into a casualty list -- only her own private, coded accounting of who
had come through the cell alive and who hadn't, kept in a shorthand only she could read, precisely
because a real ledger was the one thing 1804 could never afford to keep. "I do the same thing she did.
Just backwards. I write down enough to remember. Not enough to be found."

"That's not backwards," Daba said. "That's the same religion, adjusted for the world we actually live
in."

She smiled at that, the small private smile she almost never let anyone else see, and went back to her
stitching, and he went back to failing at his, and the evening continued being ordinary in the specific
way that ordinary evenings had become, for both of them, a thing they no longer took for granted. Odalys
had died an old woman, in her own bed, of nothing more dramatic than a winter that had finally caught
up with her, the physical ledger boxed and burned at her own explicit instruction rather than left for
anyone to find. Mika had kept the instruction. She had not kept the fear that had made it necessary,
or had kept it differently -- worn in, like a boot broken to the shape of the foot that wears it,
rather than raw the way it had been the night the tannery filled with names.

"You never answered," she said, after a while. "What you were, before you were my second."

"A boy who called colors in a game," Daba said, "so children wouldn't hear boots on the stairs." He
said it without weight, the way you say a fact you have finally stopped needing to brace yourself
against. "I think I've been trying to keep calling colors ever since. Just with better odds, now, and
worse boots."

Mika laughed -- short, real, the kind of laugh that required no audience but the one already present
-- and threw the resoled boot at his chest, and told him it was his turn to make the tea, and for the
length of that small, unremarkable task, the two people in the room who remembered the actual stairs
were simply two people, sharing a fire, keeping each other's count.
