# Daba Chronicle XXI: What the Hands Cannot Learn

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1591`). Entry 21 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,

The cellar smelled of quench oil and old grain, which was correct, because it had been a grain
cellar before it was anything else, and would go back to being one the day 1804 moved on from this
particular stretch of ground. Daba had chosen it for the same reason he chose everything: two ways
out, no way to see the coal-glow from the road above, and walls thick enough that the ring of a
hammer wouldn't carry past the treeline.

Kanja set the broken knife on the anvil's corner rather than the anvil itself, which Daba noticed
and filed away without comment. The blade was Dead Drakma, trade-quality, snapped clean six inches
from the tip. Someone had died over it, probably, or nearly had -- weapons this far from Jicome
usually changed hands that way.

"I want to be honest with you about something before we start," Kanja said, "because I think you'd
rather hear it now than find it out three months in and feel like I let you waste the time."

Daba waited. He had learned, across a year of these lessons -- the other kind, the kind fought at
night with no forge involved -- that Kanja's honesty announcements were worth sitting still for.

"My hands know this metal because my blood does. Generations of people who never touched a sword
put their palms on raw seams of it in the dark and learned, over centuries, what it was telling
them. I didn't earn that. I was born already knowing it, the way you were born knowing how to read
a slope for cover before anyone taught you the word for it." He turned the broken knife over once.
"You don't have that. I can't give it to you. If I tell you otherwise I'm lying to make myself feel
generous."

"Then what am I doing here."

"Learning the other half. The half that isn't blood." Kanja set the knife down and picked up a
strip of scrap iron instead -- nothing precious, nothing that would matter if it went wrong. "Before
there was a bloodline that could feel a flaw in raw stone, there were people who found flaws by
testing. Loading a thing past what it should bear and watching exactly where it gave. That part
isn't inherited. It's just patient."

He heated the strip until it went the color of a low coal, then struck it three times along its
length with a small hammer, pausing after each strike with his head tilted, listening the way a man
listens for a second heartbeat under his own.

"Hear that." Third strike. The ring came back shorter, duller, swallowed half a beat early. "That's
not blood-sense. That's just a sound a sound thing doesn't make. Try it."

Daba took the hammer. His first two strikes told him nothing -- to his ear both rang the same,
clean and long. Kanja said nothing, didn't correct him, let him strike a third time, a fourth. On
the fifth, low along the strip near where Kanja's own third strike had landed, he heard it: not a
different note so much as a note that ended too soon, as though something inside the metal had
already decided not to finish it.

"There," Daba said, and marked the spot with a nub of chalk before he'd fully thought through why
he was certain.

Kanja looked at the mark, then at him, something close to a smile settling into the corner of his
mouth that Daba had only seen a handful of times, usually after a night action had gone exactly to
plan and not a breath better.

"You already know how to find the place a thing will fail under weight it wasn't built to carry,"
Kanja said. "You've been doing it to enemy formations for as long as I've known you. I haven't
taught you anything tonight. I've just shown you it rings the same in steel."

Daba turned the chalk mark toward the coal-light and studied it -- a flaw in dead metal, found the
same way he'd have found the seam in a patrol line -- and understood, distantly, that something had
just been handed to him that no amount of blood could have delivered any faster.
