# The Name They Carved for Him

*Locked canon, Batch 275, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1423`). Captain Alias Chronicle XCIII, wave 31, closing the wave. Garren Hask's memorial at the Pier Nine wall, deliberately mirroring and contrasting the founding-crew reunion of wave 30's second entry.*

---

They gathered at the same long table wave 30 had filled with warmth, and the contrast was the whole point, the way Efa Gol said it plainly before anyone else found the words: *last season we sat here because someone remembered the date. This season we're sitting here because someone can't.*

There was no formal eulogy roster, because there never had been one, not since a founding-era rigger's own quiet sea-burial set the pattern this crew had followed ever since (`MCD-998`). Corren Halst spoke first, brief and steady, about a man who'd kept the ledger honest through wars, surrenders, and every ordinary Tuesday in between, and had never once let anyone — including Kanja — round a figure in his own favor. Callum Breck spoke about the notebook Hask had kept for years before anyone else knew he was keeping it, the one that had first put the mortality question into words the crew could actually argue about (`MCD-920`). The ledger's own keeper, hands steadier than they'd been that morning at the table, read the crew's very first entry aloud, the one Hask had written the week Kanja first called this table something other than the name the Directorate had already begun filing.

Mira carved the name herself, the way she had for Joran's (`MCD-1372`), the same steady hand and the same unhurried care, cutting *Garren Hask* into stone that would hold it long after every person at that table was itself only a name on the same wall. She didn't say anything while she worked. Nobody expected her to.

When it came to Kanja, he didn't reach for anything grand, the same way he never had at this table. "I've had a lot of names," he said, echoing words the table had heard from him before, at a warmer gathering not so long ago, "and he's the reason most of them ever amounted to anything more than a threat classification. The ledger doesn't lie about what a life added up to. His never did."

He looked at the wall a long moment after that, at a name freshly cut beside older ones, and thought — the way he had, once, walking this same pier alone — about a future none of tonight's table would live to see. It didn't feel like an abstraction anymore. It felt like a debt, freshly and specifically owed, and for the first time in ninety-three Chronicles under this particular name, he understood exactly what Efa Gol had meant, all those years ago, about something built that didn't need him to keep existing — and understood, just as clearly, that it would need him to remember. That much, at least, was his to carry.
