# The Match He Chose to Lose

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1838`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CIX, fourth entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave (part A), set strictly
pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. A scene where young Ozmund
consciously chooses restraint over demonstration — holding back his own ordinary physical strength,
not the Density Spike, at a harvest-day wrestling contest near Greyfen — and pays a specific, personal
cost for it: the passing friendship of a knot of local peers who read his modest showing as proof he
was unremarkable, and move on without him. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/
`CC-020`. No new named characters.*

---

Not every cost the General ever described to me came from holding back the thing everyone already knew
to fear in him. Some of them, he told me, came from holding back something far smaller and, in its way,
far harder to give up — the plain, ordinary strength any young man his age might have been proud to
show, and been liked a little more for showing. This is one of those. He gave it to me with a kind of
rueful humor that surprised me the first time I heard it, because most of what he told me from those
years carried some real weight to it, and this one, he insisted, was allowed to be a little foolish as
well as a little sad.

It was a harvest-day gathering at Greyfen, the kind of loose, informal festival a settlement holds for
itself once the grain is safely in and the work of the season has a moment to breathe — and among the
usual contests of the day, the footraces and the archery marks and the greased pole no one ever climbed
successfully, there was a wrestling ring set up in the trampled grass behind the granary, open to any
young man willing to try his luck against whoever else stepped in. He told me he had watched a knot of
local boys his own age circle that ring for the better part of an hour before he let himself be talked
into it — boys he half-knew from prior visits, loud and easy with each other in the particular way of
young men who have grown up together and expect, without quite saying so, that anyone new who wants in
has to earn it first.

He told me he understood, stepping into that ring, exactly how much of himself he could safely let
through without anyone so much as suspecting there was more behind it — and that the true trouble was
never the Spike, which of course never so much as stirred, but the ordinary strength of his own two
arms and back, built by years of the kind of work most lords' sons never do, honest labor at a grain
line or a stuck gate or a training yard, strength that had nothing supernatural in it at all and would
still, he judged, have been more than enough to win every bout in that ring cleanly. He held it back
anyway. Not all of it — he told me he was careful, even telling it to me, not to claim he'd thrown the
matches outright — but enough, consistently enough, across three separate bouts, that he placed
respectably and unremarkably, beaten in the end by a miller's son built half again as broad as he was,
to the entirely ordinary and entirely satisfied roar of a crowd that had gotten exactly the outcome it
expected.

He told me why, and it was smaller and more honest a reason than I expected the first time he gave it
to me. It was not fear of being noticed by anyone who mattered — no one watching that ring that
afternoon had the first idea what he actually was, or ever would. It was simpler and, he said, in its
way more embarrassing than that: he did not trust himself, in the heat and noise of an actual contest,
to know with total certainty exactly where the line sat between *strong for a young lord* and
*strong in a way that asked questions no one in that yard could safely be allowed to ask* — and rather
than risk finding that line by crossing it in front of witnesses, he simply stayed so far back from it
that the question could never arise at all.

What it cost him, he told me, was smaller than a battle and larger, in its own way, than he expected.
Those same boys, the ones who'd circled the ring loudest before he stepped in, gathered up the miller's
son afterward with the easy, unthinking warmth of a group that has just watched someone prove himself
worth including, and went off together toward the evening's fires without a second thought for the
well-dressed boy who'd lost respectably and said little. No one was unkind to him. No one excluded him
on purpose. He simply wasn't, by the measure that yard had just finished taking of him, someone worth
going out of their way for — and he told me he stood by that ring a while after they'd gone, understanding,
not for the first time and certainly not for the last, that the discipline keeping him and everyone
around him safe was also, with the same quiet and total efficiency, the thing standing between him and
every ordinary door a boy his age might otherwise have walked through without a second thought. He told
me he never once, telling it to me, called it a mistake. He only called it a price, paid in full, for a
kind of safety that never once announced itself as a cost until years afterward, when he finally started
counting what it had actually taken from him.
