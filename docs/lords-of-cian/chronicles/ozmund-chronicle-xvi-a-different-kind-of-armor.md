# A Different Kind of Armor

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1745`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XVI — closing entry in the Val Mirel strand. Set strictly pre-Book-1, years before
the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not present in
this entry. Ozmund is roughly twenty-five. Dramatizes Val Mirel teaching him a distinctly Kareth form
of emotional endurance, set against House Verehimu's civic-composure model, as the strand's emotional
high point — the first genuinely three-dimensional, sustained scene of warmth between mother and son
in the whole project. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed
from a story Ozmund told him years later. No new named characters.*

---

Of everything the General ever told me about his mother, this is the one I've thought about the most,
and the one I think he told me for a reason beyond the story itself — because by the time he gave it
to me, he had a Legion of his own to grieve for, piece by piece, year after year, and I think some part
of him wanted me to know where he'd learned how to carry that before I ever had to watch him do it.

There had been an old soldier in the House Guard named — the General never gave me the name, or if he
did I've lost it to time in a way I've never lost anything else he told me, and I've come to think that
was deliberate on his part, that some griefs a man keeps the specifics of for himself even when he
hands you the shape of them. What I know is that the man had served the House since before Ozmund was
born, had taught him to sit a horse before any formal instructor was assigned the task, and had died,
finally, of nothing more dramatic than age and a winter chest-fever, in his own bed, surrounded by
people who loved him, which the General said should have made it an easy death to bear and somehow
didn't.

He was twenty-five. He told me he stood through the funeral rites exactly the way his father had
taught him a Verehimu stood through anything in public — spine straight, face composed, grief folded
away behind a bearing that read, to every eye in the yard, as dignified and untouched. Aethelgard told
him afterward, meaning it as comfort, that he had done his House proud. The General said that was the
first time in his life his father's praise had ever felt like a door closing rather than one opening,
because what he actually wanted, standing there having done everything correctly, was somewhere to put
the thing he'd folded away, and no one in that yard could tell him where.

Val Mirel arrived four days later — not summoned, he was fairly sure, though he never asked how she'd
known, and by then he'd stopped being surprised by how often she simply seemed to know. She found him
alone in the training yard where the old soldier had once taught him to sit a horse, not training,
just standing in it the way a man stands in a room he can't yet bring himself to leave. She didn't ask
him what was wrong. She'd have known before she asked.

What she gave him that afternoon she called, in her own tongue, a phrase that came into his own speech
afterward as the Hollow Stand — and he told me, both times he described it to me, that it was built
from the same bones as the Stone Count she'd taught him as a boy, but aimed at something entirely
different. The Stone Count taught a man to out-wait his own urgency. The Hollow Stand, she told him,
taught a man to hold a grief all the way through instead of folding it away — to stand with the weight
of it fully in his chest, unhidden, unperformed, for as long as it took to stop needing to be carried,
rather than pretend at composure until the pretending became the only shape the grief was ever allowed
to take.

She stood with him through it. That, he said, was the part that undid him most — not the words, which
were few, but that she simply stayed, hour after hour, saying almost nothing, while he stopped being
composed in front of another living person for what he told me was the first time since he'd been old
enough to understand composure was expected of him at all. She told him that House Verehimu had taught
him, rightly, how to be strong where the world could see him. She told him the Order had taught her,
just as rightly, that a strength which only ever appeared where it could be seen wasn't strength at
all — it was armor with nothing underneath it, and a man who wore nothing but that armor his whole
life would eventually be hollowed out by exactly the things he'd never let himself put down.

"Your father gives you the armor the world needs to see," she told him, and this line he gave me
twice, unchanged both times, the way he only ever did with the words that had actually rebuilt
something in him. "I can only give you the kind that holds you up when no one's watching at all. You
will need both before you're done. Most men only ever learn one."

He told me he wept that afternoon the way he never once let himself weep again in all the years I knew
him — not because the lesson stopped the grief, he was careful to make that clear, but because for the
first time in his life he understood that carrying a loss all the way through wasn't a failure of the
composure his father's world demanded. It was simply a different kind of armor, one his mother had
worn through wars long enough to have buried more than he would ever have to count, and one she had
finally, fully, given him.

I think of that afternoon often, now that he's the one I've had to learn to carry. I never once saw
him fold a grief away and pretend it wasn't there. I understand now where he learned not to.

*Continuity notes (not narrative): set roughly three years after Chronicle XV, still strictly pre-
ceremony, Aethelgard alive but present only in reference. Closes the Val Mirel strand as its
deliberate emotional high point, giving Val Mirel Kareth (`MCD-101`/`CC-004`) genuine, sustained
three-dimensional warmth toward her son for the first time in the project — matching the launch
protocol's instruction that her distance (Chronicle XIV) be understood as a chosen cost, not coldness.
The Hollow Stand is introduced as a second original Kareth War-Order discipline, deliberately built
from the same root as the Stone Count (Chronicle XII) but aimed at grief rather than patience, and
plants the emotional root of the profile's "grief converted directly into infrastructure" defense-
mechanism layer — the instinct that later channels Aethelgard's death, and the Unchained Legion's own
losses, into building rather than collapse, is shown here being taught rather than emerging fully
formed. The unnamed old soldier is a one-scene, deliberately unnamed figure per the General's own
narrated reluctance to share the name — not a new named character. Does not touch Cassius Verehimu,
Draconis, Blackthorne, or Grulak, and makes no reference to the Kareth war-sisterhood or Kanja's side
of the family. Closes the five-entry Val Mirel strand; no further entries in it are queued.*
