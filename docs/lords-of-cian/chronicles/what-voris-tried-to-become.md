# What Voris Tried to Become

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Crow King Alias Chronicle XXI, wave 7, closing
entry. Rebellion era, roughly two years after the Night of the Crow King (`MCD-236`, age 23), from
Commandant Voris's own perspective — already a locked named figure. Not a territory Chronicle.
Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters beyond the already-locked
Commandant Voris.*

---

Voris had told the young officer, years before this, that the only real defense against the Crow
King was becoming unpredictable enough that there was no confidence left to exploit — and he had
spent the two years since trying, personally, to become exactly that, rotating his own patrol
doctrine on no fixed schedule, varying his garrison's routines deliberately enough that even his own
staff sometimes struggled to predict what the morning's orders would be.

It worked, in its way. No further scarecrow ever turned up on any post under his direct command.

It also nearly broke his garrison from the inside before it broke anything of the Crow King's.

"Sir, the men don't know what to expect from one week to the next," his own second told him,
plainly enough that Voris couldn't dismiss it as ordinary complaint. "Unpredictability isn't free.
It costs us the same trust in our own routines that we're trying to deny him. Half the garrison
thinks you've started doubting your own judgment."

"I have started doubting it," Voris admitted, later, alone with the thought rather than his staff.
"That's rather the point. I built a career studying a man who wins by exploiting confidence, and the
only cure I could find was removing confidence from my own side too. I didn't think through what
that would cost until I'd already spent two years paying it."

He scaled it back within the month — not abandoning the lesson entirely, but learning its own limit
the hard way: unpredictability aimed outward, at an enemy studying his patterns, was a genuine
defense; unpredictability turned inward, against his own men's need for something solid to stand on,
was simply a different kind of vulnerability wearing the shape of a solution.

"I finally understand something he's probably always known," he told the same young officer, now
years into her own career. "The trick was never becoming impossible to predict. It's knowing exactly
which parts of yourself can afford the cost of changing, and which parts have to stay solid enough
for the people depending on you to still have something to hold onto. I built half of that lesson.
He apparently built the whole thing from the start."
