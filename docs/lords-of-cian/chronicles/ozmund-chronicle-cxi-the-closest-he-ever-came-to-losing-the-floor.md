# The Closest He Ever Came to Losing the Floor

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1840`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CXI, sixth entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave (part A), set strictly
pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. The strand's heaviest entry — an
unplanned, instinctive moment on a mountain road where young Ozmund's reflexive grip nearly exceeds
what his practiced, deliberate control has ever been tested against, frightening him privately in a way
no crisis of raw power ever has. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
explicitly noted within the text as a story told to him only once. No new named characters.*

---

The General gave me a great many stories across our years together that were meant, I think, to
reassure me — proof of a control so total it had become almost boring to describe, a man who had
mastered the most dangerous inheritance either of us had ever heard of so thoroughly that danger no
longer seemed the right word for it at all. This is not one of those stories. He told it to me only the
once, late, in a voice I did not often hear from him, and asked me afterward not to raise it again, and
I have kept that request faithfully until now, because I believe he would want it told honestly, at
last, as part of the fuller account rather than left out of it entirely.

He was on a mountain road above the estate, he told me — narrow, cut along a shoulder of stone with a
long fall on one side of it, nothing unusual for that country, a road he had ridden a hundred times
without incident. The cart horse pulling the supply wagon that day spooked at something he never
identified to me, a snake in the brush or a sudden bird or nothing at all that either of them ever
found, and reared hard enough, close enough to the road's edge, that for the length of perhaps two
heartbeats the entire weight of the wagon and everything in it stood a real chance of going over with
the horse still in the traces.

He told me he did not decide to catch the harness. There was no decision in it at all, and that, he
said, was the entire substance of what frightened him afterward — every use of the Spike he had ever
practiced, every hour at the quarry below Aldenmoor finding the exact threshold of a held breath, every
year of the Stone Count and the copied line and the whole architecture of discipline he had built
around himself since before he could remember building it, all of it lived in the space where there was
time to choose. This had no time in it. His hand had simply closed on the yoke and the harness both,
faster than thought, and held.

He told me the wagon did not go over, and the horse did not fall, and no one in that yard or on that
road that day was hurt in even the smallest way — and that none of that was the part that stayed with
him. What stayed with him was the yoke itself, a solid piece of seasoned oak thick enough to hold a
loaded cart on a mountain grade, which had not merely held under his grip but had *cracked*, clean
through in one place, the wood giving way like something far more brittle than oak has any right to be,
in the same instant his hand closed on it. No one else in that yard understood what they were looking
at. To them it was an oddity, a flaw in the wood finally showing itself under strain, nothing more. He
told me he stood there afterward, the horse calmed, the cargo safe, and understood with a clarity that
frightened him more than any battle he ever later fought, that the amount of force that had gone through
his hand in that instant had not been chosen, had not been measured, had not passed through any part of
the discipline he had spent his whole life building — it had simply happened, the way a reflex happens,
faster than the parts of him that knew how to be careful.

He told me the thought that would not leave him afterward, for longer than he liked to admit, was not
about the wagon or the horse at all. It was the plain, unavoidable arithmetic of what that same
unchosen, unmeasured instant of force would have done to a person, had a person been standing where the
yoke had been instead — a groom's hand on that same harness, say, reaching for the same panicking
animal a half-second before he did. He told me he had built his entire life around the certainty that
he alone stood between his own strength and the people around him, that every threshold was his to
cross or not cross by deliberate choice — and that the mountain road had shown him, in the space of two
heartbeats he never asked for and could not have prevented, that the choice itself might not always be
his to make. He never spoke of it to Osric, or to his father, or to anyone at all, he told me, until the
night he finally gave it to me — not because he thought the telling would change anything about what had
happened, but because he had never found a way to say it out loud without admitting, plainly, that the
floor he had spent a lifetime learning to stand on might not hold every single time he needed it to.
