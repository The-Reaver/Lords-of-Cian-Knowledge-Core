# The Day the Diggers Set Down Their Tools

*Locked canon, Batch 244, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1070`). Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle LXIII, wave 21, closing the wave. A wage-justice entry testing Kanja's own class identity against his command authority for the first time. Not a territory Chronicle.*

---

Forty-one hired laborers — men and women brought on for the tunnel work who'd never sworn to the resistance and never asked to — put down their picks at the start of the seventh week and didn't pick them back up.

Garren Hask found Kanja before anyone else did, the ledger already open in his hands. "They're not striking against the cause. They're striking against the pay schedule. Gas pockets, cave-ins, two dead already this month from conditions that didn't exist when the original wage was set — and they're still drawing the rate we agreed on in week one, before any of that was true."

"They're right." Kanja didn't need the numbers to know it, but he looked at them anyway, because looking was the whole point of Hask keeping them. Hazard had climbed every week the siege dragged on and pay hadn't moved once to match it — an oversight born of urgency, not intent, but an oversight that had cost people money for risking more than they'd bargained for.

It put him somewhere he hadn't stood before. Every prior negotiation in these tunnels — the guild, the rival crews, the quartermaster — had him on the workers' side of the table by default, one tradesman talking to another. Here, for the first time, the wage schedule bore his own authorization at the bottom, and forty-one people who dug beside him every day were standing on the other side of it, waiting to see which side he actually belonged to when it cost something to prove it.

He didn't argue the schedule. He asked Hask for the full accounting — every hour worked under conditions worse than what the original rate priced for, going back to the week the gas readings first spiked — and had it read aloud in the open gallery where the laborers had gathered, no summary, no rounding in his own favor. The number came to more than the crew's discretionary reserve could comfortably absorb. He authorized it anyway, out of the resistance's own operational funds, and told the assembled workers plainly why.

"I dig beside you. That doesn't make me one of you when it's convenient and something else when it costs money. If the rate was wrong, the rate was wrong the whole time, not just today when you finally said so."

A woman near the front — a mucker who'd lost a partner to a cave-in three weeks earlier — asked the question the whole gallery was actually there for. "And the next time the conditions get worse before anybody notices? We strike again, or does somebody watch it this time?"

"Hask watches it now. Every week, against the actual conditions, not the schedule we wrote in week one." He looked at the ledger in Hask's hands, then back at her. "You shouldn't have had to stop digging to be believed. That's on me, not on you for stopping."

They went back to work the next morning, paid in full, the schedule rewritten to track hazard week over week rather than sitting fixed from a day none of them could go back to. Nothing about the tunnels had changed. Only who was accountable for reading them honestly, and to whom.
