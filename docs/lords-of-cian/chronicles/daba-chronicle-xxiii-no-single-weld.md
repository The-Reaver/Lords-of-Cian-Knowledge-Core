# Daba Chronicle XXIII: No Single Weld

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1593`). Entry 23 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series.
Connects the forging thread directly to guerrilla practicality, foreshadowing `MCD-1570`'s eventual
"not mass-produced from a central armory" principle: Kanja draws on the Rexmar tradition's own
organizational doctrine (`MCD-299`, `MCD-300`) to redirect Daba away from building 1804 a single
prized armory and toward teaching the craft onward, cell by cell. No new named characters. Abad's
approval: "rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

---

Daba had the plan drawn before he thought to ask whether it was a good one, which should have
warned him, because that was never how his plans usually arrived.

He'd found the place himself -- a collapsed salt mine two ridgelines from the nearest cell, dry
enough for a forge, deep enough that no smoke would show, with a single approach a handful of people
could hold against a company. He walked Kanja through it with the particular pride of a man showing
off a fortification rather than a room: here is where the bellows would sit, here the quench, here
the racks, and given six months and the right hands, here is where 1804 stops fielding whatever
scrap a cell happens to scavenge and starts fielding blades built the way his had begun to be built.
One place. The best hands he had. Everything worth making, made there.

Kanja let him finish before he said anything, which Daba had learned to read as its own kind of
warning.

"You'd put your best people in one room, behind one approach, making the only weapons worth
carrying."

"Defensible weapons," Daba said. "That's what defensible means."

"It means one raid away from every cell in 1804 carrying scrap again. Worse -- one raid away from
someone learning that if they take this room, they've taken the only place that can arm you." Kanja
crouched, drew a rough map in the dust with one finger: five small marks scattered wide, no single
line connecting them. "Jicome's never fallen. Not because its forges are unreachable. Because there
isn't one forge that matters more than the others. Every smith fights. Every fighter can mend a
blade well enough to matter. You take one, you've taken one. You haven't taken the thing."

Daba looked at the dust map and felt the shape of his own doctrine looking back at him from a
direction he hadn't expected it to come from. He had built 1804 on exactly this principle without
ever once applying it past the reach of a rifle. Dispersed cells. No function resting on a single
loss. Anything large enough to be seen was large enough to be burned -- he had said those words to
new recruits a hundred times and had somehow, in the space of a season learning to forge, forgotten
they applied to the forge too.

"So I don't build the room," he said slowly.

"You don't build the room. You build the thing that doesn't need one." Kanja reached for the
reinforcing weld he'd been teaching Daba over the last two lessons -- a pattern that took a
practiced hand the better part of a day to lay properly, strong enough to outlast the blade it
joined. He worked it a different way this time, faster, cruder, losing perhaps a third of its
strength for a fraction of the time and none of the training. "This version, anyone in a cell learns
in an afternoon. It won't hold as long as mine. It'll hold long enough, and it'll hold in five places
at once instead of one place perfectly."

Daba watched him lay the simplified weld and understood, with the flat clarity of a lesson finally
closing a loop he hadn't known was open, what he was actually being taught. Not a technique. A
demotion of his own ambition, offered kindly. He wasn't meant to become 1804's one great smith,
carrying the craft alone in a defensible hole in a mountain, waiting to be the target someone
eventually found. He was meant to become the thing that made a great smith unnecessary -- fifty
competent hands scattered where no single column could reach all of them in one season, each one
able to fail alone without the network coming apart.

"I was about to build the exact thing I've spent three years teaching people to never become," Daba
said.

"You weren't wrong to want it good," Kanja said, tapping the crude weld once to test it. "You were
wrong about where good needed to live."

Daba erased the dust map with his boot before he stood, already redrawing it in his head -- not one
room, but a dozen small lessons carried outward the way word of an ambush point traveled cell to
cell, no single place holding more than its own cell could afford to lose.
