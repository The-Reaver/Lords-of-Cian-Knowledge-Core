# What the Third Generation Asked

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Iron Bastard Alias Chronicle LVI, wave 19. Rebellion era. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The second student had taken on an apprentice of her own by the time Kanja next passed through her
district — a fact she mentioned almost in passing, the way a thing stops feeling remarkable once it's
simply become ordinary practice.

He hadn't expected to meet a third generation of the doctrine standing in front of him, listening rod
in hand, asking questions neither he nor the student who'd trained her had quite been asked before.

"She wants to know why the doctrine only reads tension," the second student said, half apology, half
genuine curiosity of her own. "Not weight. Not age. Not whether a thing is good or worth saving. Just
whether it's holding or about to stop."

Kanja considered it longer than the question probably warranted, aware of exactly how many engagements
had turned on an answer he'd never actually had to say out loud before.

"Because tension is the only thing a structure tells the truth about," he finally said, crouching to
the apprentice's own height rather than answering down at her. "Ask a wall if it's good, and it can't
answer — that's not a question with a sound. Ask it if it's holding, and it always answers, every time,
whether the news is good or not."

"But you use it to decide things," the apprentice pressed, younger and less careful than either of the
adults about following a question all the way to its edge. "Whether to break something. Whether to save
it. That's not just tension. That's you."

The second student went quiet at that, watching Kanja's face for how he'd take being caught out by
someone barely old enough to hold the rod steady.

"She's right," he said, after a silence longer than he usually allowed himself. "The doctrine tells me
the truth about the structure. It's never once told me what to do with the truth. That part's always
been mine. I think I let people believe otherwise sometimes, because it's easier to trust a method than
to trust a man's judgment underneath it."

The apprentice didn't look satisfied, exactly, but she looked like she'd gotten an honest answer rather
than a comfortable one, which seemed to matter to her more.

"Keep asking her that," Kanja told the second student before he left. "Every generation this doctrine
survives ought to ask the one before it that same question at least once. I'd rather it get asked than
quietly stop being true."
