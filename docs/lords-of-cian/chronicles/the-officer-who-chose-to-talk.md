# The Officer Who Chose to Talk

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Bane Alias Chronicle XL, first entry in the
fourteenth wave. Rebellion era, age 19-20, roughly a year after the Black Trench. Not a territory
Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The captured Suppression officer had been taken at the Marrenfeld battlement, one of a handful of
prisoners the column kept rather than released, and for the first week of his captivity he gave
nothing — not out of discipline exactly, but out of the same conviction the man at Voss Hollow's
gate had carried, that cooperating with a story soldiers told to excuse losing would mean admitting
he'd lost to something more than an ordinary enemy.

What changed his mind wasn't interrogation. It was simply watching, over a week of enforced
proximity, how the column actually treated its prisoners — fed the same rations as everyone else,
wounds treated by the same medic who'd finally started treating Bane's own, no threats made that
weren't kept and no promises made that were broken, the plain unglamorous decency the reputation
never advertised because decency didn't travel the way fear did.

"I expected worse," he said, the morning he finally spoke, unprompted, to the guard rotation rather
than waiting to be asked anything. "Everyone in my company expected worse. That's most of why the
name works on us the way it does — nobody actually knows what happens after, so everyone assumes the
worst version."

Bane came to hear it himself once word reached him. "What made you decide to talk now?"

"You've had a week to make me disappear quietly, or make an example of me, or do any of the things
my own officers would have done with a captured enemy who'd cost them what I cost your column. You
didn't. I kept waiting for the version of this that matched the file on you, and it never showed
up." The officer didn't offer much in the way of true operational secrets — nothing that would have
cost his own people meaningfully — but what he gave was real: honest confirmation of exactly how
distorted the Directorate's internal accounts of Bane's engagements had become, entire casualty
figures inflated or invented to justify continued funding for the effort to stop him.

"That's not nothing," Bane said, when the accounting was done. "That tells me as much about your
own command as it does about me."

"It should. I don't think most of my superiors have any better idea what they're actually fighting
than I did a week ago." The officer was released, unransomed, weeks later, carrying nothing back
with him but what he'd seen firsthand — which travelled, in its own quiet way, further than any
document the column could have extracted from him by force.
