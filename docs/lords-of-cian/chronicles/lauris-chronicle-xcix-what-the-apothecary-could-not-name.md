# Lauris Chronicle XCIX: What the Apothecary Could Not Name

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1716`). Ninety-ninth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, Strand W. A stakes-free entry pairing Lauris with Matar (`CC-102`), the crew's
most reserved member -- an evening spent helping him identify an unlabeled compound in the
Apothecary, his six-century toxin case, conducted almost entirely in comfortable silence. Puts
`CC-134`'s combat-joy trait on the page in its quietest register yet: satisfaction taken in patient,
unhurried, largely wordless work. No new named characters.*

-----

*Archive fragment, present-day, undated by her own habit: "Matar does not speak more than a
sentence requires. I have found I do not mind this. Most evenings require fewer sentences than
people spend on them."*

There is little I can say of Matar with any confidence, and I say this as a man who has spent thirty
years making a profession of confident description, because he has given me, across six centuries of
crew history preceding my own account of it, remarkably little to work with. Six hundred years of
discipline built him into something no variant biology could improve on, and I have come to
understand that the discipline itself extends to conversation, which he treats, so far as I have
observed, with the same extended, unhurried study he brings to a target before he ever engages it --
which is to say he offers almost none of it until he has decided it is warranted.

Lauris, I have discovered, finds this restful.

She came to him one evening over a vial in the Apothecary that had lost its label sometime in the
transit from one safehouse to another -- a compound of his own six-century refinement, unidentifiable
even to him by scent or color alone, which I gather troubled him rather more than he let show, since
an unlabeled toxin among several hundred correctly labeled ones is, to a man who has built his entire
method on precision, roughly equivalent to a single wrong figure in an otherwise perfect ledger. He
did not ask for her help. She offered it, and he did not decline it, which I have come to understand
is, from Matar, very nearly the same thing as a request.

What followed was, by every account I have gathered of it, almost entirely silent. He worked through
a sequence of careful, patient tests -- solubility, reactivity, the specific graduated discoloration
each of his six centuries of compounds produces under controlled exposure -- while she sat across
from him doing nothing so much as watching, occasionally handing him an instrument before he had
finished reaching for it, having noted, correctly, which one he would need next. Neither of them, I
am told, spoke for the better part of two hours.

I asked her afterward what the two hours had actually consisted of, since an account with no
dialogue in it is a difficult thing for me to render honestly, and she told me, with the particular
plainness she reserves for observations she considers self-evident, that this was rather the point.
"He does not test a compound the way most people test anything," she said. "He tests it the way I
have spent six thousand years learning to test a claim -- refusing to advance past uncertainty until
the uncertainty resolves itself honestly, and refusing to fill the silence with guesses simply
because the silence is uncomfortable to someone else. I have very rarely been in a room with someone
who found the same silence I find comfortable."

They identified the compound, in the end, as an early, since-abandoned formulation of what he now
calls the sedative he has used against targets three times his own density -- a version he had judged
insufficiently reliable decades ago and set aside without destroying, on the theory, he told her, that
an imperfect result correctly labeled is worth more than a perfect result he cannot fully account
for. It was, I gather, the longest single statement he offered across the entire evening, and she
told me afterward that she considered it a genuine gift, from a man who does not extend words freely.

"Thank you," he said, when the vial was relabeled and returned to its proper place in the case, and
I am told he looked at her directly when he said it, which she informed me later is, from Matar, a
considerable departure from his ordinary custom.

"You are welcome," she said. "I enjoyed it."

I do not think she meant this as a courtesy. I have come to recognize, across the many quiet evenings
I have now recorded of her, that she means this particular sentence with total, uncomplicated
sincerity whenever she says it, regardless of how unremarkable the evening that produced it might
seem to anyone reading the account afterward -- and I have come to suspect that an evening spent in
near-total silence, with a man who asks nothing of her but the patience she has spent six thousand
years learning to give freely, is closer to genuine rest for her than most of what this crew would
recognize as leisure.
