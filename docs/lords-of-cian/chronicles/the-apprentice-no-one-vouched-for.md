# The Apprentice No One Vouched For

*Locked canon, Batch 262, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1313`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LXVII, first
entry in the twenty-third wave. Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241). A
genuinely new register of suspicion/trust, distinct from the prior structural-sabotage entry.
Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

He arrived alone, which was the first thing that made people look twice — no settlement to send
word ahead of him, no relative to walk him up the gangplank and hand him off with the usual
mutter of thanks. He gave a name, a trade he claimed to have half-learned somewhere upriver, and
nothing else that anyone could check, and within two days the whispered arithmetic of the
terrace had already run its course: a stranger, unvouched, asking careful questions about where
the Dead Drakma stores were kept.

"He's asking the same questions the saboteur asked," one of the older apprentices said, low,
remembering MCD-501 the way the whole terrace still did. "Maybe not the same reasons. But the same
questions."

Kanja watched him for three days before doing anything about it — not confronting, just watching,
the way he'd watch a new alloy before trusting it near a blade. The boy asked about stores because
he was hungry and embarrassed to ask directly for food. He asked about the schedule because he
was terrified of being late and losing the one place that had let him stay without asking where he
came from first. He flinched at raised voices in a way that had nothing to do with tactics and
everything to do with wherever he'd actually come from, upriver or not.

"You could have just asked me," Kanja told him, on the fourth day, sitting down across from him at
the meal line rather than pulling him aside for it. "About the food. About the hours. Nobody here
needs you to have papers."

The boy went very still. "People were talking."

"People talk. It's what people who got burned once do." Kanja didn't soften it, because softening
it would have been its own kind of lie. "We checked. Not because we didn't want you here — because
we've been lied to by someone who looked exactly this nervous, once, and it cost us. You're not
him. But you understand why we had to be sure before I could say that out loud."

"I do," the boy said, quieter than before, and something in his shoulders came down that hadn't
come down since he'd walked up the gangplank alone. He stayed. Nobody watched him quite the same
careful way again, and within a season he was the one walking new, frightened arrivals through
where the meal line was and when the shift changed, so they wouldn't have to be too proud to ask.
