# Daba Chronicle XII: The Test Daba Didn't Announce

Daba did not approach him again for eleven days. In that time he watched him, or had him watched,
almost without pause, and told no one on the cell -- not even Riasa -- what he was building toward,
because Daba had learned early that a hypothesis announced too soon becomes a conclusion defended
too hard.

He watched the boy carry ore-sacks on a schedule designed to break grown men by the third week and
saw him pace himself against it the way a soldier paces a march he already knows the length of,
though nothing in the boy's history, as far as Daba's own careful asking around the cut could
determine, involved a single day of formal service to anyone. He watched him move through a crowded
loading yard during a shift change -- three hundred bodies in poor light, the worst kind of ground for
a man to keep track of anything -- and never once collide with another worker, never once take the
inefficient path, the way water finds a channel without appearing to search for one.

And on the ninth night, Daba built him a problem.

It was not subtle, as tests went, and Daba was not proud of the crudeness of it, but crude tests
answer honest questions fastest. He had one of his own people -- a cell runner passing through the
territory on unrelated business, someone the boy had never seen and would never see again -- stage a
theft from the boy's own gang: a week's grain ration, taken in the confusion of a shift change and
carried toward the tree line with enough clumsiness that a boy paying ordinary attention would lose
the trail within a hundred paces.

The boy did not lose the trail. He did not chase blindly either, which was the part that told Daba,
watching from a rise above the treeline with his own scouts already in position in case the boy did
something that required intervention, that this was not luck compounding on luck. The boy read the
runner's stride length from the depth of the prints, adjusted his own pace to close distance without
ever breaking into a run that would announce him, and cut the angle -- not toward where the runner
was, but toward where the treeline narrowed, where the runner would have to slow regardless of how
fast he wanted to move. He had the grain back inside twenty minutes, without ever laying a hand on the
runner, who reported afterward, half-admiring and half-unsettled, that the boy had simply stood in
his path at the narrow point and waited, as though he'd known the ground would do the work for him
before the runner had chosen which way to run.

Daba had read, in what fragments of the old campaign accounts still survived in 1804's own gathered
archives, about Haku the Unifier's wars against the one the old records called only the Deposed
King -- battles won not by weight of numbers but by a commander who seemed to already know the shape
a fight would take before the first blow landed, who treated terrain and formation and the enemy's
own momentum as a single continuous surface to be read rather than fought. Daba had always assumed,
reading those fragments, that they were flattered by five thousand years of retelling the way every
old war gets flattered.

Watching a boy who had never held a weapon in formal instruction cut an angle through a dark treeline
as though the ground itself had told him where the runner would tire, Daba stopped assuming that.

He found the boy the next morning, at the cut, and this time he did not stay at the treeline.

"That grain theft was mine," Daba said, without preamble, because the boy had earned that much
honesty and Daba suspected he would have known it was staged regardless. "I needed to see what you'd
do without anyone telling you what to do."

The boy's expression didn't change much, but something in his shoulders eased, fractionally, the way
a man's shoulders ease when a threat resolves into a proposition instead. "And?"

"And I don't think anyone taught you what you did last night. I think you were born already knowing
half of it, and the other half you've been teaching yourself alone, badly, because nobody's ever shown
you it could be done well." Daba let that sit. "I could show you the rest. If you're willing to be
taught something you've spent your whole life doing without being told."

The boy looked at him for a long moment -- longer, Daba thought, than the offer strictly required --
and then nodded, once, the way a man nods at a debt he intends to honor rather than a gift he intends
to enjoy.

"Then teach me," he said. "But I'm not doing it for nothing."

Daba, who had spent eleven days deciding whether this boy was a danger worth fearing, found himself,
for the first time since the Rookery, almost amused. "Neither am I," he said. "We'll get to that."
