Master Alric Fenmoor had outlived his own usefulness to House Verehimu by the time I tracked him
down — long retired from tutoring, kept on a modest House pension out of what he called, dryly, "an
old debt someone still remembered owing" — and he answered my questions from behind a writing desk
buried under three separate unfinished translations, the kind of man who had clearly never once in
his life stopped working simply because no one was paying him to. He told me plainly, before I'd
asked a single real question, that he expected to disappoint me. He said everyone who came asking
about the General wanted a story about power, and all he had to offer was a story about arithmetic.

He'd been engaged to the household when Ozmund was ten, on the ordinary understanding given to every
tutor House Verehimu had ever hired before him — teach the heir his letters, his histories, his
figures, enough to govern competently and no further ambition required. Fenmoor said he'd broken that
understanding inside the first month, deliberately, because he had never in thirty years of teaching
found a student worth less than his own actual standards, and he saw no reason a lord's son should be
the first exception.

What that meant in practice, he said, was refusing to accept an answer simply because it was
plausible, or well-phrased, or delivered with the particular confidence young nobles were trained
into from the cradle. He gave the example, unprompted, of an early lesson in the Sovereign Trust's
Scrip mechanics — a subject he considered essential and most tutors considered beneath a future lord,
who would only ever need to know enough to be told he was being robbed, not enough to prove it. Ozmund
had given him, on his first attempt, an answer that was correct in its conclusion and wrong in every
step that led there — the kind of answer, Fenmoor said, that would have satisfied any examiner who
cared more about the destination than the road. He failed the boy on the spot and made him redo the
whole working from the beginning, twice, in front of no one, with no explanation beyond that a wrong
method dressed as a right answer was worse than an honest failure, because it taught a mind to stop
checking its own work the moment the outcome looked acceptable.

He said the boy took it badly, the first several times — not with anger, which Fenmoor said he could
have worked with, but with a kind of quiet, controlled frustration that read, to a man who'd taught
enough young nobles to know the type, like someone unaccustomed to being told he hadn't yet earned
something. Fenmoor didn't soften the standard for it. He told me he considered that the entire point
of what he was there to do — that a household full of people trained to defer to the boy's rank would
never once tell him the plain truth about the quality of his own thinking, and someone in his life had
to, or he'd grow into a lord who mistook being agreed with for being right.

It took, by Fenmoor's own careful count, the better part of two years before the boy stopped treating
correction as an insult and started treating it as information — a shift Fenmoor said he could mark
almost to the week, because it showed first not in the boy's answers but in his questions, which
went from seeking confirmation to genuinely, openly seeking to be told where he was wrong before
Fenmoor had to tell him. He said that once that shift happened, teaching Ozmund became the most
satisfying work of his career, not because the boy was uniquely gifted — Fenmoor was careful, again,
not to overclaim that — but because he had become, by simple and repeated insistence, a mind that
would rather be corrected than flattered, which Fenmoor said was rarer among lords' sons than any
gift of intellect could ever be.

I asked him, near the end, whether he'd ever suspected there was more to the boy than a future
governor's education required — whether anything in those years had hinted at what the General
actually was. Fenmoor considered the question for a long while before answering, and when he did, it
wasn't really an answer at all. He said he'd never had any reason to look for power in a student, only
for honesty, and that in his experience the two had very little to do with each other — that he'd
taught plenty of powerful men who couldn't reason their way out of a single wrong assumption, and he
suspected, without ever having proof of it, that whatever power the boy eventually turned out to
carry, it was the two years of failed Scrip workings that had actually built the man capable of
carrying it without being ruined by it.
