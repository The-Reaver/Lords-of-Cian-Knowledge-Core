# What Efa Gol Learned to Read

*Locked canon, Batch 194, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-626`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle XXI, wave 7 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

Long before Warehouse Twelve, long before she was commanding decoy forces at Iron Shallows or
steadying crowd flow through the Ash-Wharf evacuation, Efa Gol was a cargo-press operator who had
never given a single order to anyone in her life, and had no particular reason to believe she ever
would.

The Compliance patrol routed toward the Marrow Landing meeting wasn't supposed to be there. Somebody
had miscounted the schedule, or somebody had talked, and either way the patrol was forty minutes from
walking directly into a gathering that couldn't afford to be found.

Efa Gol was the only person close enough to do anything about it, and she had never misdirected so
much as a supervisor's attention in her life.

"You want me to what," she said, when the runner reached her with the warning and the only plan
anyone had time to make.

"Walk toward them. Slow. Carry something heavy enough that dropping it looks like an accident, not a
choice. Give them a reason to stop and help you instead of a reason to keep walking toward Marrow
Landing." The runner didn't have time to explain further. "You're the only one in position."

She did it wrong the first time — dropped the crate too early, too far from the patrol's line of
sight, and had to scramble to make a second, more convincing accident happen twenty paces closer
before they'd fully committed to their route. It worked anyway, barely, the patrol's sergeant
ordering two men to help her right the spilled cargo while the rest waited, and by the time they were
moving again the meeting at Marrow Landing had already dispersed clean.

Kanja found her afterward, still shaking slightly from the adrenaline of having improvised something
she had never once rehearsed.

"You bought us forty minutes with a dropped crate," he said. "That's not luck. That's reading a
patrol's own attention well enough to know exactly what it would stop for."

"I got it wrong the first try."

"You corrected it the second time, in the moment, with people's lives depending on whether you
guessed right. That's the part that isn't luck." He didn't make more of it than it was, but he didn't
let it pass unremarked either. "I don't know what to call what you just did yet. But I know I'd
rather have someone who can read a patrol's attention and redirect it than someone who can only fight
one. We're going to need both, before this is finished."

She would spend years learning to make the second correction unnecessary — to read the attention
right the first time, every time — but the habit, the instinct that would eventually anchor an entire
evacuation's crowd flow at Ash-Wharf, started with a mistimed crate and forty borrowed minutes at
Marrow Landing.
