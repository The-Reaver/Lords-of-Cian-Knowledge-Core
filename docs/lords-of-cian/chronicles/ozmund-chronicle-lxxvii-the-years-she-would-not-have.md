# The Years She Would Not Have

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1806`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXXVII — eighth entry in the Val Mirel strand's third wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, unmentioned in this
entry. Ozmund is roughly twenty-six. Dramatizes Val Mirel's private reckoning with the disparity
between her own vast Kareth lifespan (`MCD-101`) and her son's far shorter one, an existential register
not yet shown in the strand. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years later. No new named characters.*

---

The General gave me this story only once, near the end, and told me afterward that he wished he had
found the words to give it to me sooner, because by the time he did, he told me, he had already begun
to understand it from the inside in a way he hadn't when it first happened to him.

He found her, that evening, somewhere she had never let him find her before — not the training yard,
not the north terrace, but a small, unremarkable room in the older part of the House that he'd always
understood, without ever being told outright, was hers alone whenever she stayed, a room with almost
nothing in it beyond a single chair set to face a window that looked out over nothing in particular. He
told me he'd gone looking for her only because dinner had passed without her appearing, and he'd found
her sitting in that chair in the dark, not reading, not resting, simply sitting, in a stillness that
was different, he said, from every other stillness he'd ever watched her hold.

He asked her, carefully, if something was wrong. She didn't answer at first, and he told me he nearly
left rather than press her, the old instinct from a hundred smaller silences telling him some things
were not his to ask twice for. But she gestured, eventually, to the chair beside hers, and he sat, and
for a long while neither of them said anything at all.

What she finally told him, once she spoke, he gave me almost word for word, because he said he had
turned it over in his own mind often enough since that he could no longer separate her exact phrasing
from his own remembering of it, and he had stopped trying. She told him that she had been alive, by
then, for very nearly ninety thousand years — a span, she said, that had stopped meaning anything to
her personally sometime in the first few thousand of them, the way a distance stops meaning anything
once you've walked far enough past the point where you could still picture where you started. She told
him she did not fear her own death, had not feared it in longer than House Verehimu's oldest records
could account for. What she had never learned to hold, in all that time, was the arithmetic of loving
someone whose own span was so much shorter than hers that she would, in the ordinary course of things,
watch the whole of his life pass the way a person watches a single season change.

He asked her, he told me, whether that frightened her. She told him no — fear was not the right word
for it, she said, and she had spent centuries trying to find a better one and never quite had. What she
described instead was something closer to a debt she had entered into with her eyes fully open, the
day she chose to have him, knowing exactly what the shape of it would cost her: that she would love him
for a span of years so brief, against the length of her own life, that it would barely register as a
season, and that when it ended — however it ended, whenever it ended — she would go on, by any measure
that mattered, for tens of thousands of years more, carrying the whole of what he had been to her
inside a life that would otherwise have had no one left in it who remembered him the way she did.

She told him she had never once regretted the choice. She was careful, he said, to make that plain
before anything else — that loving him for what would be, to her, a brief and precious span was not a
lesser love for being brief, and that she had watched enough of her own kind choose never to love
anyone whose life ran shorter than theirs, protecting themselves from precisely this reckoning, and
had come to think of that choice, whatever comfort it bought them, as its own kind of small death,
paid in advance to avoid a larger one later. She told him she would rather carry the whole weight of
missing him for ten thousand years than never once have known him at all.

He told me he sat with her a long while after that, neither of them speaking further, and that he
understood, sitting there, something he had never once considered before about the whole architecture
of her careful distance, her timed visits, her chosen absences — that some part of it, alongside
everything she had already told him on that terrace years before, was also this: a woman learning, in
real time, how much of a finite thing she could allow herself to hold before the holding of it became
unbearable to set down again, even briefly, even for good reason.

"She never told me that story again," he said to me, the only time he ever gave it to me at all. "I
don't think she needed to. I understood, after that night, that every single visit she ever made me
after it was a woman choosing, deliberately, to spend a little more of a debt she already knew she
would spend the rest of her own vast life still paying."
