# The Notebook Garren Hask Finally Opened

*Locked canon, Batch 251, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1090`). Captain Alias Chronicle LXII, wave 21. The
mortality-succession thread advances, deliberately without resolving. Not a territory Chronicle.*

---

Six weeks into doing exactly what Sera had ordered him to do, Garren Hask called the dispute council
into session for the second time in its history with a real page to put on the table, rather than a
blank one. He'd kept the notebook for years already — a private thing, filled a page at a time
whenever a thought about what came after him got too heavy to carry alone, never once shown to anyone
until the night his own heart had made the question stop being theoretical.

"I told you once I'd become the one who remembers, when the time came," Kanja said, before Hask could
open it. "You don't have to do this because you nearly died. You could just tell me and let me carry
it."

"That's exactly the mistake I'm trying to stop us from making," Hask said, and set the notebook down
unopened between them. "If it only lives in your head, it's not an institution. It's a favor you're
doing us, and favors end when the person doing them decides to stop, or isn't there to keep doing
them. I've watched you turn everything else this crew leans on into something that survives you not
being in the room. This is the one thing I let you get away with keeping personal, and I was wrong to
let it slide that long."

He opened it to the council instead — Corren Halst, Efa Gol, Callum Breck, and Kanja himself, one
voting seat among the rest exactly as the charter said. The notebook held no finished answer, which
surprised no one who'd read the charter's own blank fourth line. It held questions written and
rewritten across years: whether the council should keep choosing its own chair by consensus the way
it always had, or whether someone should be named now, while the founding generation could still teach
the role instead of leaving it to be reinvented cold. Hask proposed, hesitantly, a name he'd turned
over more than any other — Corren Halst, the one among them who'd already led a split engagement on
her own and never once needed Kanja in the room to make a hard call correctly.

Halst didn't accept it on the spot, and didn't pretend the offer wasn't real either. "Ask me again in
five years," she said. "Not because I'm saying no. Because if the point is that this outlasts any one
of us, then naming me today because Hask's heart scared him last month is exactly the kind of decision
the charter was built to slow down, not speed up."

Nobody argued with that. The council didn't vote that night, and Hask didn't ask it to — he closed the
notebook the way he'd once closed a charter session he wasn't finished with, filed it in front of the
ledgers instead of behind them, and told the room plainly that a question worth taking seriously
deserved more than one meeting's worth of courage. What changed was smaller than a decision and larger
than nothing: for the first time, the fourth line of the charter had a real conversation sitting next
to its blank space instead of an empty page waiting for one.

Kanja walked out beside Halst afterward. "Five years," he said.

"Five years," she agreed. "Ask me again then, and I'll actually have thought about it instead of just
reacting to Hask nearly dying in front of us."
