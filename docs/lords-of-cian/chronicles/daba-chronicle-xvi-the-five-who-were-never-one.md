# Daba Chronicle XVI: The Five Who Were Never One

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1586`). Entry 16 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes small-unit
doctrine as a specific, live-drilled lesson during Kanja's mentorship under Daba (`MCD-1568`) --
distinct dispersed cells with no shared coordinator, rather than one commanded force -- and captures
the first moment Kanja's own instinct to unify and lead runs directly against 1804's founding
discipline. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make it
efficient."*

---

Daba never explained a lesson before it cost something. He had tried, early, teaching the way most
people teach -- principle first, practice after -- and watched it slide off boys who had never lost
anything to a bad plan. So he had stopped explaining and started arranging losses instead, small ones,
recoverable ones, and letting the boy in front of him supply his own explanation afterward. It worked
better. It always worked better.

The exercise was simple on its face: five cells of two, each given a different approach to a supply
cache buried at the marsh's northern lip, each cell blind to the others' routes. Reya ran the opposing
line -- eleven of hers, spread thin and patient, told only to intercept whatever crossed their ground
and report what they found. Nothing about the drill was dangerous. That was the point. Daba built his
losses cheap so the lesson would be the only expensive thing about them.

He watched Kanja fold himself into the fifth cell without being told which one to take, the way he did
most things -- not asking permission, simply arriving at the decision before anyone thought to offer
it to him. Daba let it stand. He was curious, by now, less about whether the boy would succeed than
about what shape his failing would take, because failing shapes told you more about a person than
winning ever did.

Within the hour, Kanja had done what Daba expected and dreaded in equal measure. He had found the other
four cell leaders before the drill even started -- quietly, at the water trough, in the time it took
to fill a skin -- and by nightfall there was a plan. One plan. A single approach vector, staggered
timing, a runner named Ossa carrying the full sequence cell to cell so that all five would move as one
coordinated hand closing on the cache from five directions at once. It was, Daba had to admit, watching
it assembled from a distance, an intelligent plan. It was also exactly the plan he had spent six years
teaching 1804 never to make.

Reya's people took Ossa forty minutes in, crossing open ground between the second and third cell with
the whole sequence held in her head and nothing written down to burn if caught. She was not hurt. She
was simply stopped, disarmed of information rather than a weapon, and by the rules of the drill that
was the end of it -- not just for her cell, but for all five, because every one of them had been
counting on a signal that would now never come.

Four cells reached the marsh's edge undetected. Four cells sat in the reeds past midnight waiting for
a runner who was drinking Reya's tea half a mile away, and eventually gave up and came in one by one,
having accomplished nothing they could not have accomplished hours earlier if they had simply moved.

Daba found Kanja afterward, sitting apart from the others with the particular stillness of someone
replaying a failure from every angle it could be viewed from, looking for the one where it hadn't
happened. He did not offer comfort. Comfort taught nothing.

"You built one plan out of five," Daba said.

"I built the *best* plan out of five." Kanja did not look up. "It would have worked if she hadn't
caught the runner."

"It didn't work because you built something that could be caught at all." Daba sat, unhurried, the
way he always sat when the next sentence mattered more than the ones before it. "Four of your people
never came near Reya's line tonight. And you still lost, because you gave one person the power to lose
for all five."

Kanja was quiet long enough that Daba began to suspect the lesson had not landed, that he would have
to build a costlier loss before it did. Then: "So five plans. Not one plan carried by five people."

"Five plans," Daba agreed. "None of them worth catching, because none of them is the whole thing.
A cell that fails should only ever be able to lose what it was carrying -- never what the others were."

He watched something settle behind the boy's eyes that had nothing to do with the drill and everything
to do with the shape of every fight he would ever build after this one -- the first small crack in an
instinct, inherited rather than taught, that had never once had to consider losing before. Daba did
not know yet what that crack would grow into. He knew only that it had opened tonight, in a marsh, over
a runner who had done nothing wrong except carry too much.
