# Yara Chronicle I: The Seat She Did Not Wait For

*Locked canon, Batch 64, 2026-09-05 (`MCD-336`), superseding `MCD-333`. First entry in Yara's own
Chronicles, its protagonist Yalokona (PH2-006), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, one
witness among several whose presence matters mechanically to Yalokona's own Unbought and Unbossed ability
(immune to pressure so long as it happens witnessed, in the light) -- his role is structural, not
authorial: he does not persuade her, does not advise her, only stands where a witness is needed.
Narration is close-third on Yalokona throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Slots into no existing mainline
MCD- rule -- original homage-era material set in Yara itself. Draws on the already-locked detail in
PH2-006 that "her own movement" once wanted her to wait her turn for someone more electable, and that she
ran anyway.*

---

A door held open out of courtesy is still, underneath the courtesy, a door someone else controls.

They asked her into the back room the way they always did -- warmly, the warmth doing most of the
work the argument itself couldn't. Six men she had built half her career standing beside, men who
had marched when marching was the only tool anyone had, sat around a table too small for the
decision they thought they were about to make for her.

"Nobody's saying no," the eldest of them said. He had a way of starting every difficult sentence
with the word that made it sound like one. "We're saying not yet. Let the seat open properly. Let
the district see a face it already trusts."

"The district already sees my face."

"You know what I mean."

"I do. That's the problem." Yalokona did not raise her voice in that room, the same as she never
raised it anywhere else -- she had learned early that volume was a tool men reached for when they'd
run out of better ones, and she preferred to keep hers in reserve for when it would actually cost
something. "You mean a face you trust to wait. I've been waiting since before any of you thought to
ask whether I would."

"This isn't about trust."

"It's entirely about trust. You trust the district to elect me eventually. You don't trust it to
elect me *now*, and you've mistaken the second thing for patience." A pause, the kind she let sit
exactly as long as it needed to. "I've watched patience get handed to every woman in every room like
this one since before I was old enough to be in it. It's always framed as wisdom. It's never once
been wisdom. It's been a door held open by men who control which hour it opens."

"You'll split the coalition."

"I'll test it. If it only holds together by making me wait outside it, it was never the coalition
you told me it was."

She left the back room before they finished deciding how to answer that, because she had already
decided the only answer that mattered was the one she was about to give the district itself, in the
open, where six men in a back room couldn't quietly unmake it afterward.

The announcement hall had no roof worth the name -- half of Yara's public buildings didn't, by
design, a habit the district had kept since before Yalokona was born, on the theory that a promise
made where the sky could hear it was a promise that had to mean what it said. She stood at its
center at midday, in full light, with more witnesses crowded into the open frame than the six men
who'd tried to talk her out of it could plausibly out-argue afterward. Dockhands. Shopkeepers. Two
of the six themselves, arrived after all, standing at the edges with expressions she chose not to
read too closely.

And one man she had never seen before, near the back, watching with the particular stillness of
someone who had learned, a long time ago, that showing up mattered more than most of what a person
said once they arrived.

She noticed him only because he was the one person in the crowd not watching her. He was watching
the crowd itself -- counting it, she would realize later, the way a man counts a structure's load
before deciding whether it holds.

She spoke plainly, because plain was the only register that couldn't later be reinterpreted into
something smaller than she meant. She was running. Not next term. Not when the seat opened properly.
Now, in the open, with every person in that roofless hall as her record of having said so.

The six men did not try to stop her afterward. They would tell themselves later that they'd chosen
not to, and there would be a kind of truth in that -- but the fuller truth, which none of them
said aloud, was that a promise made to that many witnesses, in that much light, had already passed
the point where six men in a back room could quietly unmake it. It had become the kind of fact a
whole district now held jointly. Nothing that many people witness together stays any one man's alone
to take back.

The stranger was gone by the time the crowd began to disperse. She never learned his name, never
saw him again that season, and would only understand much later -- turning the day over the way she
turned over every decision she'd ever made in public -- that his presence had never been the point.
He had simply stood where a witness was needed, the way strangers sometimes do on the days that
matter most, adding one more pair of eyes to a room that could not, after that day, ever be told it
hadn't seen what it saw.

She won the seat that same year. Not because six men changed their minds. Because a whole hall full
of people had already watched her refuse to ask them to.
