# The Night He Was Afraid For Him

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1739`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle X — the Aethelgard strand's fourth entry, set strictly pre-Book-1, years before the
Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`). Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, a lord and not yet a king; Ozmund is
old enough to hold a real conversation with his father as something closer to an equal. Narrated by
Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructing a story told to him only once,
matching the strand's established retrospective register. A private, vulnerable entry: Aethelgard
voices a generalized, non-specific fear about what his son's nature will eventually cost him — deliberately
atmospheric, not a premonition of the actual Fulfillment Ceremony assassination, which this entry does
not foreshadow directly. No new named characters.*

---

This is the one he told me only once, and only because I'd had too much of the Golden Terror-era
brandy he liked to keep around for exactly that purpose, and asked him, carelessly, whether his father
had ever seemed afraid of him. Not afraid of what he could do. Afraid *for* him. He went very still in
a way I recognized by then, the way he went still before telling me something he'd decided, sometime
long before I asked, that he would only ever say once.

It was late, he said, later than a lord with a country to run had any real business being awake, and
he'd come to find his father in the one room of the house Aethelgard kept for himself and almost
nobody else — a small study that smelled permanently of pipe smoke he never actually smoked anymore,
just kept the pipe for, unlit, turning it over in his hands while he thought. The boy had come in with
some question he no longer remembered by the time he told me the story, something small and
forgettable, and found his father sitting in the dark without a lamp lit, which wasn't like him.

Aethelgard didn't send him away. He told him to sit, and for a while neither of them said anything at
all, which the General said was itself unusual — his father filled silences, as a rule, with dry
observations or bad puns or questions about the boy's day, and the not-talking went on long enough
that the boy started to understand something was actually wrong before his father said a word about
it.

What Aethelgard finally said, when he said it, wasn't about any threat he could name. The General was
very clear about that, all these years later, the one time he told it — his father named nothing,
suspected no one specifically, had no plot or plan or enemy in mind that night. What he said, as near
as the General could still remember the exact words, was something closer to this: that he'd spent his
son's whole life teaching him what to do with a thing neither of them had asked for, and had gotten
good enough at that particular teaching that he'd almost stopped noticing the larger fact sitting
underneath it — that the world does not, as a general rule, leave uniquely dangerous, uniquely valuable
things alone simply because their bearer has learned to be careful with them. That a boy who is taught
young enough to carry something enormous without breaking anything around him can come to seem, even
to the people who love him most, like a boy who doesn't need protecting. And that this was, Aethelgard
said, very close to the most dangerous lie a father could ever let himself believe about his own son.

He said his father reached over at that point and took the pipe apart to clean it, an old habit he
did with his hands when he needed them occupied, and said that he didn't know what shape it would take
— the danger, whatever it eventually was — and that not knowing was its own particular kind of
unbearable, worse in its way than knowing something specific and being able to prepare against it.
"I can teach you what to do with the Spike," he said. "I've spent your whole life teaching you that.
I have no idea how to teach you what to do with being the kind of thing men will eventually decide is
worth killing you over, or using you for, or both. I don't know that lesson. I never had to learn it
myself. And it keeps me up more nights than I'd ever admit to your mother's people, who'd tell me I
worry too much about a boy who could flatten this entire house without trying."

The General told me his father didn't ask anything of him that night — no promise, no instruction, none
of the usual shape their conversations took. Just sat with him a while longer in the dark, the pipe
back together and still unlit, and then, before he sent him to bed, said one more thing, quieter: that
whatever came, he hoped his son would remember that being afraid for someone and doubting them were
two entirely different things, and that everything Aethelgard had ever taught him had come from the
first, never the second. The General said he didn't fully understand what his father had meant by that
distinction until he was much older. By then there was no one left to ask.
