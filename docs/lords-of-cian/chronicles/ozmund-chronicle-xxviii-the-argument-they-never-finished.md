# The Argument They Never Finished

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1757`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XXVIII — the Aethelgard strand's wave-two third entry, set strictly pre-Book-1, in
Ozmund's later adolescence, years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`). Aethelgard Verehimu is
alive, a lord and not yet a king. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructing a story told to him long after Aethelgard's death, matching the strand's established
retrospective register. A real, unresolved father-son disagreement — about the pace at which Ozmund
is given genuine responsibility rather than protected discipline — with both positions left standing
on their own merits rather than one being proven right by the scene's end. No new named characters.*

---

Most of what the General told me about his father resolved somewhere, even when the resolution took
him decades to see. This one didn't, and he told it to me plainly as exactly that — not a story with a
lesson folded into the end of it, but an argument, complete, that the two of them simply never finished
having.

He was old enough by then to sit in rooms his father used to send him out of, old enough to be shown
the shape of most of what governing the House actually involved. What he wasn't given, and had started
to notice he wasn't given, was any of it with real weight attached — no decision that could go wrong in
a way that mattered, no responsibility handed to him that his father wasn't quietly standing close
enough to correct before the consequence landed. He'd been drilled, tested, disciplined, examined from
every angle available to a father who took the raising of him as seriously as Aethelgard did. He had
not, in all that time, been trusted.

The argument came to a head, the General said, over something almost embarrassingly small — a border
dispute with a neighboring holding, the kind of matter that came across Aethelgard's desk a dozen times
a year and rarely amounted to anything worse than an afternoon of tedious correspondence. The boy asked
to handle it himself, start to finish, without his father standing over the letters he wrote or the
terms he offered. Aethelgard said no. Not harshly — the General was careful about that, every time he
told it — but flatly, with none of the usual explanation that came with his refusals, which the boy
took, correctly, as a sign his father had expected the request and already decided against it before it
was even asked.

What followed wasn't a shouting match. The General said his father didn't raise his voice in anger more
than a handful of times in the whole of his life, and this wasn't one of them. It was worse than that,
in its way — a long, level exchange in which neither of them backed down an inch, and both of them, the
General told me, were right.

His own argument was the one he'd clearly turned over many times before he ever said it aloud: that
being kept from real stakes wasn't discipline, whatever it called itself. Discipline taught you what to
do with a mistake. What he was being given instead was a life with no mistakes in it at all, because
every mistake he might have made had already been quietly prevented before it could happen, which meant
he was becoming powerful in private and rehearsed in public, and that the gap between those two things
would not close on its own simply because he turned some agreed-upon age. If anything, he said, it would
get worse the longer it went unaddressed, because the first real mistake he ever made would then have to
happen in front of everyone, at the exact moment it could least afford to be a first one.

Aethelgard's answer wasn't a denial of any of that. The General was insistent on this point, decades
later, in a voice that still carried something like respect for the argument he'd lost the chance to
finish. His father agreed, in substance, that a life with no real stakes in it couldn't be called
preparation for anything. What he disagreed with — what he couldn't get past, and said so plainly — was
the assumption sitting underneath his son's argument, that a mistake made by an ordinary heir and a
mistake made by a boy who could, badly handled, level a wing of the house without meaning to were the
same category of risk simply requiring the same category of trust. "Every other father in this House's
history got to let his son fail small, in private, a hundred times before it mattered," Aethelgard told
him. "I don't get that. I don't know yet what 'small' even means for you, and I am not going to guess
wrong with the whole of the Wetlands watching to see how I answer for it. I know that costs you
something. I haven't finished deciding what it costs me to keep asking you to pay it."

The General said neither of them had an answer to the other's answer. His father didn't order the
conversation closed, the way he sometimes did when a disagreement had run its useful course. He simply
let it end there, unfinished, and neither of them raised it again in quite those terms — not because it
had been settled, the General was firm about this, but because some arguments, between two people who
trust each other enough to have them honestly, don't need to be won to be worth having.

He told me he thought about it for the rest of his life, in both directions at once — that his father
had been right to be afraid of guessing wrong about him in public, and that he himself had been right
that the fear, left unanswered long enough, becomes its own kind of failure. He said it was the reason,
much later, once command of real people and real consequences was finally his to give, he never once
made anyone under him wait the way he'd been made to wait — not because his father had been wrong to
make him, but because he never wanted to find out, in someone else's life, how long is too long before
you stop asking and simply start proving it.
