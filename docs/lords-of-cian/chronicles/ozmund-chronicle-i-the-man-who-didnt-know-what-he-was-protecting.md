I never met the boy Ozmund was. By the time he found me I was already grown, already Cestari,
already worth the trouble of finding. Everything I know about the years before that came from him,
in pieces, over decades, mostly late and mostly reluctant — and one story in particular he told only
once, the night he formally gave Draconis the House Guard's outer command and I asked him why a man
who trusted almost no one trusted that one so completely. He told it to me like a confession. I've
always told it back the way he told it to me.

He was a boy on a road he had no business being on, is where it starts. Aethelgard Verehimu believed
a lord who never left his walls forgot what the walls were for, and so twice a year he took his son
out past them — to the grain markets, the river locks, the districts where the Scrip changed hands
in coins worn thin enough to see through. It was on the second such trip, in a column of six that
should have needed no more protection than a closed carriage and good manners, that a dozen men came
out of a collapsed grain-store with knives and one badly-maintained crossbow, and decided that the
Verehimu name riding past them that afternoon was worth more than whatever they'd planned to steal
before it showed up.

There was a new man in that column. Viktor Draconis had been in the House Guard eleven months,
promoted twice on the strength of drills nobody had ever seen him lose, and given this posting —
outer escort, nothing glamorous, a boy and his father and a short ride home — because his commander
wanted to see what he did with something that mattered less than it turned out to matter. He put
himself between the carriage and the first knife before anyone else had finished shouting. He put
himself between it and the second, and the third, moving the way men move who have drilled a
thing so many times it no longer feels like choosing. He took a cut across the forearm doing it and
never once looked down at it. By the time the column's other four had the rest boxed and bleeding,
Draconis alone had put three men on the ground who would not be getting up that day, and he had done
it standing directly over a boy he had known for eleven months and would, from that afternoon
forward, have died for without being asked twice.

What the General told me — and told me only that once, low, like it still cost him something twenty
years later — was what he did during it, which was nothing. He said he felt it the moment the first
man came through the grain-store door: the Spike, awake the way it was always awake, no different
from breathing, ready the way it had been ready since before he could form the memory of it not
being ready. One motion. He could have ended all twelve of them before Draconis's blade cleared its
sheath, and no one on that road would ever have known his name was worth more than the coin they'd
come to steal.

He didn't move. He told me he made himself small on the carriage floor — genuinely small, genuinely
afraid, the fear real even if the danger to him personally never was — because some instinct he
didn't yet have words for understood that if Draconis knew what he was protecting, he would stop
protecting it the same way. A man doesn't throw himself between a god and a knife. He throws himself
between a boy and a knife because the boy needs him to. The General let him need it.

Aethelgard reached the carriage before the last man was down, having come from farther back in the
column at a dead run, and what he found was his son unmarked, shaking, and alive, and a soldier eleven
months in his service standing over three bodies with a cut arm he still hadn't looked at. He named
Draconis to the House Guard's inner command that same evening, in front of the whole column, and said
— the General remembered the words exactly, all these years later — "You protected what mattered
before you knew what it was worth. That's the only kind of loyalty I'll ever trust with him." Draconis
took it as praise for keeping a merchant lord's heir alive. He had no way to hear it the other way.

The General never told him. Not that night, not in the twenty years I knew him, not as far as I ever
learned. He let Draconis believe, decade after decade, that the maddest thing he'd ever done — walking
into three knives for a boy who by every visible measure could have run — was also the bravest, rather
than the only thing that had ever mattered less than it seemed to. And whatever the General's reasons
were the day he chose that silence, by the time he told me the story he'd stopped needing a reason at
all. It had simply become the truest thing he knew about the man: that Draconis would still have moved
first, every time, even if he'd known. The General just preferred not to spend the only proof of that
he was ever going to get.
