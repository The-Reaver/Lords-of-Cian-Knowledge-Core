# What the Flood Interrupted

The river came up two feet in an hour, well past anything the low district's drainage had ever been
built to hold, and Kanja closed the ledger mid-sentence the moment the first runner reached the yard
with word of it.

There was no debate about what came next. The tally sheets, the pen, the careful cross-checked
figures — all of it went into a waxed satchel Ezio had learned, weeks ago, to keep sealed against
exactly this kind of weather, and every hand in the yard, Kanja's included, turned toward the
riverside tenements instead, where the water was already climbing the first floor's doorframes.

He carried sacks of the district's stored grain to high ground for three hours before he carried a
single person, because the grain would starve the district slower and less visibly than the flood
would drown it faster, and by the time the worst of the water had crested, he had carried both — an
old man too unsteady to wade on his own, then twin girls passed hand over hand along a chain of
workers standing waist-deep to keep the current from taking them, then sacks again, until the sun
came up on a district soaked through but nobody lost.

He did not open the ledger again until the following afternoon, once the water had receded enough
for the streets to be walked instead of waded, and when he did, the line waiting to give testimony was
longer than it had been before the flood, not shorter.

"You didn't ask anyone their wages last night," an old dockhand said, taking her place in the line.
"You just worked."

"There wasn't a question that mattered more than the water right then."

"That's the thing." She said it plainly, without performance, the way people said things they'd only
decided were true after watching for proof. "Every other book-carrying official who's come through
this district in my lifetime stood on dry ground and asked us to trust their numbers. You were up to
your waist in the river before you ever asked us anything. I'll give you my figure. I'd have given it
to you before, too, if I'm honest. But I believe it means something now, coming from you, in a way it
didn't yesterday morning."

The wage tally that followed ran no differently than any other district's — the same careful
questions, the same cross-checked entries — but it opened faster and closer to complete than any
before it, credibility bought not by the ledger's method at all, but by the hours spent, unarmed and
undocumented, before the ledger was ever opened again.
