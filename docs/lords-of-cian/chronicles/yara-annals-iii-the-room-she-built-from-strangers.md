# Yara Annals III: The Room She Built From Strangers

*Locked canon, Batch 163, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-510`). Homage-era Batey, Yara/Yalokona. Territory
Annals entry — Yalokona as protagonist, Kanja an unnamed background guest with no command, credit, or

*Corrected Batch 339, 2026-10-02: the paragraph describing "Caucus" as an ambient felt-equal-weight

The flood-control proposal needed support from four groups that had never once sat in the same room
by choice — small business owners worried about construction disruption, tenant associations worried
about displacement, dockworkers worried about job security, and a clergy coalition worried about
nothing being asked of them but their blessing after the fact. Yalokona brought all four into one
room anyway, certain the proposal's real chance depended on it.

She did not force agreement, and nothing in the room did it for her. What she brought was the thing
Yara had learned to count on from her: once the four of them reached terms at her table, none of them
would be able to walk the agreement back quietly afterward, in a side room, the way every one of them
had walked back every previous promise to the other three. Knowing that changed how they bargained.
The business owners, accustomed to being courted separately and flattered into compliance, found
themselves genuinely listening to a dockworker's concern for the first time in years, because whatever
they conceded would hold. The clergy coalition, accustomed to being consulted last, found itself asked
first.

"This isn't how these meetings usually go," one business owner admitted, several hours in, visibly
disoriented by a negotiation where nobody's voice was structured to dominate. "Normally somebody's
just waiting for it to be their turn to actually matter."

"That's exactly the problem I built this room to solve." Yalokona kept the four groups working
through the proposal's specifics rather than its politics, each concession from one side matched by a
concession from another, until a version emerged that gave every group something real rather than a
version any single group could have negotiated alone. "None of you would have reached this agreement
separately. You'd have reached four different agreements, each one certain the others didn't matter as
much as its own."

The flood-control measure passed within the month, its final language carrying fingerprints from all
four groups rather than one imposed on the rest — a coalition built entirely from people who'd arrived
strangers to each other's concerns and left, if not allies exactly, at least genuinely heard.

"You didn't convince anyone of anything," a fellow organizer observed afterward, still working out
what had actually happened in that room. "You just made sure everyone actually listened."

"That's usually all it takes. Most disagreements survive because nobody's ever actually made to sit
still and hear the other side plainly. Once that happens, the agreement often finds itself." Yalokona
reviewed the finished measure, satisfied without claiming more credit than the process deserved.

An unnamed man near the room's edge, present throughout the long session, left once the agreement was
finalized.
