# Kazi Chronicle V: What He Read Wrong

Tunji had called the scaffolding complaint green for three weeks running, and for three weeks running
he had been wrong.

Green meant early — a grievance real enough to be true but not yet ripe enough to organize around
without burning people who weren't ready to be burned. He had watched the crew working the overhead
gantries grumble about a missing third safety line for a month, watched them keep working anyway, and
read that as patience rather than what it actually was: a fuse, laid flat, that he had mistaken for
calm because it hadn't yet found its spark.

The spark came on a Tuesday he hadn't scheduled for anything. A rigger named Juma — three years on the
gantries, known for nothing in particular — lost his footing reaching
for a line that should have been there and wasn't, caught himself on a strut hard enough to crack two
ribs, and the whole gantry crew walked off the same hour, together, with none of the groundwork Tunji
had spent a month deliberately not laying.

He found out from Zola, breathless, before he'd finished his own shift two buildings over. "They're
already at the gate. Nobody called them out. They just went."

"I told them three weeks ago this wasn't ready." Tunji said it without blame, mostly at himself. "I
was wrong about what ready meant. I thought it meant when I judged it safe to move. It meant when a
man's ribs finally told them what I should have already known."

There was no standing readiness waiting for this the way there had been for every walkout Tunji had
ever actually built toward. No vetted names already counted, no quiet word already passed to the two
or three people every crew needed in place before a walkout could hold instead of scatter apart within
the hour. He had six gantry riggers standing exposed at a gate with a foreman already threatening
replacements, and nothing under them.

He did not have the luxury, that afternoon, of doing it his own way. He picked the two riggers who
looked steadiest under the crowd's own noise — a read made in minutes instead of weeks, the kind of
read he had always distrusted precisely because it was fast — and asked them directly, in front of
everyone, to hold the gate until he came back with something worth holding it for. One of the two
turned out solid. The other, a man named Torvald who'd been on the gantries barely four months,
folded the second a supervisor offered him his job back alone, and nearly walked two others out with
him before Zola — vetted properly, months earlier, exactly the way Tunji had always meant to vet
everyone — talked them back into the line.

"That's the part I got wrong twice," Tunji said afterward, once the plant had agreed to a real
third-line inspection across every gantry in Kazi rather than only the one that had already broken
Juma's ribs to prove its point. "Once, calling it green when it wasn't. Twice, thinking that if I ever
had to move fast, I could still read a man right in five minutes standing at a gate. I can't. Fast
reads are guesses wearing my method's clothes. I got lucky that only one of the two guesses was wrong,
and luckier still that Zola was already standing where five weeks of patience had put her."

A man who had been unloading crates near the gate the whole time helped carry Juma to a cart bound for
the infirmary, said nothing to the crowd either way, and was gone from the yard before the plant
manager arrived to hear the demand read out loud.

"I don't think the lesson is to move faster," Tunji said, days later, walking the gantries himself for
the first time in months. "I think the lesson is that I don't get to decide, alone, when something's
ready. I only get to decide when I'm ready to have already done the work. The floor decides the rest,
in its own time, whether I've finished or not."
