# The Night He Didn't Have a Plan

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1786`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series, Chronicle LVII, Draconis strand wave 3.*

---

Of every story I ever heard the General tell about Draconis, this is the only one he told me with his hands moving the whole way through it, as though some part of him needed to act it out again to get it said. I understood, watching him, that this was the story underneath most of the others — the one that had taught him, more than any near-discovery or quiet confession ever had, exactly what his silence might one day cost him.

It happened on a border road, years on from Brenner's own wound, on an inspection run Draconis had insisted on riding personally rather than delegating, the way he'd taken to doing more often as he'd aged into the habit of trusting fewer things to fewer other men. The column was smaller than it should have been for the ground it was crossing — a scout's report of trouble further south had pulled half the usual escort away that same week — and the raiders who found them in a stretch of broken hill country had clearly known it, and had brought, unlike the men on the road all those years before, more than knives.

The General told me he felt the first bolt before he heard it loosed, the way he always felt danger arrive a half-breath ahead of its sound, and that in that half-breath he made, without deciding to, the same calculation he had made every single time since the road: one motion, total, instant, every attacker down before the string had finished its release. He told me he had never once let himself follow that calculation all the way through before, because he had never once needed to weigh it against what actually happened next, which was that the bolt meant for him took Draconis instead, three feet to his left and moving to close the distance the instant the first arrow left its string, the way the man had been closing distances between the General and every danger that had ever found him for the whole of both their lives.

He went down hard, the General said, and did not get up, and the ground beneath him went dark faster than any wound the General had ever watched a man take and still, somehow, breathe through.

What happened in the minutes after, the General told me plainly, was the one time in his entire life he acted without a plan of any kind — not the careful stillness of the road, not the practiced restraint of the reception hall, nothing rehearsed or reasoned or chosen in advance. He fought the remaining raiders the fastest way he could without ending it in the single motion that would have answered every question anyone watching might ever have asked about him again, and told me afterward he could not, even trying, fully reconstruct how he'd made himself hold that line in the middle of it — only that some part of him had kept doing it even while the rest of him was entirely occupied with the sound Draconis was making, low and wrong, in the dirt behind him.

He reached him within a minute of the last raider going down, he told me — less time than it sounds, and longer than any minute he had ever lived through before or since. The wound was low and deep and bleeding the way the physicians later told him should have killed a man twice as young inside a quarter hour. He pressed his own hands to it, bare, and held them there through the whole of the ride back, and carried Draconis the last stretch himself when the horse could no longer manage both of them at the pace the wound demanded — a pace, he admitted to me, no ordinary man's legs should have sustained across that ground, explained afterward by everyone who heard the account as nothing stranger than a man terrified enough to outrun his own limits for one desperate hour, which was, he told me, close enough to the truth that no one ever thought to ask a second question about it.

Draconis lived. He carried the scar for the rest of his life, and a weakness in that side that never fully left him, and woke three days later to be told, by physicians who genuinely believed it, that clean luck and a fast horse had saved him. He never once suspected otherwise. The General told me that in the weeks after, lying awake the way he had that one earlier night, he understood with a clarity he'd never quite reached before exactly how close the silence he'd kept for so many years had come to costing him the one man he had never had to be careful with — and that for the first time, turning the question over, he no longer had a ready answer waiting for him.
