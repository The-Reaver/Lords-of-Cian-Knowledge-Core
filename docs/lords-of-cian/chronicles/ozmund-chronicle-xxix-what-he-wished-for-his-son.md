Almost everything in this account reached me the same way — from the General himself, across years of
brandy and long nights and the particular kind of trust that takes decades to build and no time at all
to lose. This one didn't. This one I had from Hesper Vale, the House physician who had tended him since
before he could hold his own head up, on the one occasion he ever brought me to meet her, long after
his father was gone and long after the man I called the General had become someone the rest of the
world called by other names entirely.

She was old by then, though nowhere near as frail as her age should have made her, and she looked at me
for a long moment before she decided, apparently on her own authority and without asking him first,
that I was worth telling this to. I've always suspected he brought me to her precisely so that she
would.

The night she described was an ordinary one, or ordinary enough — she'd come to check on the boy after
a fever that had worried nobody by morning, the kind of visit a House physician makes a hundred times
in a childhood and remembers none of individually, except that this one happened to end with her
sitting up late with Aethelgard afterward, sharing a cup of something while the boy slept upstairs, in
the particular quiet that settles over a house once everyone in it has finally stopped being afraid for
an evening. She said Aethelgard asked her, half in earnest and half as the kind of question tired
parents ask physicians at that hour without expecting a real answer, what she hoped for the boy she'd
looked after since his very first breath.

She told him. I don't know exactly what she said, because she didn't tell me that part — only that it
made him laugh, the real kind, and that he'd said something like *good, keep hoping that,* before
turning the question back on himself, unprompted, the way she said he rarely did with anyone.

What he told her, she said, wasn't grand. She was firm about that, decades later, as though it still
mattered to her that I understood it correctly. He didn't wish for his son to be great, or safe, or
powerful, because by then it was already plain the boy would be at least two of those three whether
anyone wished it or not, and the third had never once been in question. What Aethelgard said he hoped
for, in the plain unremarkable words of a father talking too late at night to someone he trusted not to
repeat them carelessly, was smaller than that, and harder. That his son would learn to trust people
faster than his own good sense would ever advise him to, because a boy raised to be as careful as this
one was going to have to be would otherwise spend his whole life keeping everyone at the same wary
distance, and call it wisdom. That he would find, somewhere along the way, at least one person who
wanted nothing at all from what he could do — not his strength, not his name, not the House behind
either of them — and that when he found that person, he would have the sense to recognize it for the
rare thing it was, because Aethelgard said he wasn't at all certain he'd managed to teach him how to
tell the difference yet. That he would keep some small appetite in his life that belonged to nothing
but himself — a taste, a habit, some corner of a day that owed no one an account of it — because a life
spent entirely in service of a House, however well spent, was still a life spent entirely in service of
something other than the person living it. And that whatever power eventually came to him without a
leash on it, he would hold it the way you hold something borrowed and precious rather than something
you were owed, for exactly as long as it took to put it down again.

Hesper told me she'd never repeated any of that to him directly, in all the years afterward, not
because it was a secret exactly, but because she'd come to believe some things are truer left as a
memory only one other person carries, rather than handed over and turned into something the person
they were about has to carry too. She said she'd broken that rule exactly once, the night she told me,
and only because she'd decided, watching the two of us together, that his father's wish about finding
someone who wanted nothing from him had plainly already come true at least once, and she thought I
deserved to know it hadn't been an accident that it had — that a man who wished it hard enough, quietly,
long before there was any reason to, had perhaps gone some distance toward making it so.
