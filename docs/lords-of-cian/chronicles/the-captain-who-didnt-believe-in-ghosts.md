# The Captain Who Didn't Believe in Ghosts

first entry in the seventh wave. Rebellion era, age 21, some months after Ghost Harbor (`MCD-235`).
Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material — the alias's first genuine failure of its own
reputation. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

Most Trust captains who sighted *The Audit* found a reason to be elsewhere within the hour. This one
signaled full attack in broad daylight, canvas crowded on, and kept coming even after his own
lieutenant sent up the recognition flags twice to be certain the report wasn't a mistake.

"He's not slowing," Dol Maren said, watching the approach with the specific unease of someone whose
whole strategy depended on an enemy's nerve failing first. "He's not even hesitating."

"Then he doesn't believe the stories," Kanja said. "Or he's decided not caring is worth more to him
than believing them."

It turned out to be the second. The captain said as much himself over the short range his own guns
demanded, voice carrying flat and unimpressed across the closing water: "I've read every report filed
on your ship for two years. Half of them contradict the other half. A weapon that isn't there twice
the same way isn't a weapon a captain can plan around, so I stopped planning around it and started
planning around the actual hull in front of me. Ghosts don't have to reload."

There was no vanishing this one, no allowed sighting to let dissolve into rumor. The engagement that
followed was fought at close range and full commitment — the anchor-chain weapon deployed openly
rather than held as a surprise, Mafesto and Onyx and Obsidian Malice all working in sequence exactly
as they had against every real threat that had ever forced the question, because this captain had
correctly judged that the only way to beat a reputation was to refuse to be frightened by it and simply
fight the ship underneath.

He lost. His hull was disabled inside twenty minutes, the same way any hull would be against that much
combined force brought to bear without restraint — but he'd made Kanja spend all of it, openly, in
daylight, in front of a crew that would go home and report exactly what they'd actually seen rather
than what fear had let them imagine.

"You cost us the mystery," Kanja told him afterward, standing over the wreckage of the captain's own
strategy with something that wasn't quite anger. "Every sailor on your ship just watched precisely what
we are and precisely what we can do. That's worth more to the Trust than sinking us would have been."

"Then I did my job." The captain, disarmed and unbowed, met his eyes without flinching. "I was never
trying to beat you. I was trying to make sure the next captain who faces you does it with real
information instead of ghost stories."

Kanja let him go, and the report he filed afterward — precise, unembellished, stripped of every rumor
the fleet had spent two years cultivating — spread through Trust command faster and further than any
sighting ever had, proving, in its own quiet way, that the reputation had never been the only weapon.
