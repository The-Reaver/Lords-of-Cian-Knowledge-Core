# What He Never Reported

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1732`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle III. Set some years after Chronicle II, still strictly pre-Book-1 — Aethelgard
Verehimu alive, the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) still in the future. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn
Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructing a story "the General" told him as a genuine
confession rather than an anecdote. Dramatizes a real near-discovery of the Density Spike
(`MCD-024`/`CC-015`/`017`) by Colonel Viktor Draconis (`CC-085`) — the closest the pre-ceremony
window comes to breaking the "purity-test" ignorance the series has built — which Draconis
rationalizes away and never reports, deliberately left unresolved rather than advanced any further.
No new named characters.*

---

Of every story the General ever told me about Draconis, this is the only one he told me had cost
Draconis something to tell in return. He heard it from the man himself, decades later, on a night
neither of them had planned to speak honestly about anything, and he said Draconis's voice changed
partway through — went flatter, more careful, the way a man's voice goes when he is choosing each
word specifically to keep himself from saying the truer one sitting just behind it.

It started with a beam. The old sparring gallery at the House seat was being re-timbered that season,
half its roof stripped to the joists while the household trained in the half still standing, and
during a perfectly ordinary drill — blunted steel, a dozen guardsmen working forms in pairs, nothing
that should have troubled anyone — a support the masons had sworn was sound let go without warning and
brought four feet of green oak straight down over where the General stood mid-form, his partner
already flinching back and shouting a name that wasn't going to reach him in time.

It reached him anyway, Draconis told him, because Draconis was already moving — not toward him, he
was careful to say, there wasn't time for that, but toward the place he expected to find him after. What
he found instead was the General standing exactly where he'd been standing, unhurried, one shoulder
dusted with splinters, the beam itself cracked clean across where it had struck him and lying now at
his feet like something that had simply given up. No stumble. No flung arm, no half-second of the
body's own animal flinch that every man in that yard, veterans included, would have shown and did
show, all around him, at the sound alone. Just a slight frown, the General told me, the kind a man
gives a spilled cup — mild, brief, gone before anyone thought to read it as strange.

Draconis noticed. That was the thing the General wanted me to understand, telling it to me — not that
Draconis had seen something impossible, because plenty of men in that yard had seen the beam and the
lack of a mark on him and called it luck, or good bones, or the famous Kareth stubbornness bred into
him through his mother's line, which was a true thing and a real thing and not, that day, the true
reason at all. What Draconis noticed, being the kind of soldier he was, wasn't the beam. It was the
absence of fear. A man's body tells on him before his face does, in the half-instant before he has
time to compose it, and Draconis had spent thirty years around men in exactly that half-instant,
enough of them dying in it to know precisely what it was supposed to look like. The General's had
never arrived.

He didn't report it. He told the General, all those years on, exactly what he'd done instead — which
was quietly work the question over for the better part of a season and no more, the way a soldier
worries at a strap he suspects but can't prove has frayed. He asked the masons, offhand, what green
oak of that length ought to weigh and what it ought to do to a man beneath it, and didn't like the
answer, and asked no one else. He watched the General more closely in drills after that, waiting to
catch the same stillness twice, and did catch it, once, in a startled horse that should have thrown
him and didn't, and told himself that was luck compounding on luck the way it sometimes will. He
never wrote any of it down. There was no one above him in that household to report it to besides
Aethelgard himself, and the thought of standing in front of his lord to say I believe your son is not
entirely what he appears, with nothing under it but a cracked beam and a man's too-quiet face, felt to
him — he said this part plainly — less like duty than like betrayal, of a boy who had never once
given him a single other reason to doubt him.

So he let it close over, the way a wound closes over a splinter the body decides isn't worth the
digging. He told the General he still thought of it sometimes, on quiet duty nights, and still didn't
know, even confessing it out loud twenty years later, whether he'd made himself stop seeing it out of
wisdom or out of cowardice — whether a soldier's discretion and a frightened man's willful blindness
were, in the end, two different things or only two different names for the same choice.

The General never told him which. He told me he'd wanted to, in that moment, more than he'd wanted to
in years — that some part of him had been waiting his whole life for someone to get close enough on
their own that the truth would cost him nothing to give. But Draconis had asked the question of
himself, not of him, and the General decided, the way he decided most things, that the kindest answer
was the one that let another man keep the version of himself he could live inside. He let the beam
stay a beam. He let the horse stay a horse. And he told me, at the end of it, that he thought Draconis
had known that too, somewhere under the question he never quite let himself finish asking — and had
let it go unfinished on purpose, the same way the General let it go unanswered.
