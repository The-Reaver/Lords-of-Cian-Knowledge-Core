# One Table, Five Debts

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1834`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle CV, closing entry of the Wider Verehimu Household/Guard strand, wave 2. Set strictly
pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), in Ozmund's later adolescence — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund still
House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, who did not learn of this

Armsmaster Berrin Hollis, falconer Annis Fairweather, tutor Master Alric Fenmoor, stable hand Cobb, and
Sergeant Oswin Kade. Brings all five household/guard figures together in one scene for the first time

I did not learn of this evening from one telling. I learned it in pieces, across several years of
asking, from five different people who had no particular reason to think their own small memory of it
belonged to anyone else's — and it was only after I had gathered the last of the five accounts that I
understood I had been given the same night five times over, from five different chairs at what turned
out to be the same table.

It was, by every account, an unremarkable evening by the House's own standards — no feast, no
proclamation, nothing marked on any formal calendar of the Verehimu seat. Hollis remembered it as an
invitation delivered by a steward's boy, asking only that he come to a small room off the kitchens at
sundown, with no reason given. Fenmoor remembered much the same, and told me he had assumed, walking
over, that he was about to be dismissed from his post, having run out of other explanations for why a
lord's heir would want a private word with his old tutor and no one else present. Kade remembered
being pulled from a watch rotation early, with instructions that struck him as strange enough that he'd
half expected trouble. Annis remembered simply being told the General wanted to see her, and going,
she said, without much thought at all, the way she'd have gone if he'd asked for anything in those
years. Cobb, still working the stables then, remembered being the last to arrive, having had to wash
before he felt fit to be seen in the House proper at all.

What each of them found, arriving, was the other four already there, and no clear sense among any of
them why they'd been gathered together, until Ozmund himself came in and told them plainly, without
ceremony, that he had asked them there for no reason beyond wanting, once, to thank them each in the
same room rather than separately and in passing, as he had always done before. He told them he did not
intend to make a habit of it, and that he would understand if the whole arrangement felt strange to
them. He said only that he had come to understand, in recent years, how much of what he was becoming he
owed to people who had never been asked to raise him and had done it anyway, each in a different
corner of his life, without ever comparing notes with one another — and that it had struck him, thinking
it through, as a debt worth acknowledging in the open rather than carrying silently the way he carried
most things.

None of the five gave me a full account of what was actually said over that meal. Hollis remembered
being needled, gently, about a disarm from years before. Fenmoor remembered a moment of real laughter
when Kade admitted he'd never once understood a word of the Scrip mechanics Fenmoor had spent two years
drilling into the boy. Annis remembered Cobb asking her, entirely seriously, whether a stable hand could
ever learn to work a hawk, and her telling him yes, though it would take him the better part of a
decade to be any good at it. Cobb remembered, more than anything else, simply being at that table at
all — a stable hand seated beside an armsmaster and a sergeant and a tutor and a falconer, treated by
all four, for one evening, as if the distance between their stations had never mattered in the first
place.

What struck me most, gathering these five accounts across so many years apart, was that not one of
them described the evening as significant at the time. Kade called it pleasant. Hollis called it
overdue. It was only much later, each of them told me in their own way, that they came to understand
what it had actually been — not a reward, and not quite a farewell, since none of them left his service
that night or for years afterward, but a young man doing the one thing his nature seemed built to do
with a debt he felt he owed and could not repay in kind: naming it plainly, once, to the people who had
given it to him, before whatever came next in his life made an evening like that one impossible to
arrange again.

I asked each of them, in turn, whether they'd known, sitting at that table, what the boy they had each
helped raise would go on to become. All five gave me some version of the same answer. They hadn't
known. They hadn't needed to. They had simply been thanked, once, in the open, by someone who had never
been required to do it at all — and every one of them told me, in their own words, that it was enough.
