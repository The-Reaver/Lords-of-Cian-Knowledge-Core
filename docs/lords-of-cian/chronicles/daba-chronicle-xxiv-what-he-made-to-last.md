# Daba Chronicle XXIV: What He Made to Last

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1594`). Entry 24 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series.
Daba, now capable in his own right, forges something specific and meaningful for a member of 1804
for the first time -- the first fruit of the teaching thread `MCD-1591`-`1593` built, and a direct
step toward `MCD-1570`'s eventual craftsmanship spreading cell by cell through the network. Reuses
Ilse Corvane (introduced `MCD-1592`); no new named characters. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles
in batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

---

He had made things before this that worked. He had never made anything before this that he meant to
outlast him.

That was the distinction Daba turned over while he worked the last hour of it alone, the cellar
quiet except for the low patient sound of a fire that had stopped needing to be watched so closely.
Kanja had left him to it -- had left, in fact, three lessons ago, in the sense that mattered, though
he still came by most evenings to look at what Daba had made and say little, which by now was its
own kind of approval. Tonight he sat on the grain-cellar steps with his knees drawn up, watching, not
teaching, and Daba understood the difference without needing it explained.

The piece was for Ilse. He'd decided that back on the night her hand had caught the shard off his
first failed blade, though he hadn't told her so, and hadn't been ready to make good on the decision
until now. Not a weapon, not really, though it could serve as one if it had to. A folding set --
three blades sized for a medic's actual work, edges that would hold through a season of use without
needing to be resharpened against whatever she could find, the whole thing small enough to sit flat
in a coat lining the way his own first knife had been meant to. He'd built the reinforcing joints the
crude, fast way Kanja had shown him, the kind any cell's own hands could mend if it ever needed
mending, because he'd meant every word of the lesson that taught him that, and it would have been a
strange kind of hypocrisy to give her something that only he could fix.

He quenched it slowly, the way the very first broken trade-blade had taught him to, listening for a
ring that came back whole. It did.

He found her the next morning cataloguing bandage stock by lamplight, and set the folded case on the
table without ceremony, because ceremony had never been how anything moved between people in 1804.
She opened it, turned the smallest blade once under the light, and didn't say anything for long
enough that he began composing an apology out of old habit before he caught himself and let the
silence stand instead.

"You marked it," she said finally, running a thumb along a small worked notch near the hinge -- not
a name, nothing that could identify the case or its owner to anyone who found it on a body, just a
private mark he'd have known blind. He hadn't planned to explain it and didn't now.

"So you'd know which hands it came out of, if it ever mattered."

"It won't need to matter. It'll just need to cut." She closed the case, weighed it once in her palm,
and set it into the same coat pocket the ledger usually lived in, already treating it as
unremarkable, which was exactly the reception Daba had wanted and hadn't known how to ask for.

He found Kanja afterward on the cellar steps, banking the last of the coals for the night.

"First thing I've made that's actually out there now," Daba said. "Not scrap. Not a test piece.
Something somebody's carrying."

"How does it sit."

Daba thought about it honestly before answering, the way Kanja had taught him to answer anything
that mattered. "Heavier than I expected. Not the case. The fact of it." He looked at his own hands,
still faintly marked from a year of learning what they hadn't been born knowing. "Everything I've
built before this, I built to disappear the moment it did its work. This one, I built to still be
there in ten years, in somebody else's pocket, doing something I'll never see."

Kanja banked the last coal and let the cellar go dark by degrees. "That's most of what teaching is,"
he said. "Making something that keeps working after you've stopped watching it."

Daba said nothing to that, only nodded once in the near-dark, and understood that he had just been
told, as plainly as Kanja ever told him anything, exactly what the last year had actually been for.
