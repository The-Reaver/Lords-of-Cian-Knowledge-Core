# The Boy Who Learned to Bow

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1826`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCVII, Wider Household/Guard strand wave 2. Set strictly pre-Book-1, in Ozmund's later
adolescence (roughly age eighteen), years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard
alive, Ozmund still the House heir. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from an account the General gave him directly, continuing the register established in
Chronicles XLIV and XCIII. A second, deeper test of Cobb's friendship against station — this time a
genuine fork in Cobb's own life rather than a single evening's discomfort — without resolving his
ultimate later fate. No new named characters.*

---

The General told me this one last of everything he ever gave me about Cobb, and told me, before he
began it, that it was the last one he had — that whatever came after belonged to a part of both their
lives he'd never gone looking to reopen, for reasons he still wasn't willing to name even that many
years on.

Kade had put the offer forward, not the General — that detail mattered to him, and he repeated it more
than once while telling me the story, as if anxious I not mistake it for something he'd arranged
himself. The House Guard needed a mounted courier, a position that carried real standing, real pay, and
real distance from the stable yard — long postings, message runs to holdings a day's ride and more away,
a life built around horses still but no longer built around any one horse, or any one household's
familiar ground. Kade had noticed Cobb's hands with animals years before anyone thought to make
anything formal of it, and put his name forward the moment the post opened.

The General said Cobb came to him before accepting, which surprised him — not because Cobb needed
permission, which he plainly didn't, but because Cobb seemed to think he owed him the conversation
regardless. He said Cobb sat with him in the stable yard, in the exact spot where the two of them had
sat as boys, and asked him straight out whether taking the post would be a betrayal of something neither
of them had ever put a name to.

He told me his answer took him longer to find than he expected, given how easy the right answer should
have been. Part of it, he admitted, was simple loss — Cobb gone on long postings meant a version of
their friendship that had survived every other test the years had put to it finally, genuinely changing
shape, and he said he hadn't fully accounted, until that moment, for how much of his own steadiness over
the years had quietly depended on knowing that stable yard would still hold the one relationship in his
life that asked nothing of him but honesty.

What he told Cobb, in the end, was the truth as he understood it: that a friendship built on two people
staying in the same place their whole lives wasn't a friendship worth defending, it was just proximity
wearing the name, and that if he ever caught himself wanting to keep Cobb small and close purely so his
own life felt less changed by it, that would be the actual betrayal — of Cobb, not of anything the two
of them had built together. He said Cobb took the post within the month.

The formal leave-taking, when it came, required exactly the kind of bow the General had once watched
cost Cobb something at that long-ago dinner — full formal address, in front of the assembled Guard, a
courier's oath sworn to the House rather than to any one man in it. He told me he watched Cobb give it
without a flicker of the old discomfort, standing straighter than he'd ever seen him stand in a stable
yard, and understood, watching it, that the bow itself had stopped being a wall between them sometime in
the years since that dinner — that Cobb had made his peace with the forms long before the General had
fully made his own, and had simply been waiting, patiently, for him to catch up.

They said their real goodbye afterward, at the same side door as before, with none of the formal
language either of them was capable of by then avoiding when they chose to. The General told me he never
gave Cobb a title in that doorway, and Cobb never gave him one either. He told me that was the last time
he saw him for a great many years, and that he'd never fully explained to himself, even by the time he
told me any of this, why he'd let that particular door stay closed as long as he had, when nothing about
their parting had ever suggested it needed to be.
