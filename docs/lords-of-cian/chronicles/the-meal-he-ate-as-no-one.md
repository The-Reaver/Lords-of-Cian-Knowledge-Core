# The Meal He Ate as No One

*Locked canon, Batch 200, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-825`). The Scourge Alias Chronicle XL, wave 14 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The tavern sat two streets back from the harbor, ordinary and crowded, and he took a corner table in
a plain traveler's coat with the Forge-Coat folded in a sea-chest aboard ship, unrecognized by anyone
in a room where at least three separate conversations, over the course of one meal, found their way
around to the same subject.

"My cousin swears he saw the black sails off the northern cape last month," a dockhand told his table,
low and thrilled, the way people told stories they wanted to be true more than they were sure of it.
"Says the eyes glow like a demon's. Says he can hear a man's fear before the man knows he's afraid."

"That's not what I heard," a woman at the next table corrected, equally certain. "I heard he doesn't
speak at all. Just points, and whatever he points at burns."

He ate his meal slowly, listening without comment, cataloguing the drift with the same flat attention
he gave any operational report — none of it true, exactly, and none of it entirely a lie either,
each version some fragment of an actual night reshaped by three tellings and a listener's own need
for the story to be larger than a man doing a difficult, specific job.

An old sailor two tables over, quieter than the rest, offered the only account that made him pause
mid-bite. "I was on a transport he boarded, years back. Wasn't any of that. Just a man who moved fast
and didn't waste a single motion, and afterward helped carry the wounded belowdecks same as anybody
else would. Whole crew talked about the smoke and the eyes for years after. I always thought the part
worth remembering was that he checked on us before he checked on the cargo manifest."

Nobody at the old sailor's table seemed especially interested in that version. The demon-eyed cousin
story clearly traveled better.

He finished his meal and paid in plain coin, unremarked, unrecognized, and left the tavern turning
over a small, strange satisfaction he hadn't expected to feel — not pride in the legend, which had
gotten nearly everything wrong, but something quieter, aimed at one old sailor's plain, accurate
memory sitting mostly ignored at the edge of a room that preferred the version with the glowing eyes.

Some debts, he thought, walking back toward the harbor unseen, got remembered correctly by exactly
the people who'd actually been there to need them settled — which was, in the end, the only audience
the truth had ever really been for.
