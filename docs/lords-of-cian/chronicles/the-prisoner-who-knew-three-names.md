# The Prisoner Who Knew Three Names

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Scourge Alias Chronicle L, wave 17. Rebellion era,
age 27. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

The officer they pulled out of the convoy's strongroom had commanded garrison troops against Bane
at two separate sieges before his transfer to slaving-escort duty, and he recognized the fighting
rhythm before he recognized the face — the way a strike would come in disarming rather than killing
when killing was the easier option, the same restraint he'd watched cost the Trust a fortress two
years earlier.

"You're the same one," he said, once the fighting had stopped and Kanja crouched to cut his bonds
rather than leave that to a subordinate. "Bane. The one from the Furnace District before that, if the
stories line up right. And now this." He studied the coat, the Trinity relics still humming down from
the fight. "Three names. One man wearing all of them."

Kanja didn't answer directly. He finished cutting the rope instead.

"I'm not fool enough to think you'll confirm it," the officer went on, "and I'm not fool enough to
think I could prove it if you denied it. But I know what I felt in that room, and I've felt it twice
before."

"Then you have a choice most people in your position never get," Kanja said. "Say it, and become the
officer who claims one rebel is secretly three legends — believed by no one who matters, hunted by
everyone who does, on both sides, for the rest of a career that ends the day someone decides you're
more useful discredited than alive. Or say nothing, and walk out of here a man who survived a
slaving-convoy raid and lost a promotion he was owed anyway."

"That's not mercy. That's arithmetic."

"It's both. I'm not going to insult you by pretending it isn't."

The officer was quiet a long moment, turning the calculation over the way a man turns over a coin
he already knows will come up the same side twice. "The arithmetic holds," he said finally. "For
now."

He kept his silence for the rest of his life, and never once, in later years, corrected anyone who
assumed he'd simply lost that fight to an ordinary pirate captain with an ordinary reputation.
