# What He Owed Outside the Ledger

*Locked canon, Batch 242, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1062`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LXI, wave 21,
first entry. Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. A cost of the alias's own founding battle that no
tally can repay. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

She came to Warehouse Twelve on an ordinary tally afternoon, waited three hours in the line of
workers with grievances, and when her turn came she didn't have a wage dispute at all.

"You flooded the Kessic flats," she said. "To drown the column before it reached the dredge line."

Kanja set down the sheet he'd been checking. "I did."

"My father's grove was on the Kessic flats. Sixty years of grafted root stock, planted by his
father before him. It's silt now. Salt-killed, most of it — the water came in off the canal's brack
end, not the fresh channel." She wasn't angry, or not only angry; she'd clearly rehearsed the
calm of it the way the clerk in the district had once rehearsed a confession. "Two hundred soldiers
never reached the workers they were sent to punish. I know what that bought. I watched three
families in this district keep their sons because of it. I'm not here to say it wasn't worth
doing."

"Then why are you here."

"Because nobody ever wrote it down. Every wage you've ever gotten back for anyone in this district
has a number on it somewhere, in Garren Hask's hand, checked twice. My grove doesn't have a number
anywhere. It isn't on anyone's ledger that the flood that saved this place also ended something.
I wanted it to be on one."

Garren Hask, two tables over, had already stopped pretending not to listen. He didn't reach for a
sheet. There was nothing to enter — no owner to charge, no wage to trace, no theft with a figure at
the bottom of it. The entire method the district had come to trust ran on the premise that a wrong
could be measured and then made whole to the coin. This wasn't that kind of wrong.

Kanja didn't offer her a number, because he didn't have one that would have meant anything, and
he'd learned by now that a district could smell a hollow figure from across a warehouse. He offered
labor instead — his crew's hands, seasons of them if it took seasons, clearing the salted rows and
carrying in fresh soil from upriver, grafting new stock from whatever cuttings of the old line still
survived elsewhere in her family. He said it plainly: it wouldn't be her father's sixty years back.
Nothing he could put his hands to would be. He wasn't offering to fix it. He was offering to be the
one who didn't pretend it hadn't happened.

"That's not nothing," she said. "But it's not what I came for, either."

"What did you come for?"

"I told you already. I wanted it written down somewhere that wasn't just my own memory."

He asked Hask for a fresh page — not the tally ledger, a separate one, with no owner's name at the
top and no repayment schedule beneath it. He wrote the grove down himself, in his own hand, plain:
what it had been, what the flood had cost it, and that the flood had also been necessary, both
things true on the same line without either one canceling the other. He didn't sign it as the
Trench Monarch. He signed it plainly, with the name the district had never chalked on anything —
his own.

She read it twice before she folded it away. She didn't thank him for it, and he didn't expect her
to. Some debts, he understood walking back to the tally table, weren't the kind that closed. They
were only the kind that got carried honestly, by someone willing to be the one who carried them.
