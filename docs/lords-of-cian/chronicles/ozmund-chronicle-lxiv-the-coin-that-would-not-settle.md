He told me this one lightly, more amused than anything else, though I came to understand later that
the amusement was doing some work — that it was easier for him to tell a story about his father being
outmatched by an old boundary dispute than it would have been to tell most of the others straight.

The Rell and Tamsy families had been arguing over the same half-acre of Wetlands scrub for three
generations by the time the General was old enough to sit in the room where it finally reached his
father directly rather than being handled, as it usually was, by whichever district steward drew the
short straw that season. Neither family had ever come to blows over it, as far as anyone could recall,
but neither had they ever once let it rest either — a slow, patient, generational grievance that
resurfaced every few years like a tide, always over the exact same half-acre, always with both sides
equally certain the land had always been theirs.

This time it had come to Aethelgard because both patriarchs, aging men by then and apparently tired
enough of the argument to want it settled in their own lifetimes rather than handed down again, had
requested him personally. The General said his father brought the coin. Not out of any particular plan,
he thought — simply habit, the same worn half-scrip that had spun on a hundred tables across the boy's
own childhood, and Aethelgard had learned, over the years, that two men too angry to hear each other
rarely got less angry by being told to calm down, but often did by being given something with weight to
watch fall instead.

It worked, in its way. The General said both old men went quiet the moment the coin started spinning,
the same way he himself always had, and by the time it clattered flat neither of them was shouting
anymore. Aethelgard used the calm to get each of them to state their claim plainly, without
interruption, the way he always insisted things be stated to him rather than around him. And when both
had finished, he understood — the General said his father told him this part afterward, a little
ruefully — that he had just spent a great deal of patience solving a problem the coin was never built
to solve.

The dispute wasn't about temper. It never had been. Somewhere back near the start of it, three
generations before, a flood had moved the old boundary marker off whatever spot it had originally
stood in, and nobody living now could say with any certainty which side of the current marker's
position the original line had actually fallen on. Both families were arguing in good faith, from
genuinely different memories of a fact neither of them had ever personally witnessed and no surviving
record settled cleanly. The coin had done exactly what it was built to do — bought two calm men enough
patience to speak plainly instead of shouting — and had nothing whatsoever to offer the actual question
underneath the anger, because the actual question wasn't a matter of anyone's mood at all.

The General told me his father sent for the House's old land surveys that same week, and when those
turned out incomplete, sent a man out to walk the ground itself and compare what remained of the
original grading against both families' stories, the kind of tedious, unglamorous work that took the
better part of a season to produce an answer neither side could fully love but both, eventually,
accepted as fair. It wasn't the coin that ended three generations of argument. It was a surveyor with a
measuring chain and more patience than either Rell or Tamsy had ever managed to summon for the other.

Afterward, the General said, his father told him something he'd never quite said about the coin before,
in all the years of watching it spin on tables meant to teach the boy his own temper. "It buys you the
room to find the truth," Aethelgard told him. "It has never once, in my whole life, been the truth
itself. I think I forgot that for a moment in there, watching two old men go quiet the way you used to
go quiet, and mistook the quiet for an answer. It's only ever been the beginning of looking for one."

The General told me he thought about that distinction for years afterward, in rooms of his own where
people went quiet in front of him for reasons that had nothing to do with any coin at all — that his
father's oldest trick had a floor to it, same as everything else he'd ever been taught by the man, and
that knowing where the floor was had turned out to matter almost as much as the trick itself.
