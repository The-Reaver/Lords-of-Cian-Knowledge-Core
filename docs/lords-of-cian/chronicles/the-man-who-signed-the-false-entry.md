# The Man Who Signed the False Entry

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1157`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXXIII, wave 25. The method's first coerced-collaborator case: a worker who helped falsify records against his own coworkers under threat to his family, testing the ledger's discipline between culpability and coercion.*

---

The false entries were his handwriting. That much was never in dispute — a junior clerk's careful hand, recording hours a full third lower than what six of his own coworkers had actually worked, entries that had cost them real wages for the better part of a year. What took Ezio three days to establish, cross-referencing dates against an unrelated dispute in the district's tax rolls, was why a man with no obvious motive to steal from people he ate lunch beside every day had done it.

The mill's owner had learned, eighteen months earlier, that the clerk's younger sister worked a debt-bonded post at a sister estate the same owner controlled through a cousin. The arrangement was never written down anywhere Ezio could find, which was itself the point — an unspoken understanding, renewed by implication every quarter, that the sister's conditions would worsen the moment the clerk's cooperation on the hour-books stopped.

"He falsified six men's wages," a worker said flatly, once the shape of it came out. "Doesn't matter why."

Ezio didn't dispute the fact. The entries were false, the harm was real, and nothing about coercion erased either. But the ledger's discipline had never been only about what happened — it had been, from the first district, about who held the actual power to make a different choice, and Ezio's own cross-referencing made clear the clerk had refused the arrangement twice before finally complying, refusals that had visibly worsened his sister's post each time before he gave in.

Kanja didn't ask the six wronged workers to forgive the clerk on the spot, and didn't decide for them that they should. What he separated, plainly, in the finding itself, was the theft from the thief who'd profited by it: the six men's wages were restored in full, charged against the mill owner directly rather than the clerk, with the owner's coercion of the sister's conditions entered as its own documented wrongdoing — a second, distinct finding, not a mitigating footnote to the first. The clerk kept his post, at the six workers' own eventual, unpressured agreement, on the condition that his sister's arrangement be independently audited going forward by someone the owner couldn't quietly threaten.

"You didn't clear him," Ezio said afterward, working through what the ledger would actually record.

"I didn't clear him. I named the thing that made him do it, in the same ink as the thing he did. Both are true. Neither cancels the other." One of the six workers, weeks later, was the one who eventually spoke for letting the clerk stay — not because the harm hadn't been real, he said, but because watching the finding hold the owner to account for the coercion, in writing, in the same document that named the clerk's own wrongdoing, was the first time anyone with power over either of them had bothered to ask which threat had actually done the deciding.
