# The Flag That Wasn't Theirs

---

The village on the western headland had never once been visited by the ghost fleet, and yet every
survivor of the night raid that burned its grain stores and drowned its harbor master swore, consistently
and in matching detail, that black sails under no flag had done it — a description close enough to *The
Audit*'s silhouette that Danne Sok's charts placed the fleet forty miles distant on the night in
question, provably elsewhere, and it made no difference at all to what the survivors believed they'd
seen.

"It wasn't privateers covering plunder this time," Dol Maren said, laying out what little evidence
existed. "Nothing was taken. The grain was burned, not stolen. Someone built this to look like us on
purpose, and they didn't want anything from that village except for it to hate us afterward."

Corren Halst's inquiries turned up nothing usable — no ship matched, no crew identified, no thread that
led anywhere but a dead end consistent with deliberate, professional concealment. Whoever had staged it
had done so with the specific competence of people who didn't intend to be found, which told Kanja
almost as much as finding them would have.

"We could hunt for a month and not catch whoever did this," he said. "And even if we did, half that
village would still believe what they saw with their own eyes over whatever confession we dragged out
of someone afterward. Forcing an admission worked once, on a privateer who wanted to keep operating.
Whoever did this doesn't care what we prove. They only wanted the fear to land."

So he went to the village himself — unarmed, in daylight, *The Audit* anchored openly offshore in
plain sight of the harbor rather than concealed, Dol Maren and Garren Hask beside him with the fleet's
own supply manifests and the charted proof of its position that night laid out for anyone who wanted to
examine it.

It did not go cleanly. Some of the survivors listened. Others turned away without a word, unwilling to
set aside what their own eyes had told them for a stranger's paperwork, however honestly offered. An old
woman who'd lost her harbor-master husband spat at Kanja's feet and told him the charts could say
anything a ghost wanted them to say.

He didn't argue with her. He left the fleet's grain stores — three months of provisions the crew could
not easily spare — with the families who'd lost theirs, made no further claim on the village's belief
either way, and sailed on without knowing how many of them had actually been convinced.

"That's not a clean win," Garren Hask said, once they'd cleared the headland.

"It isn't. Some of them will hate us for the rest of their lives over something we didn't do, and there
was never a version of today that fixed that for all of them." Kanja watched the headland fall astern.
"The confession trick worked because the man doing it wanted to keep his name useful. Whoever did this
wanted our name ruined. You can't out-argue someone who was never trying to win an argument in the first
place — you can only show up honestly and let people decide what that's worth, knowing some of them
never will."
