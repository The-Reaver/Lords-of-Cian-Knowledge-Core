# What the Room Had Learned to Expect of Him

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1819`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XC, House politics strand wave 3, eleventh and closing entry.*

---

I asked the General, once I'd gathered enough of these — the hedgerow, the envoys, the old quota, the
Reckoning Walk quietly withdrawn, the two old men made to admire each other in spite of themselves —
whether he'd noticed, at the time, that the House and the region around it had begun to expect
something particular of him in these rooms, or whether each negotiation and each small dispute had
simply felt, in its own moment, like an isolated problem in front of him with no pattern attached to
it at all. He gave the question longer thought than he usually gave my questions about himself, and
when he answered, it wasn't quite the answer I'd expected.

He said he hadn't noticed it happening to him. He'd noticed it happening to the room.

By his late twenties, he told me, something had shifted in how the various parties who came before
him — feuding tenants, envoys with something to measure, Houses with old grudges dressed up as
present disputes — carried themselves into whatever chamber or field or yard the matter happened to
be settled in. It wasn't deference, exactly, though there was some of that in it. It was something
closer, he said, to a lowered guard: people arrived expecting, correctly by then, that he would hear
the actual dispute rather than only the performance of it, that he'd look past whatever posture of
offended pride a party had walked in wearing to whatever real grievance sat underneath it, and that
whatever he decided, he'd have decided it for reasons he could explain plainly rather than reasons
built to serve his own House's advantage at someone else's expense.

He said that expectation, once it had settled into the region's general understanding of him, changed
the nature of the disputes that reached him. People began arriving more honestly, he said — not
because honesty had become fashionable, but because a room that expects to be heard fairly has less
reason to perform outrage it doesn't feel, and a room that's learned a false grievance will be
recognized as one has considerably less appetite for bringing him a false grievance to begin with. He
said he'd never set out to build that reputation deliberately, in the way a man sets out to build a
fortune or an army. It had simply accumulated, dispute by dispute, the same slow, uncredited way the
household's own quiet stories about him had accumulated — except this time in full public view,
where every Corwyth and Adderwell, every Ashworth and Skarne, every envoy who left with a report to
carry home, had unknowingly added one more small brick to something none of them, and for a long
while not even Ozmund himself, had recognized as a structure at all.

I asked him what he thought that structure had actually been, put together and looked at all at once.
He was quiet for a while before answering, and when he did, he said something I hadn't expected him
to say so plainly. He said he thought it had been the only kind of authority he'd ever fully trusted
in himself — not the authority of his blood, which he'd carried since before memory and never earned
in any sense he could point to, and not the authority his Crown-Scar could have exercised over lesser
men without a single word of persuasion, which he'd spent his whole life deliberately refusing to
lean on. This was different. This was authority built one hedgerow, one quota, one stalled
negotiation at a time, out of nothing but a room's own accumulated experience of being heard
honestly, and he said he understood, even then, that it was the one kind of power available to him
that the world could never simply hand him and that nothing in his own blood could take away.

He told me he didn't know, saying all of this, what use that particular kind of authority would prove
to have in the years that came after — he was careful, telling me this one, not to reach forward
toward anything he hadn't yet lived when these smaller stories took place, and I didn't press him to.
What he said instead, closing the thought the way he so often closed these — quietly, and without
any of the weight he might have been entitled to put on it — was that a man who has spent his whole
adolescence and young manhood learning to be trusted in small rooms over small matters has learned
something no single grand gesture could ever teach him nearly as well: that trust, built this
patiently, doesn't need defending nearly so often as it needs simply continuing to be worth having.
