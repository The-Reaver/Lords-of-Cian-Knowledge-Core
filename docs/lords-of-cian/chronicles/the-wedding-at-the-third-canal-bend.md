# The Wedding at the Third Canal Bend

*Locked canon, Batch 266, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1396`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle XCIII, wave 31,
closing the wave. Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. A warm, celebratory register -- the wave's first
entry with no crisis, correction, or loss at its center.*

---

Nobody asked Kanja to attend the wedding. That was, as far as he was concerned, the entire point
of going.

The bride worked the third canal bend crossing Corren Halst had been overseeing since the plank
bridge first went up — one of a dozen dredge hands who'd learned, over two years, to trust a
tally sheet before they trusted a foreman's word. The groom hauled stone two sites down. Neither of
them had asked the crew for anything beyond what every other worker on the line had already been
given: an honest count, paid on time, and nobody's boot on their neck while they worked for it. They
hadn't invited him as the Trench Monarch. They'd invited him because Corren Halst had mentioned,
in passing, that Kanja liked to eat at the same crowded tables as everyone else when nobody made a
production of seating him somewhere better.

So he came the way anyone came — with a folded coin for the household jar, no different from
what Danne Sok or Maret Vos dropped in beside his, and stood at the back of a gathering built out
of borrowed chairs and two long trestle tables dragged in from a nearby site.

Garren Hask closed his ledger for the afternoon, an event rare enough that Callum Breck made a
point of announcing it to the whole gathering, drawing the kind of laugh that only a man everyone
trusted with their money could safely earn. Efa Gol and Pell Ostra sat together near the food,
neither one on duty for once, trading the kind of unhurried talk that never made it into either of
their reports. Maret Vos, cornered by three of the bride's younger cousins, spent most of the
evening teaching a knot nobody had asked him to teach, because it was the only thing he seemed to
know how to do with his hands when he wasn't holding a tool.

Kanja didn't give a toast. Nobody expected one from him, and when the moment for toasts came and
went without anyone looking to him to fill it, he understood, more clearly than any tally sheet had
ever told him, exactly how much of the last two years had actually landed the way he'd hoped it
would. He was a guest at somebody else's happiest day, welcome and unremarkable, the same as
everyone else at the table.

He left before the dancing wound down, walking back along the canal in the dark with the sound of
it still carrying behind him, and thought, not for the first time, that a wedding he hadn't been
needed at was worth more to him than most of the corrections he had been.
