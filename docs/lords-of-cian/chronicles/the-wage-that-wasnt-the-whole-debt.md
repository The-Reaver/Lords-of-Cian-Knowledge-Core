# The Wage That Wasn't the Whole Debt

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1172`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXXXVIII, wave 30. The method's first workplace-injury liability case, extending the ledger's arithmetic beyond simple wage recovery into long-term medical compensation for harm caused on the job.*

---

His wages were correct. That was the first thing Ezio confirmed, and the fact of it nearly ended the case before it began, since every prior entry the crew had ever built started from a shortfall between what a man was owed and what he'd been paid. This man had been paid exactly what his contract specified for every hour he'd worked at the forge — right up until a badly maintained furnace door, which the owner had been warned about twice in writing by his own foreman, failed and took two fingers from his working hand in a single afternoon.

What he'd received since was nothing. No wages, because he could no longer work the post. No compensation for the injury itself, because his contract, like every contract in the district, was silent on what happened to a man the work itself permanently damaged. The owner's position, stated without much apparent shame, was that an injury wasn't a wage dispute and therefore fell outside anything the crew had authority to address.

"He's right that it's not a wage dispute," Ezio admitted, turning the unfamiliar shape of the case over. "I don't have a rate to calculate against. There's no hours-times-pay arithmetic that produces a number for two fingers."

Kanja had him build it anyway, from a different direction than any prior case had required: not backward from a contract, but forward from documented fact — the two written warnings about the furnace door that the owner had ignored, a comparison against six other forges in the district whose owners had made the same repair the warnings recommended without incident, and an honest reckoning of what the injury had actually cost the man going forward — his post at the forge gone permanently, his earning capacity reduced for the rest of his working life, a cost no single wage figure could capture but that Ezio, working with a district physician for the first time, translated into a lump settlement calculated against the man's likely remaining working years at a reduced trade he could still practice with one hand short.

The owner argued the injury was an accident, unforeseeable, no different from any hazard inherent to forge work. The two written warnings, dated and signed by his own foreman, said otherwise plainly enough that no magistrate would need much convincing. The finding held him liable not for underpaying labor but for a documented, ignored hazard that had cost a man his hand and his trade, a distinction Kanja made sure the finding stated explicitly, since it was the first time the method had ever needed to.

"We've spent two years proving what people were paid," Ezio said, closing the case. "This is the first time we've had to prove what something actually cost someone."

"It won't be the last. A door doesn't care whether the harm it does fits neatly into a wage column. Neither should we."
