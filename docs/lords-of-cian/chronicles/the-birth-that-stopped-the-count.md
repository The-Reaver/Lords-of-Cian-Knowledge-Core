# The Birth That Stopped the Count

*Locked canon, Batch 255, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1132`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LXXV, wave 25,
closing the wave. Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. The tally table stops for one life.*

---

The negotiation had been running for two hours, five owners and their foremen on one side of the
table, Garren Hask's figures laid out between them, when the shout came from outside that stopped
everything at once — a woman in labor, three streets from Warehouse Twelve, the midwife already sent
for and the street between her house and the midwife's own too choked with a cart accident to pass.

Kanja was out from behind the tally table before anyone had finished explaining why.

He didn't run the calculation the way he ran a battle's — there was no flood timing here, no
collapsing terrain to weaponize, nothing about the problem that resembled anything the Trench Monarch
name had ever been built to solve. There was a blocked street, a midwife on one end of it, and a
woman on the other end running out of time faster than the cart's owner could clear the wreckage
alone. He didn't ask permission from the negotiation he'd left mid-sentence. He simply lifted the
cart's overturned rear axle himself, cleared enough of the street for a single rider to pass, and sent
the fastest runner in the room ahead to bring the midwife through on foot the rest of the way while he
held the gap open.

The five owners and their foremen, left alone at the tally table with an unfinished negotiation and no
one to negotiate with, did not resume without him. Nobody suggested it. The eldest of the owners
later admitted, when Hask asked him about it weeks afterward, that watching the exchange collapse
so completely and so instantly over one woman's labor had told him more about the man across the
table than three hours of figures had managed.

The birth went well. Kanja didn't stay for it — his part ended at the cleared street, and he returned
to Warehouse Twelve on foot, the negotiation waiting for him exactly where he'd left it, nobody having
touched Hask's figures in his absence.

"We could have finished without you," one of the owners said, not unkindly, as he sat back down.

"You could have. I couldn't have finished it knowing I'd let her wait." He picked up the sheet he'd
been reading when the shout came, found his place in it again. "Where were we."

The negotiation concluded an hour later, on terms neither side loved and both accepted, and Hask
entered the closed figure in the ledger without remark. What he did note, in the margin, wasn't a
number at all — just the child's birth-hour, cross-referenced against nothing, kept for no reason the
method required.
