# Daba Chronicle XXV: The Give in the Steel

Kanja had been at the same piece for the better part of an hour, and Daba had learned enough by now
to know that an hour was long past the point where the trouble was really about the metal.

It was a reinforcing plate, meant to sit inside a shoulder guard for one of 1804's forward
scouts -- nothing elaborate, nothing that should have resisted him this long. Twice now Kanja had
tempered it to what should have been its full, correct hardness, and twice now, under the same
controlled test strike that a dozen lesser pieces had shrugged off without complaint, it had failed
along the exact same line, a hair's width from the plate's center, as though the metal had decided
in advance where it meant to break and simply waited for permission.

"It's not a flaw," Kanja said, half to himself, turning the cracked plate in the firelight. "I keep
checking for one and there isn't one. It's clean stock, evenly worked. It's hard exactly where I
wanted it hard. And it keeps failing in the same place regardless."

Daba, who had stopped by only to return a borrowed hammer and had ended up staying to watch, found
himself thinking not about the plate at all but about a ridge position two winters back -- a
garrison post the Trust had spent a season reinforcing, walls thickened, approach narrowed to a
single defensible causeway, every improvement making the place more obviously, uniformly strong.
1804 had taken it in one night without losing a person, not by matching that strength anywhere, but
by finding the one seam a wall built for uniform strength always has: the place where the builders,
trusting the strength everywhere else, stopped checking.

"You built it to be strong everywhere at once," Daba said, before he'd fully decided to say it.

Kanja looked up.

"A position like that," Daba went on, gesturing loosely at the plate without quite meaning to
gesture at the plate, "we don't beat it by being stronger somewhere. We beat it because uniform
strength has no give anywhere in it -- so once you find where the pressure actually lands, that's
the only place left for it to go. It doesn't matter how strong the rest of it is. It never gets the
chance to matter."

Kanja went very still, the way Daba had learned meant a thing was landing all the way down rather
than skating across the top of his attention.

"I tempered the whole plate the same way," Kanja said slowly. "Full hardness, edge to edge. No
place in it soft enough to bend instead of hold. Which means the first strike that finds the load
point doesn't meet resistance and give -- it meets resistance and nothing else. The break has
nowhere to be except exactly there."

"Same as a wall with no weak gate," Daba said. "Density's not the problem. It's density with nowhere
for the force to go once it arrives."

Kanja set the cracked plate down and reached for a fresh piece of stock, working faster now, with
the particular focus Daba recognized from the nights an ambush plan had finally come together in his
own head. He tempered it unevenly this time, on purpose -- full hardness along most of its face, but
a narrow band left deliberately softer running straight through the load point Daba had pointed to
without meaning to, a place built to flex rather than resist.

He struck it with the same test blow that had broken the first two. It rang, bent a fraction along
the soft band, and held.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Daba looked at the plate, then at Kanja, and understood
that something had just closed between them that had been open, unnoticed, since the first night in
the grain cellar -- not a lesson passed one direction, forging from Kanja to Daba, tactics from Daba
to Kanja, but the same single fact wearing two faces the whole time, waiting for the right failure to
show it plainly.

"You already knew this," Kanja said finally, turning the reworked plate once in the firelight.

"So did you," Daba said. "We just each only had half the word for it."
