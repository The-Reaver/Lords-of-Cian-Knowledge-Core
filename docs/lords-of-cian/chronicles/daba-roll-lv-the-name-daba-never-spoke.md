# Daba Roll LV: The Name Daba Never Spoke

*Locked canon, Batch 316, 2026-09-28. Daba Roll LV, second entry of the third wave. Close-third on
Daba, no dedicated narrator. Extends the blind-succession structure Daba built for individual cells
(`MCD-1597`, "Yeva Tolan"/"Marn," Roll XXVII) to the network's own top, a question forced open
by his own near-death in Roll LII. Reveals, for the first time, whether Daba built the same
structure in for himself — and names Kether as the answer. Reuses Kether, Bren, Wrenna. No new
named characters.*

---

Bren asked the question three weeks after Threnfall, on an evening when the wound across Daba's
ribs had closed enough to stop demanding daily attention and had started demanding, instead, the
kind of quiet reckoning that came after a body healed faster than the mind that had watched it
nearly fail.

"You built Yeva a second," Bren said. "You've built every cell of any size a second, going back
years. Who's yours?"

Daba did not answer right away, which Bren had learned, across years of proximity, to read
correctly — not evasion, calculation.

"There isn't one," he said finally.

"That's not true, or it's the worst decision you've ever made, and I don't think you make the
worst decision available to you when the stakes are the whole network."

"There wasn't one," Daba corrected, "until Threnfall taught me there needed to be. The doctrine
protects every cell against my own logic. I never applied it to myself, because the network's
whole architecture was built around the assumption that I was the one constant piece — the person
who held the shape of the thing in his own head, the one loss the design itself couldn't route
around. Making a second for myself meant admitting the design had a hole in it exactly where I'd
always told everyone else there couldn't be one."

"And now?"

"Now I've spent three weeks doing the only thing I know how to do with a fact I don't like." He
met Bren's eyes. "I counted it honestly, and built the fix."

He had already chosen her, in the two weeks since, the same way he chose every second: not by
announcement, not by test she would ever recognize as a test, but by the accumulation of small,
unexplained authority handed over on days that looked ordinary from the inside. He had let Kether
run the vetting process end to end for a month without his own review sitting behind it. He had
asked her, twice, to make a call on a resource dispute between two cells that would once have come
to him directly, and had watched her resolve both without asking him a single question afterward
about whether she'd gotten it right. He had, three days ago, without telling her why, handed her the
key to the back room that held the annual list — the one place in the entire network he had never
let another living person enter alone.

She had not asked why. She had simply taken the key, and returned it that evening, and said nothing
at all about what she had or hadn't read inside, which told him more about her fitness for the role
than any test he could have designed on purpose.

"Kether," Bren said, when Daba finally told him, and did not sound surprised so much as satisfied,
the way a man sounds when a suspicion he'd never voiced is finally confirmed. "She doesn't know."

"She doesn't know. And she won't, unless it's needed, for exactly the reason Marn never knew about
the river cell until the morning he had to. If I'm taken, or if the well at Threnfall had gone the
other way three weeks ago, there's nothing in Kether's head worth extracting, because she doesn't
know she's carrying anything at all. She only becomes dangerous the moment she's needed, and by
then it's already too late for anyone to reach her first."

"That's the same argument you made about Yeva's cell."

"It's the same argument because it's still true. I only ever thought it stopped applying to me
because I mistook being necessary for being exempt." He was quiet a moment, turning the fact over
the way he turned every fact, checking its edges. "I nearly proved myself wrong at Threnfall
without meaning to. I'd rather prove myself right on purpose, before the ground makes the choice
for me a second time."

Wrenna, told of it days later — not the name, only that the structure now existed, matching what
she already understood of how the network protected itself — asked the one question that mattered
to her particularly.

"Does she know how close you came? At Threnfall?"

"No," Daba said. "She knows a safehouse was lost and I was wounded closing it. She doesn't know the
numbers. Nobody outside this room knows the numbers but you and Bren, and that was never going to
change just because I finally admitted the hole in my own design."

"Because telling her would make her carry it."

"Because telling her would make her watch me differently, and a second who watches her own leader
for signs of the next near-miss is a second who's already half doing the job before she's ever
asked to. I need Kether running the network the way she's always run it — clear-headed, unwarned,
entirely her own — right up until the one morning it turns out she has to."

The list that night carried no new name, same as it had for most of the year now, and Daba closed
the back room's door on a fact that had cost him three weeks of honest reckoning to admit and had
taken him, in the end, less than a month to fix once he let himself see it clearly: that the
doctrine he had built to survive everyone else's death had never, until Threnfall forced the
question, been asked to survive his own.
