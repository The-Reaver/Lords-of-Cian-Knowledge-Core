# What Kade Passed Down

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1828`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle XCIX, Wider Verehimu Household/Guard strand, wave 2. Set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony
(`MCD-025`), in Ozmund's later adolescence — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund still House heir. Narrated by
Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, drawn from the same extended conversation that
produced Chronicle XLV — Sergeant Oswin Kade gave him more that afternoon than the one institutional
account he'd first used. A generational/legacy register: Kade training the guardsman he means to leave
the gate to, and what he chooses to pass down beyond drill and discipline. No new named characters.*

---

I have already told the reader what Sergeant Oswin Kade thought an oath was for. What I left out,
because it belonged to a different part of the same long afternoon, was what he told me about the man
he'd spent his last five years of active service training to take his place — a story he offered, I
think, mostly to prove that the arrangement he'd described to me wasn't something he only believed in
the abstract.

He never gave me the younger guardsman's name, and I did not press him for it, sensing the omission was
deliberate rather than forgetful. Kade said the man hadn't asked to be written about, and Kade wasn't in
the business of handing out someone's name to a stranger without his leave. What he would give me was
the shape of the thing: a guardsman recruited off a farm two days' ride from the Verehimu seat,
competent enough with a blade but unremarkable in every other respect Kade had learned, across forty
years, to value more than swordwork — patience, the willingness to be corrected, the particular
attention that notices a gate hinge wants oiling before it actually fails.

Kade said he'd chosen him for training not because he was the best swordsman on the wall — he wasn't,
not by a fair margin — but because he was the only man in that year's intake who'd once come to Kade
privately, unprompted, to report an error he'd made on watch rather than hope no one noticed it. Kade
said that told him more about a man's fitness for the gate than any drill score ever could.

What followed, over those five years, was less instruction than translation, as Kade put it — taking
everything he'd spent his career learning about what the Guard owed the House and the House owed the
Guard, all the quiet policy he'd described to me before, and handing it across in a form a younger man
could carry forward once Kade himself was gone from the yard. He was careful, he told me, never to
present any of it as his own invention. He wanted the man to understand it as an inheritance belonging
to the post itself, not a personal philosophy that would leave the wall the day Kade did.

He told me of one evening, late in that training, when the younger man asked him outright whether any
of it — the pensions kept in full, the families housed, the whole reciprocal arrangement Kade set such
store by — would survive him. Kade said he hadn't had a ready answer, and told him so honestly, because
he didn't actually know whether the shape of the thing depended on any one man holding the gate or on
something steadier underneath it. What he did tell him was that the only honest answer he had was to
keep doing it exactly as he'd been taught, and to teach whoever came after to do the same, and to trust
that an arrangement kept faithfully long enough eventually stops depending on any single person
remembering to keep it.

Kade allowed, near the end of his telling, that he still didn't fully know the answer even now — that
he'd been retired long enough to have lost the day-to-day proof of it, and that all he had left to go on
was the fact that the man he'd trained had, as far as he'd heard, never once let the arrangement lapse
in the years since. He said that was as much certainty as forty years of service had ever bought him,
and that he'd decided, somewhere along the way, it was enough.

I asked him, because I could not help myself, whether he thought the General had ever known how
deliberately that particular inheritance was passed hand to hand rather than simply existing on its
own. Kade considered this a while before answering, and then said he doubted it — that a young lord
sees the results of an arrangement like that far more easily than he sees the specific, unglamorous
work of keeping it alive from one sergeant to the next. He said he didn't think that mattered. An oath
kept quietly, generation after generation, by men who never expected credit for it, was — in his
experience — the only kind worth keeping at all.
