# Daba Chronicle XLIII: What the Smoke Never Touched

They had come to the smelting district for a different reason entirely -- a foundry clerk named on
three separate debt-forgery complaints, a man Daba wanted alive and talking before the Trust's own
inspectors thought to ask him anything -- and it was only the smoke that told them something larger
than their own errand was already underway.

It rose from four furnace stacks at once, which was wrong. Elowen Marn noticed it before Daba did,
from the ridge where they'd stopped to let the clerk's escort pass below them unbothered. "Banked,"
she said. "All four, same hour. That's not a production stop. That's a decision."

By the second day the decision had a shape: four thousand workers standing idle in the yards below,
and at the center of them, small enough at this distance to be anyone, a single unarmed man moving
from crew to crew with nothing in his hands but what looked like a ledger of his own. No column of
Trust soldiers had come yet. No violence had started that Daba could see from the ridge, and none of
his own instincts told him one was coming.

"We could clear the district in an hour," Elowen said, not quite a suggestion. "If it turns. If the
Trust sends a real force instead of overseers with clubs."

"It won't turn," Daba said, and he was surer of this than he had any right to be from a ridge two
miles off, surer than the intelligence in front of him strictly supported. He watched the small
figure below work its way through another crew, unhurried, unarmed, and something in the rhythm of
it -- the patience of a man who had decided violence would cost him more than it would win him --
reached up the ridge and touched a part of Daba that had nothing to do with tactics at all.

He did not say the name he was thinking. Elowen didn't ask him to.

"Our own business is a foundry clerk two streets that way," Daba said instead, nodding past the
strike entirely, toward the smaller and uglier work that was actually theirs to do. "Four thousand
men putting down their tools is the kind of thing that draws every eye the Trust owns. We are not
eyes the Trust owns, and we are not going to become interesting to anyone by standing near something
this large."

"You want to watch a while longer first."

It wasn't a question, same as it never was with the people who'd been with him long enough to stop
asking him things directly. Daba let himself have the hour anyway -- watched the unarmed man work the
yards below, watched four thousand people hold a line that had nothing behind it but their own
collective refusal, watched a district that could have burned choose instead not to -- and felt, not
for the first time since the dispatch that had named no one, the particular ache of standing close
enough to something he had helped shape from a distance to see it clearly and still being obligated
to let it belong entirely to someone else.

"We move at dusk," he said finally, and turned the escort south, away from the yards, toward the
clerk and the forgeries and the smaller, colder work that was 1804's own to finish. Behind them the
strike held for two more days by his own network's later count, and resolved -- so the eventual
report read, filtered up through three cells and stripped of every name that mattered -- without a
single life lost on either side.

Elowen didn't ask him again what he'd recognized on that ridge, and he didn't offer it, and the two
of them rode south together carrying a silence that had nothing accusatory in it and everything to do
with a discipline neither of them had needed to discuss out loud in years. Some fires were not
theirs to feed or fight. The hardest doctrine 1804 had ever asked of him was learning, again and
again, to let the ones that weren't burn exactly as they needed to, unwatched by anyone who would
ever say so.
