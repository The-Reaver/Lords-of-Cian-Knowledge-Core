# What Efa Gol Learned About Waiting

Efa had run decoy columns and crowd diversions at the Ash-Wharf evacuation and Maw-9 both, and she
found, to her own mild surprise, that four thousand people lined up peacefully to give wage testimony
took a different kind of nerve than either.

"Nobody's chasing this crowd," Kanja said, the first morning, when she asked why he wanted her
running the line instead of one of the ledger clerks. "That's the problem. A crowd running from
something organizes itself. A crowd standing still for three days in the heat, waiting to be heard,
comes apart from boredom and thirst and the person four places back who's decided he's been waiting
longer than is fair. I need someone who's moved people under worse pressure than this to make sure it
doesn't."

She organized the line the way she'd have organized an evacuation route — water carried down the
queue on a rotation instead of pooled at one table where a crush would form, shade structures raised
over the worst of the mid-day sun, a second line opened for anyone too old or too injured to stand
the full wait, and a rule, enforced gently but without exception, that testimony order followed
arrival order and nothing else, so that no one could accuse the queue itself of favoritism before
they'd even reached the table.

By the second day, the four thousand had become a genuine test — heat, fatigue, a rumor moving down
the line that the ledger would close before everyone was heard, which she killed within the hour by
walking the queue herself and telling every group in it, plainly, that the tent stayed open until the
last name was taken, however long that took.

It took three days. Nobody went unheard, and nobody was hurt in the waiting, and when the final entry
was recorded on the third evening, Efa realized she'd spent seventy-two hours managing a crowd more
carefully than she'd managed most retreats — not because the stakes were smaller, but because the
whole promise of the ledger depended on nobody in that line ever feeling like they'd been made to
wait unfairly for something owed to them from the start.

"You made three days of standing in the sun feel like it mattered," Kanja told her, once it was
done.

"It did matter. That's the only reason it worked." She watched the last of the line disperse,
satisfied in a way she hadn't expected patience to feel. "Chasing them out of danger, I know how to
do. Holding a crowd still long enough to be heard, fairly, without anyone deciding the wait itself was
the injustice — that's harder than it looks, and nobody ever writes a ledger entry for it."
