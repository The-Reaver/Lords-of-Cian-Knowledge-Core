# The Cohort He Couldn't Teach Alone

*Locked canon, Batch 237, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1048`). Iron Bastard Alias Chronicle LIX, wave 20. A teaching-at-scale entry: Kanja is asked to train a full cohort rather than a single student, and the doctrine's own lineage becomes its solution. Not a territory Chronicle.*

---

Twelve volunteers stood waiting in the yard, sent by a settlement council that had heard the doctrine
could be taught and wanted it taught to more than one pair of hands this time. Kanja had trained a
student before. He had never trained twelve.

He walked the line once, already counting the problem rather than the volunteers. Diagnostic listening
demanded a kind of attention that didn't divide well — he'd learned that with his first student, the
young engineer who'd come close to using the technique out of resentment before choosing restraint, and
again with the second, who'd needed years before he trusted her working a structure without him standing
at her shoulder. Twelve pairs of hands against live tension, twelve chances for a misread nobody caught
in time, and one set of eyes to watch all of it.

"I can teach one of you to hear it," he told the council elder who'd sent them. "I can't teach twelve of
you to hear it safely at the same time. Not alone."

He hadn't come alone, though — not really, not anymore. The first student, no longer young enough to be
called that without the word feeling wrong, had made the journey with him at his own request. The second
student sent word she could reach the settlement inside a week if the cohort could wait that long. It
could.

What formed, once she arrived, was something the doctrine had never had before: not a master and a
student, but three teachers standing in one yard, each carrying a different distance from the original
listening — Kanja's own, the first student's decade of applied restraint, the second's proof that the
skill survived a full generation working entirely without him. He split the twelve into three groups of
four. Each teacher took one group and worked a structure nobody's life depended on, over and over, until
a wrong read stopped feeling like a guess and started feeling like the obvious mistake it was.

"You always taught this like it only had one shape," the first student said, watching her own four work
a scaffold under her instruction rather than his, "because it only ever passed through one set of hands
at a time before now."

"It only ever needed to, before now," Kanja said. "Before this, I was the only proof the doctrine
could be taught at all. Now there are three of us standing in the same yard who learned it differently,
and none of us taught it identically to the one who taught us. That's not a flaw. That's what it looks
like, growing past being just mine."

Nine of the twelve passed the cohort's final trial by the second month. The three who didn't were sent
home with an honest accounting of why, not a lesser certificate — the doctrine's own standing rule, that
a wrong read costs more than an ungranted title, holding even at this new scale.
