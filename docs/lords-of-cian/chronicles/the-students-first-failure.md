# The Student's First Failure

*Locked canon, Batch 201, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-839`). The Crow King Alias Chronicle XXIV, wave 8 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The apprentice's own student — the one she'd taken on without asking permission, months into her own
growing mastery of the craft — misjudged a false signal's timing on her first solo field operation,
sending a diversion two minutes early, before the crew it was meant to protect had actually cleared
the ground it was supposed to keep clear. No one died. The operation succeeded anyway, on the margin
the crew's own contingency planning had built in for exactly this kind of gap. But the mistake was
real, and everyone involved knew it.

She expected to be pulled from the field.

"You're not being removed," the apprentice told her instead, plainly, once the after-action review
had laid out exactly what had gone wrong. "You're going to walk me through every decision you made,
in order, so we can find precisely where the timing slipped — not to punish it, to understand it."

"I got the count wrong. That's what happened. I don't need a review to tell me that."

"You got the count wrong because you were listening to your own nerves louder than you were
listening to the actual signal you were tracking. That's not the same mistake as simply miscounting.
One of those corrects with practice. The other one needs to be caught early, or it repeats itself in
a shape you won't recognize next time." The apprentice said it without softening it, the same way
she remembered being told her own early failures plainly, years before. "I lost my voice for eleven
days learning this. You're going to lose considerably less than that, if you let this actually teach
you something instead of just frightening you out of trying again."

The student did walk through it, in full, twice — and the correction that came out of the review
became, within weeks, a standard verification step the apprentice folded into how she trained every
student after this one, the failure itself outliving its own embarrassment and becoming, quietly,
part of the craft's own growing discipline.

"I thought failing meant I wasn't ready," the student admitted, once the review was finally done.

"Failing means you're doing something real enough to fail at. The craft doesn't actually have a
version where nobody ever gets the count wrong. It only has a version where getting it wrong once
teaches you enough that you don't get it wrong the same way twice."
