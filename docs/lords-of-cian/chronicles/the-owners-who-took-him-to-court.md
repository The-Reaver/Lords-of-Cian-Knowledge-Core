# The Owners Who Took Him to Court

*Locked canon, Batch 255, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1130`). Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LXXIII, wave 25.
Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. A coalition of owners petitions Canal House to have the method
itself declared coercion, and Kanja must answer for it personally.*

---

The Trust's own inquest at Canal House had ended in a non-finding, months back — six hours of hostile
examination that the tally records had simply survived. This was a different room entirely. Eleven
owners, none of them individually caught in anything the method had ever proven, had pooled their
grievances into a single formal petition: that the tally-verification process, however accurate its
figures, constituted a form of coercion — men showing up to examine a business's books under the
implicit threat of a reputation that had, by then, flooded a canal and put sixteen armed men down in
under four minutes. They weren't disputing the numbers. They were arguing the numbers didn't matter,
because no owner facing the Trench Monarch's crew at his door could be said to consent to anything.

Kanja attended without Onyx at all, the first hearing he'd ever entered fully unarmed rather than
merely restrained, at the advice of no one — his own decision, arrived at the night before.

"They're not wrong that people are afraid of you," Hask said, laying out the petition's language line
by line. "That's not a figure I can argue against with a ledger."

"No. But I can argue the figures were never the coercion." Kanja read the petition twice before the
hearing, circling the same clause each time — the claim that verification itself was the threat,
regardless of what it found. "An honest owner has nothing to fear from a true number. The only
coercion in this room is whatever fear an owner brings with him because he already knows what an
honest check will show."

The magistrate — a Trust appointee with no evident sympathy for either side — pressed him on it
directly for the better part of an hour. Had he ever threatened an owner. Had his reputation ever been
invoked, by him or by anyone claiming to speak for him, as leverage separate from an actual proven
debt. Kanja answered each question plainly, citing Hask's own records — every closed case, every
figure verified and either confirmed or dismissed on its merits, none of them settled by
intimidation alone.

The eleven owners had, among them, exactly one legitimate grievance buried inside the petition's
broader claim: a single case, two years prior, where an overzealous crew member had implied
consequences beyond what the method actually authorized. Kanja conceded that one point without being
pressed to, named the incident, and stated plainly it should never have happened and hadn't been
repeated.

The petition was denied — not because the magistrate found the fear itself unreasonable, but because
fear of an honest process, however real, wasn't coercion the court had any standing to remedy. Kanja
left Canal House with nothing decided in his favor that hadn't already been true before he arrived,
which felt, he told Hask afterward, like the correct outcome for a case that should never have needed
deciding at all.
