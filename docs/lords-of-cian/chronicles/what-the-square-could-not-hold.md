# What the Square Could Not Hold

*Locked canon, Batch 195, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-666`). The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle XXXI, wave 11 of the ten-wave sixth-through-fifteenth run. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The market square's undercroft gave way on an ordinary morning, no soldiers involved at all — just
decades of unmaintained civic stonework finally failing under the weight of a crowded market day,
swallowing two dozen stalls and everyone standing near them into a sudden, dust-choked pit.

Kanja was three streets away when he heard it.

There was no Trinity combat in what followed, no enemy to fight — only Mafesto's Kinetic Transfer
System steadying his footing on unstable rubble that shifted with every step, and Obsidian Malice
used exclusively as a lever and a cutting tool, clearing collapsed stalls off trapped vendors one
careful section at a time while civilians who'd never heard the alias's name in connection with
anything but rumors of a war they weren't part of watched a stranger in worn armor dig through
their own market by hand.

"Careful there — that beam's still carrying weight," he called to a shopkeeper trying to help,
redirecting the man's effort to a section that wouldn't collapse further under a second body's
weight. "Pull from this side instead. That one's safe to move."

Nineteen people came out alive over the following two hours, three more than the district's own
emergency crews, arriving late and overwhelmed, had expected to find breathing at all.

"You're the one they call a titan," an elderly vendor said afterward, pulled clear with nothing worse
than a fractured wrist, staring at the man who'd spent two hours moving rubble without once
mentioning a war, a cause, or a rebellion. "I thought that meant something to do with soldiers."

"Today it didn't need to." Kanja checked the last cleared section for any remaining voices before
allowing the emergency crew to declare the site closed. "A collapsed market's not a battle. It's
just people who needed someone who knew how to read a collapse properly, and happened to be close
enough to help before it mattered less."

He left before the district's own officials arrived to take credit for a rescue they'd had no part
in, the market's vendors left with nothing but an unclaimed act of help and, weeks later, a
persistent local story about a stranger who'd known exactly which beams to trust and which to
avoid — a story that traveled further than the war itself in that particular district, entirely
disconnected from any alias, any cause, any side.
