# Daba Chronicle XX: One Mind in Five Bodies

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1590`). Entry 20 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. The capstone of this
stretch of Kanja's mentorship under Daba (`MCD-1568`), weaving small-unit doctrine (`MCD-1586`),
terrain-as-weapon thinking (`MCD-1587`), and the discipline of making concentrated force irrelevant
(`MCD-1588`/`1589`) into a single designed exercise, and closing on the exact framing later credited as
the shared root of Kanja's own Dredge-Line Ambush (`MCD-231`) without restaging it. Abad's approval:
"rate 50 Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

Daba had never once, in twenty years of teaching, told a student the exercise would be his last for a
while. He decided, walking out to the marsh's western flat before dawn, that this time he would.

He had built the drill over three sleepless nights, and it borrowed from every lesson that had come
before it without repeating any of them outright. Reya's people would move as a single mock force,
forty strong, advancing on a supply position along the one dry causeway crossing the flat -- a stand-in
for a Trust column large enough that no honest fight could meet it head-on. Kanja would have six people
to answer it: three cells, no shared commander, each blind to the others' exact plan, and whatever the
ground itself was willing to offer.

Daba did not tell him how to use any of it. That, too, was deliberate. A lesson taught three times
separately meant nothing until someone was made to reach for all three at once, unprompted, under
pressure that felt real even when it wasn't.

Kanja spent the better part of an hour walking the flat alone before he spoke to anyone. Daba watched
him from the ridge, watched him crouch at the causeway's edges the way Sorin had taught him to, testing
ground with his weight rather than his eyes, and watched him circle back twice to a shallow drainage
channel that fed into the flat from the north before he seemed satisfied with something he'd found
there.

The plan, when he finally laid it out to his three cell leaders, took him less than five minutes to
explain, and Daba noted -- with something that was not quite pride, because pride implied surprise, and
he was no longer surprised -- that no single one of the three leaders received the whole of it. One cell
would widen the drainage channel just enough, just before dawn, to soften the causeway's western
approach without flooding it outright, forcing Reya's forty to compress onto the eastern half of a
crossing that had looked, the evening before, wide enough for all of them together. A second cell would
do nothing at all except watch the compression happen and report where the column's own density had
begun bottlenecking itself against ground it no longer trusted. The third -- the one Kanja placed
himself in -- would strike only the point where that bottleneck was narrowest, take what could be taken
in the time it took the rest of the column to realize its own numbers had stopped meaning anything, and
be gone before Reya's remaining thirty could bring themselves to bear on a fight that had already ended.

It worked cleanly enough that Reya, debriefing afterward with the dry amusement of a woman who had
built her own career on doctrine like this, told Daba she had genuinely not understood why her column
kept losing width until it was already too late to matter.

Daba found Kanja afterward at the drainage channel, refilling by hand the small cut he'd made that
morning, packing the earth back the way it had been before any of this had happened. It was, Daba
thought, watching him do it without being told to, exactly the instinct the whole doctrine depended on
-- leave nothing behind large enough for anyone to trace.

"You didn't need forty men to lose today," Daba said. "You needed forty men to stop being forty men at
the same moment you had to face them."

Kanja looked up. "That's what the ford taught. And the drill with the runner. And the marsh with
Sorin." He said it plainly, without claiming credit for having noticed, only naming what he'd found.
"It's all the same thing wearing different ground."

"It is." Daba crouched beside him, helping settle the last of the earth back into place. "Density isn't
power, not on its own. Not against ground willing to argue with it. A hundred men, a thousand, a body
built stronger than anything else standing on the field -- none of it decides a fight the moment the
terrain decides how many of them are allowed to matter at once. That's the whole of what I have to
teach you about this. Everything else is just this, said in a different place."

He did not tell him, that morning, what he suspected -- that the boy would carry this particular lesson
further and longer than any of the others, that some version of a narrow crossing and water arriving
exactly where it was needed would follow him into fights Daba could not yet see the shape of. He said
only that this stretch of teaching was finished, that what came next between them would run the other
direction for a while, forge and fire rather than ground and water, and that he expected Kanja to keep
walking terrain on his own regardless of who was or wasn't beside him to name it.

"I will," Kanja said, and Daba, watching him smooth the last of the drainage channel flat with the
careful, unhurried hands of someone who had already stopped thinking of the ground as a place things
merely happened to occur in, believed him completely.
