# Two Doctrines, No Common Ancestor

The neutral trade enclave at the river fork owed nothing to the Trust or the Rebellion either one, and
its bridge inspectors had never heard the name Iron Bastard before the day Kanja arrived asking after a
structural failure that had claimed two lives the season before.

What he found waiting for him wasn't fear, or a countermeasure, or an imitation trading on a
reputation that meant nothing there. It was a working method almost identical to his own, developed by
enclave engineers with no contact with the Trust, no knowledge of the Directorate's classified
engagements, no reason to have ever heard of a resonance doctrine at all.

"You test the tension by ear," the enclave's lead inspector said, more curious than alarmed, watching
Kanja's hand rest against the same failed joint her own crew had already marked for repair. "So do we.
Different tools. Same question asked of the wood."

They compared methods across the better part of a day — her crew's decades of accumulated,
undocumented craft-sense passed inspector to apprentice for three generations, built from nothing but
patient failure and correction, against a doctrine Kanja had built the same way in far less time under
far worse pressure.

"I half expected to find someone who'd stolen it," he admitted, turning one of her own listening rods
over in his hands, a simpler tool doing a version of what the Aegis-Talisman did with less reach and
more skill required to compensate. "Or someone who'd have to be taught. Not two answers to the same
question, built an ocean apart with nothing passing between them."

"Wood and stone only allow so many honest ways to be understood," the inspector said. "Maybe it isn't
strange that more than one people found the same one."

Neither treated the meeting as a rivalry to settle. She corrected two of his working assumptions about
listening through waterlogged timber that he hadn't encountered before; he showed her crew the
frequency-cataloguing habit that let a single listener track four structures' signatures at once
instead of one at a time. The bridge was repaired jointly before he left, neither method claiming
priority over the other.

"I've had the doctrine feared, stolen, banned by council vote, and taught to my own students," Kanja
said before he left. "I hadn't yet had it simply already exist somewhere else, built by people who'd
never need to ask my permission for it." He looked back at the repaired span. "I think I like that
answer best of all of them."
