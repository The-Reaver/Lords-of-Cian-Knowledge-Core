# Daba Chronicle LI: The Day With No Name in It

*Locked canon, Batch 315, 2026-09-28. Daba Chronicle LI, first entry of a next-wave sweep. Close-third on
Daba, no dedicated narrator, matching the existing 50-entry corpus. Develops the psychological
question Section 2 of his gate profile flagged as genuinely open — whether he wants anything for
himself distinct from the network's survival — without fully resolving it. Reuses Mika, present
since the cistern in Chronicle I. No new named characters.*

---

Mika found him on the roof of the old grain warehouse, where he went sometimes when the count in
his head got loud enough that a room felt too small to hold it. She climbed up without asking, the
way she'd been climbing up to wherever he was for longer than either of them tracked in years
anymore, and sat down beside him with her boots hanging off the edge.

"I bought a house," she said.

He didn't turn his head. "Bought it for what."

"For nothing. For a roof that doesn't leak and a window that faces the water and a kitchen I intend
to be bad at cooking in for the rest of my life." She let that sit a moment. "Half a day's walk from
the coast road. No name that means anything to anyone. I want you to come see it."

"When."

"Not for a job. Not for a route survey. Not because the house is useful." She finally looked at him.
"For nothing, Daba. I'm asking you to come look at a house I bought for no reason, because I am
tired, and because I would like to be tired somewhere with you in it."

He was quiet long enough that she almost thought he hadn't understood the question, which she knew
better than to believe, because he understood every question. He simply didn't have practice
answering the kind that had no operational shape to hold onto.

"I don't know how to want a house," he said finally.

"I didn't ask if you knew how. I asked if you'd come see it."

"That's not what you asked. You asked me to come for nothing. That's different from coming."

Mika exhaled, not quite a laugh. "You count everything. Even my sentences."

"You taught me that was useful."

"I taught you counting kept the network alive. I never taught you it had to keep you that way too."
She drew her knees up, wrapping her arms around them, watching the same rooftops he'd been watching.
"I'm not leaving 1804. I'm not asking you to leave it either. I bought one house, half a day's walk
from a road that isn't dangerous, and I would like you to have somewhere that isn't a ledger to
stand in sometimes. That's all this is. It doesn't need a reason bigger than that."

"Everything needs a reason."

"No," Mika said. "That's the thing you built because you had to. It isn't a law of the world. It's
a law of a night eight years before the Rebellion, and you've been living inside it since, and I
love you enough to tell you that to your face instead of letting you count your way around it."

He said nothing for a long time. Below them, the district went about the business of an ordinary
evening — a cart, a shuttered stall, a child's voice calling a name that wasn't Fennic's, that had
never been Fennic's, and still, twenty-some years on, made something in his chest tighten for half a
breath before releasing.

"If I go and see the house," he said slowly, working the sentence out the way he worked out an
approach route, one confirmed step at a time, "and I like it. If some part of me actually wants to
be there. What does that mean about the part that's supposed to be counting."

"It doesn't mean anything about that part. It means you also have this part. People have more than
one part, Daba. It's not a contradiction to resolve. It's just — more of you than the ledger got to
keep."

He thought about Chronicle XL's own list, the one he read once a year and called accounting rather
than penance, and about the answer he'd given Wrenna at the closing meal, honest and empty in equal
measure: *I don't know. I've never known.* He had told the truth then. He suspected he was about to
tell a different truth now, one that didn't cancel the first, only sat beside it.

"I don't think I can tell you what I want," he said. "I've never had practice at it. But I can tell
you what I don't want, which might be the same door from the other side: I don't want the list to
grow, ever again, past whatever it already is. That's the only want I've ever been certain of. If
your house doesn't ask me to trade that away — if it's not instead of the count, just alongside it —
then I think I would like to see it."

Mika reached over and took his hand, the specific, undramatic gesture of two people who had already
survived the hard part of a conversation and didn't need to make ceremony of the rest.

"It's not instead of anything," she said. "It's just a window and a kitchen and a bad cook. Come
whenever the count lets you."

"I don't know when that will be."

"I know," she said. "I bought a house, not a deadline."

They sat on the roof until the evening cart finished its round and the district's ordinary sounds
thinned into the ordinary quiet of a night with nothing wrong in it, which was, for 1804, its own
kind of rare accounting: a day that closed with no name added to the list, and — for the first time
either of them could remember asking the question out loud — one small, real thing that Daba might,
someday, want simply because he wanted it.

He did not go see the house that week. He did not decide against it either. The door Mika had opened
stayed open, unclaimed and unrefused, which was, for a man who had spent his whole adult life
demanding an answer before he'd act on anything, its own kind of new arithmetic: a question he was,
for once, allowing to stay open on purpose.
