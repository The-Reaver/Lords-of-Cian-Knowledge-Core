# The Lie Told in His Name

*Locked canon, Batch 231, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1029`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LVIII, wave 20.
Rebellion era, pre-Black-Trench. A false confession offered out of love rather than malice tests
whether the reputation can survive being borrowed honestly. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose.*

---

The clerk was seventeen, thin-wristed, and had clearly rehearsed the speech before walking into the
owner's office and saying it too fast: he had been skimming wages for four months, he was sorry, and
he understood the Trench Monarch's crew already knew and had told him to come forward on his own
terms rather than be exposed.

None of that last part was true. Garren Hask heard about it within the hour, because the district's
owners had learned by now that any confession invoking the name got checked against the tally sheets
before anyone believed it. He checked this one twice, because the first pass didn't make sense: the
boy's shift rotation never touched the ledger drawer where the skimmed coin would have to come from.
Someone else's did — a laundry-and-errand circuit that ran through the same office twice a week, on a
schedule that belonged to his mother.

Kanja found her before the owner's men did, in the small room behind the tally house where she'd
been hiding since her son's confession bought her exactly enough time. She didn't deny it. She laid
out the numbers herself, unprompted, the way the district had learned to — four months of small
amounts, never more than she needed, each one matched almost to the coin against an apothecary
receipt for medicine her son didn't know she was still paying for. She'd let him take the blame
because a confessed thief with connections to the Trench Monarch got protected pending review. A
confessed thief with none got beaten before sundown.

"He lied under my name," Kanja said, not as an accusation yet, just laying the fact down between
them the way he'd lay down a tally sheet. "I never authorized that. I never even knew him."

"I know," she said. "I told him you'd forgive it faster than the alternative would kill him."

He sat with that longer than he expected to. The impulse underneath the lie wasn't the problem — a
son protecting his mother was not a thing that needed correcting, and no version of the district's
justice he'd ever fought for wanted to be the kind that punished that instinct out of people. The
problem was narrower and colder: a name that meant anything had to mean the same thing whether or not
someone was watching, and a lie borrowed under it, even a kind one, was still a debt opened in his
name without his knowledge or consent.

He went to the owner's office with both of them the next morning and put the real tally down first,
before either of them could speak — the exact shortfall, the exact receipt, four months matched to
the coin. He didn't ask for forgiveness on her behalf. He asked for restitution through labor at a
fair rate against the balance, the same terms he'd have negotiated for any worker caught short, and
made clear the Trench Monarch's crew had authorized nothing and shielded no one — the numbers alone
were doing the asking.

The owner took the deal, mostly because refusing it in front of a tally sheet that precise had
stopped being good business months ago.

Afterward, the boy tried to apologize again, and Kanja stopped him halfway through it. "Don't
apologize for loving her enough to lie for her," he said. "Apologize for thinking my name was a coat
you could borrow without telling me. Next time you need protection, ask. I'll give it honestly. What
you took instead wasn't mine to give."
