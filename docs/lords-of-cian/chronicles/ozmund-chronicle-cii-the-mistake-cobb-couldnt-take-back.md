# The Mistake Cobb Couldn't Take Back

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1831`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle CII, Wider Verehimu Household/Guard strand, wave 2. Set strictly pre-Fulfillment-Ceremony
(`MCD-025`), several years after Chronicle XLIV — Aethelgard alive, Ozmund a young man already taking
a real hand in the House's daily governance. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per
`VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, one of the rare pieces the General gave him directly rather than secondhand.
A genuine mistake and its aftermath, handled with grace — extending Chronicle XLIV's "fear is not a
reason to stop" lesson into its natural companion: a mistake is not a reason for shame. No new named
characters.*

---

The General told me this one not long after he told me about the horse stall, on the same quiet
evening, as if the two stories belonged together in his own memory even though years separated them.
He said this second one was harder to tell, and that he'd thought about it more, over the decades,
than almost anything else from those years that hadn't involved his father directly.

Cobb was older by then, well past being a stable hand nobody minded losing track of — trusted, by that
point, with animals the rest of the staff still handled carefully. The General said there had been a
new gelding in the stable that season, strong and green and not yet fully steady, and that Cobb had been
given the evening feeding alone on a night when the usual second hand was ill. He had latched the stall
the way he always did. He said, afterward, that he would have sworn on anything asked of him that he had
latched it properly, and the General believed him — not out of loyalty, he was careful to tell me, but
because he had watched Cobb work animals long enough by then to know precisely how careful the man
actually was.

The latch failed anyway, sometime in the night, for reasons no one ever fully worked out — a fault in
the hardware as likely as any error of Cobb's own hands. The gelding got loose, panicked in the dark
stable yard, and put its shoulder through a section of fencing before anyone woke to the noise. No one
was hurt. The horse was recovered by morning with nothing worse than a few scrapes. But Cobb, the
General said, had convinced himself entirely that the fault was his — that he had latched it wrong, or
not fully, or simply badly, and that he had failed at the one piece of trust the House had finally
extended him after years of smaller work.

The General told me he had found Cobb himself, before the steward or the head groom had even been
informed, sitting outside the damaged stall in the pre-dawn cold, not moving, refusing to go back inside
until someone told him what his punishment would be. He said Cobb wouldn't accept that there might not
be one — that a man who couldn't be trusted to latch a gate had no business being trusted with anything
else, and that he expected, plainly, to be sent back to mucking stalls for the rest of his working life
if he wasn't dismissed outright.

What the General said he remembered most clearly, telling it to me all those years later, was how
completely Cobb's own account of himself had collapsed the moment something had actually gone wrong —
the same steady, unshakeable man who had once told a curious boy that fear wasn't a reason to stop
working, unable to extend himself the same mercy over an honest mistake he might not even have made. The
General said he'd thought, sitting there in the cold beside him, of a tutor's voice telling a much
younger version of himself that a wrong method dressed as a right answer was worse than an honest
failure — and understood, for what he said felt like the first time from the other side of it, that the
lesson had never really been about arithmetic at all.

He told Cobb, he said, only what he actually believed: that a hinge could fail on its own, that a man
who checked his work as carefully as Cobb always had didn't stop being trustworthy because a fault
found him rather than the reverse, and that the House wasn't in the business of measuring a man's whole
worth by the one night something went wrong that he couldn't have prevented even doing everything right.
He had the hardware replaced across the whole stable block within the week, quietly, without ever
saying why, so that no one — least of all Cobb — would need to keep wondering whose fault it had
actually been.

The General said Cobb never fully let go of that night, not entirely, in all the years he knew him
after. But he said something in the way Cobb carried himself afterward had changed, all the same — the
particular, permanent difference, the General told me, between a man who has been forgiven and a man
who has simply never been asked to answer for something he didn't do.
