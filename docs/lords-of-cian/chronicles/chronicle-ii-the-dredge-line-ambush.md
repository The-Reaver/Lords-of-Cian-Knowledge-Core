## SCENE 1: THE NEW SIXTY

They came in ones and twos, in the way that dockworkers came to everything: on their own time, through the doors they knew, carrying the weight of the shift they had worked and the decision they had already made before they walked in.

The back room of the Anchor and Anvil was too small for a hundred men. Kanja had known it would be, and he had not moved the meeting to a larger space, because a larger space meant a space he did not control and the Anchor and Anvil's back room had one exit, one window, and a kitchen staff that asked no questions because the owner owed Garren Hask a debt older than the building's current roof. So the hundred filled the room in layers: the original forty against the walls and on the crates, the new sixty standing in the center and the doorway and the kitchen corridor, shoulder to shoulder, breathing each other's air.

Kanja stood at the long table. He did not stand on a crate. He did not elevate himself. He was five feet eleven inches tall and he spoke at the volume of a man giving instructions to the worker beside him, because that was what he was doing. The room leaned in to hear. This was more effective than shouting and he knew it and he did not know whether he knew it by instinct or by calculation and he did not spend time determining which.

"Who sent you?" he asked the room.

Silence. Then a woman's voice from the center of the crowd. Middle-aged. Strong Portside accent, the vowels flattened by years of talking over machinery.

"Nobody sent us. The rubbings did."

Her name was Efa Gol. She operated the cargo press at Warehouse Twelve, a hydraulic machine that compressed bulk cargo into standardized shipping bales. She had worked the press for fourteen years. Her hands were wider than Kanja's, and her forearms carried the specific development of a person who had spent a decade and a half pulling a lever that resisted pulling. She was forty-one years old. She had brought eleven people with her, all from Warehouse Twelve's day shift, all of whom had tested their own Scrip-notes after seeing the rubbings and heard the same result.

"You tested the notes," Kanja said.

"Every one of us. My supervisor brought his own assay kit from home. Fourteen percent across the board. Three years of fourteen percent." She looked at him with the flat assessment of a woman who operated heavy machinery and did not tolerate imprecision. "You burned the Forge. That's done. What's next?"

The room held the question the way a hull holds water: completely, with no space for it to go except deeper.

"The Sovereign Trust is sending a punitive column into the Dredge-Line," Kanja said. "Two hundred infantry. Twelve patrol boats. A Compliance Officer with a fine-book and the authority to levy charges against any business in the district. They are not coming to investigate the fire. They are coming to punish the district for harboring the people who set it."

"When?" This from a man in the doorway. Young, mid-twenties. Dockhand's build. He had not given his name.

"Eighteen days. The Southern District Command's deployment orders follow a standard logistics chain. Troop allocation, transport requisition, operational briefing, march order. The chain takes twenty-one days from authorization to deployment. The authorization went through three days ago." Kanja paused. "My source is reliable."

He did not name his source. The source was a clerk in the Southern District Command's logistics office who had been paid in debased Scrip for four years and had, after seeing the charcoal rubbings, walked into a Portside tavern and asked if anyone knew how to reach the people who had burned Forge-7. The clerk's information was precise. The deployment would arrive in eighteen days, entering the Dredge-Line from the northern canal mouth, advancing along the main towpath, conducting "structural inspections" of every canal-side warehouse. The inspections were a pretext. The fines were the operation.

Dol Maren spoke from his position against the back wall, where he stood with his arms folded and his weight on his back foot. "Two hundred soldiers. Five hundred to seven hundred times density each. Twelve boats with crew. Plus the Compliance Officer and whatever bureaucratic escort he drags in." The crane operator's voice was the same gravel-drum monotone it had been in the first meeting. "And we are a hundred dockworkers."

"A hundred dockworkers who know the Dredge-Line," Kanja said.

"Knowing the terrain and surviving the terrain are not the same conversation."

"They are if you change the terrain."

Maren looked at him. The room looked at him. Kanja let the silence hold for three seconds, because three seconds was the amount of time it took for a room full of workers to stop thinking about what he had said and start thinking about what he meant.

"The Dredge-Line's lock system controls the water level in the lower canal network," he said. "The locks are mechanical. Hand-cranked iron gates. Three of those gates sit between the upper canal, which holds approximately 200,000 gallons of industrial runoff, and the lower canal towpath that the column will be marching on. If those three gates open simultaneously, the lower canal floods. The towpath disappears under three feet of water."

Callum Breck, standing near the door with the posture of a man who had not slept in a week because his daughter was teething, tilted his head. "Three feet of water stops a column?"

"Three feet of water stops a column that weighs five hundred times what we do," Kanja said. "A baseline man in three feet of canal sludge is wading. A seven-hundred-times-density soldier in three feet of canal sludge is sinking. Their mass drives them into the canal bed. The heavier the soldier, the deeper they sink. Full Dead Drakma plate armor in industrial muck, with that kind of density behind it, goes waist-deep in seconds."

The room processed this. Kanja could see the arithmetic moving through the faces. Dockworkers understood weight distribution. They loaded cargo for a living. They knew what happened when you put too much mass on a surface that could not bear it. Every man and woman in the room had seen a pallet break through a deck plate because the load exceeded the plate's rated capacity. The principle was the same. The canal bed was the deck plate. The soldiers were the overloaded pallet.

Efa Gol broke the silence. "And while they're sinking, where are we?"

"Above them. The Dredge-Line has elevated walkways connecting the warehouse rooftops. Maintenance access. Crane gantries. Service bridges. I am going to pre-position plank-bridges across seventeen points along the column's projected route, three meters above the canal level. We walk the planks. They wade the muck. We are baseline. We weigh nothing compared to them. The planks hold us. The planks would not hold them even if they could reach them."

"You want us to stand on planks above two hundred sinking soldiers and do what, exactly?"

"Disarm them. Cargo hooks, crane cables, dock winches. We pull them out of the muck one at a time, strip their weapons and armor, and put them on dry ground at the district's edge. No killing. Disarmed, de-armored, and covered in canal sludge."

Efa looked at him for a long time. Her expression did not change. Press operators did not change expression during load calculations any more than crane operators did.

"No killing," she repeated.

"No killing. We are dockworkers defending our district from an illegal shakedown. We are not soldiers fighting a war. The moment we kill a Trust infantry, we become an insurgency. An insurgency gets a military response. A neighborhood that humiliates a shakedown column gets a bureaucratic response. The bureaucratic response takes months. The military response takes days."

"You've done this math."

"I've done nothing else for three weeks."

Garren Hask, who had been silent through the entire exchange, cleared his throat. It was the sound of an older man deciding to spend the authority that his age and his thirty-one years on the docks had accumulated.

"The boy's math was right at the Forge," Hask said. The room listened to Hask the way a dock crew listened to its lead hand: because the lead hand's judgment was backed by scar tissue. "Forty men. No casualties. The rubbings did more damage than the fire. If he says no killing, the math has a reason."

Kanja looked at Hask and felt something he did not have a word for. It was not gratitude, because gratitude implied a debt and Hask was not lending anything. It was closer to recognition. The old smith had understood the argument and was translating it into the dialect the room trusted: experience confirming calculation.

The man in the doorway who had not given his name spoke again. "What about the Compliance Officer?"

"The Compliance Officer is a bureaucrat with a fine-book. He is not a combatant. He does not get pulled into the muck. He gets relocated."

"Relocated where?"

Kanja allowed himself the first and only smile of the evening. It was small, brief, and it carried the specific warmth of an engineer who had solved an elegant problem and was about to share the solution.

"A rooftop. Clean, dry, with an excellent view of his column learning to swim."

No one laughed. But Efa Gol's mouth did something that was not quite a smile and not quite a press operator's neutral assessment, and the room's posture shifted from evaluation to commitment in a way that had nothing to do with the plan and everything to do with the fact that the man proposing the plan was eighteen years old and had the mechanical precision of a fifty-year-old foreman and the grin of a boy who had not yet learned to hide the parts of himself that enjoyed the work.

## SCENE 2: THE LOCKS

For eighteen days, Kanja's people worked.

They worked the way dockworkers worked: in shifts, on schedule, with the tools they brought from home and the skills they had been building since they were old enough to hold a wrench. The Dredge-Line was their district. They had built its infrastructure with their fathers and their fathers' fathers. They knew where the lock mechanisms were housed because their grandfathers had installed them. They knew which walkway beams were load-bearing and which were cosmetic because the original builders' families still lived in the warehouses overhead. The Sovereign Trust thought the Dredge-Line was a municipal waterway managed by a government agency. It was a neighborhood maintained by the people who lived in it, and the people who lived in it were now preparing it for a conversation the Sovereign Trust was not expecting to have.

Kanja ran the lock modifications himself. He worked the night shifts, after the regular dock operations closed, in the mechanical housing of Lock Gate Seven with Dol Maren and a woman named Pell Ostra who had been maintaining the Dredge-Line's lock system for twenty-two years and could diagnose a gate fault by the sound the crank handle made when she turned it.

"You want the gates to open fast," Pell said on the third night, her hands inside the mechanism housing, grease to her elbows. She was speaking the way mechanics spoke: to the problem, not to the person asking about it. "These gates are designed to open slow. Controlled. You crank them down over ninety seconds and the water equalizes gradually. You want all three to drop in, what, two seconds?"

"Simultaneously," Kanja said. He was kneeling beside the mechanism, holding a lamp while Pell worked. The housing smelled of canal water, iron oxide, and the mineral grease that the lock system consumed at a rate of two gallons per month. "All three gates. Two seconds. No crank."

"Quick-release pins," Pell said. She pulled a component from the mechanism and held it to the lamp. A locking pin, iron, six inches long, fitted into a bracket that held the gate in its closed position. "I replace these with a shear-pin variant. Same dimensions. Half the tensile strength. One hard pull and the pin snaps. The gate drops. The water follows."

"How long to install?"

"Per gate? Three hours. I need to machine the shear-pins on my lathe at home. The dimensions have to match within a tenth of a millimeter or the gate won't seat properly and the lock leaks before we want it to." She wiped her hands on her trousers. "I'll need material. The pins need to be iron, not steel. Steel shear-pins don't snap clean. They bend and jam."

"I have iron stock at the dock-smithy."

"Then bring me four pounds of round bar, three-quarter inch, and I'll have the pins ready in two days."

Maren, who had been measuring the walkway beam above Lock Gate Seven with a plumb line and a length of knotted rope, lowered himself from the gantry and joined them at the mechanism housing. The crane operator moved the way large, careful men moved in confined spaces: deliberately, with an awareness of where his mass was relative to the things that could break if he put his mass in the wrong place.

"The walkway beams at this section are rated for twelve hundred pounds," he said. "That holds four baseline adults standing still. Moving, with equipment, you lose about thirty percent to dynamic load. Call it three people per beam section in motion."

"How many beam sections along the column's route?"

"Seventeen where I can position planks. Some of them are continuous runs. Some are short spans between gantry supports. The longest run is forty meters along the Warehouse Row stretch. The shortest is six meters over the junction at Lock Gate Three." He paused, running the calculation. "If we limit each section to three fighters moving at a time and rotate them through on a stagger, we can deploy the full hundred across the seventeen positions in under eight minutes."

"The column's march rate along the towpath is approximately thirteen meters per minute," Kanja said. "Four hundred meters in thirty minutes. That puts the front of the column at Lock Gate Seven when the tail is still at the northern canal mouth. The entire column is in the canal when I release the gates."

"You need them at four hundred meters precisely," Maren said.

"I need the Compliance Officer at four hundred meters precisely. The infantry will be spread along the full route behind him. The Compliance Officer sets the pace because the Compliance Officer stops at every warehouse to issue fines. Each stop takes approximately four minutes. Ten warehouses along the route. That's forty minutes of stops plus thirteen minutes of walking per hundred meters." He looked at the towpath stretching south through the lantern light. "They will be in the canal for over an hour before they reach four hundred meters. I will know exactly where the Compliance Officer is at every moment because he will be the one with the fine-book, standing in a doorway, taking longer than he needs to because bureaucrats bill by the hour."

Pell Ostra, who had returned to the mechanism housing and was sketching the shear-pin dimensions on a scrap of oilcloth with a grease pencil, spoke without looking up. "You talk about the column like it's a piece of machinery."

"It is a piece of machinery," Kanja said. "It has a power source, a drive mechanism, a load rating, and a failure mode. The power source is the deployment order. The drive mechanism is the Compliance Officer. The load rating is the towpath's bearing capacity versus the column's aggregate density. The failure mode is what happens when the bearing capacity drops to zero."

"And the soldiers? The two hundred men in armor who think they're here to kick doors and collect fines?"

"The soldiers are components. They are doing what the machinery tells them to do. When the machinery breaks, the components stop. We remove the components from the broken machinery, strip the parts we need, and send the rest home."

Pell looked up from her sketch. She had grey hair tied back with a cable tie and the face of a woman who had been solving mechanical problems for longer than Kanja had been alive. "You're eighteen years old," she said.

"Yes."

"You sound like you're describing a pump rebuild."

"A pump is simpler. A pump doesn't have a Compliance Officer."

Pell held his gaze for a moment. Then she returned to her sketch. "Four pounds of round bar, three-quarter inch, at my workshop by tomorrow evening. I'll have your pins in two days."

The plank-bridges went up over the next twelve nights. Maren supervised the installation, working with eight volunteers who had experience on the warehouse roof crews and were comfortable at height. The planks were cargo-grade hardwood, the same lumber used for dock decking, rated for the loads that dockworkers moved every day. They were positioned across the walkway gaps and lashed to the gantry supports with the double-wrap knot that every crane operator learned in their first week. From the canal level, three meters below, the planks looked like maintenance scaffolding. Temporary structures left in place by a work crew that had not bothered to take them down. No one questioned temporary structures in the Dredge-Line. The district was made of temporary structures that had become permanent through neglect.

On the fourteenth night, Kanja walked the entire route. He started at the northern canal mouth and moved south along the upper walkways, crossing each plank-bridge, testing each span with his weight and his stride and the specific attention of a man who was going to put a hundred people on these structures and needed to know that every one of them would hold. Maren walked behind him, counting steps, noting the flex points where the planks bowed under load, marking the sections where additional lashing was needed.

They reached the seventeenth and final bridge, a six-meter span over the junction at Lock Gate Three, and Kanja stopped. Below them, the lower canal stretched south in the darkness, its surface still, its water black, its smell the permanent chemical signature of a working industrial waterway. In eighteen days, this canal would be three feet deeper and the color of everything the district's industry had flushed into it for the past decade.

"How do you know they won't turn back?" Maren asked. It was the first time the crane operator had asked a question that was not about load ratings or beam specifications. The question carried the weight of a man who had committed to a plan and was now, in the quiet of the fourteenth night, allowing himself to consider the possibility that the plan's assumptions were wrong.

Kanja looked at the canal. "Because the Compliance Officer has a quota. Every punitive column generates a revenue report. The fines collected have to justify the deployment cost. If the column turns back before the Compliance Officer meets his quota, the deployment is classified as a loss, and the officer who authorized it faces review. The column cannot afford to turn back. Their bureaucracy won't let them."

"You're betting a hundred people's safety on a bureaucrat's performance metrics."

"I am betting a hundred people's safety on the one thing the Sovereign Trust values more than territory. Accounting."

Maren was quiet for a while. The canal breathed below them. Somewhere in the warehouse district, a night-shift crane was moving cargo, its motor a low hum in the distance.

"My father operated Crane Six for thirty-four years," Maren said. "He told me one thing about load management that I've never forgotten. He said: the load doesn't care what you think it weighs. The load weighs what it weighs. If you guess wrong, the crane doesn't argue with you. It falls."

Kanja waited. Maren was not a man who spoke in preamble. If he was telling a story, the story had a terminal point.

"You're not guessing," Maren said. "I've watched you work these locks for two weeks. You measured the canal depth at six points. You calculated the sludge density with samples. You timed the lock release with Pell's shear-pins and a stopwatch. You mapped the towpath's load capacity by walking it in weighted boots." He looked at Kanja. "You're not guessing. You're reading the load. That's the only reason I'm standing on this plank."

It was the longest statement Dol Maren had ever made in Kanja's presence, and it was the closest thing to trust that a crane operator could express. Trust, for a crane operator, was not faith. It was the professional confidence that the numbers had been verified and the load was within tolerance.

"The load is within tolerance," Kanja said.

Maren nodded. They walked back north along the planks in the dark, and neither of them said anything else, because the conversation was complete and the work was not.

## SCENE 3: THE INVITATION

On the eighteenth day, the column came.

Kanja heard them before he saw them. Two hundred pairs of boots on cobblestone, the cadence of a march that was not a march because punitive columns did not march. They ambled. They ambled with the specific arrogance of armed men entering a neighborhood that could not fight back, and the sound of their amble was the sound of two hundred sets of Dead Drakma plate armor clinking against two hundred sets of regulation weapon harnesses as two hundred soldiers walked at the pace of the slowest bureaucrat in the formation.

The Compliance Officer led. He was a small man in a clean grey coat, carrying a leather portfolio under his arm and wearing shoes that had never touched canal water. He walked ten paces ahead of the lead infantry squad, flanked by two soldiers who served as his escort and his witnesses. The portfolio contained his fine-book, his authority stamps, and the blank levy forms he would fill out at each warehouse. His name, Kanja would learn later, was Tavin Greer. He had been a Compliance Officer for eleven years. He had levied fines totaling more than 400,000 Scrip in that time. He had never visited the districts he fined. He worked from deployment manifests and revenue projections. This was his first field operation.

He looked, from Kanja's position on the Warehouse Row walkway three meters above, like a man who had been told he was visiting a neighborhood and had dressed for a different neighborhood entirely.

The twelve patrol boats entered the canal behind the infantry. Flat-bottomed, armored, each carrying a crew of six and a forward-mounted Blight Frequency projector that could suppress biological function at twenty meters. The boats sat low in the water, their Dead Drakma hulls drawing nearly a meter of draft. They were designed for clean harbors and maintained channels. The Dredge-Line's canal was neither.

Kanja lay flat on the Warehouse Row walkway, watching through the gap between two plank sections. Beside him, Efa Gol lay in the same position, watching the same column, her breathing steady, her hands flat on the walkway timbers.

"The one in the grey coat," Efa said. "That's the one with the fines."

"That's the one."

"He doesn't walk like a man who's been in a canal district before."

"He hasn't. His deployment history is administrative. He levies fines from an office. This is his first field assignment."

"How do you know his deployment history?"

"The same clerk who told me about the column."

Efa absorbed this. Below them, the Compliance Officer stopped at the first warehouse. He consulted his portfolio. He spoke to his escort. One of the soldiers stepped forward and pounded on the warehouse door with a gauntleted fist that made the sound of a sledgehammer on oak.

The door opened. A warehouse foreman, a man Kanja recognized from the cargo exchange on Pier Nine, stood in the doorway. The Compliance Officer spoke. The foreman spoke. The Compliance Officer opened his fine-book and began writing.

"Four minutes," Kanja said.

"What?"

"Each inspection takes approximately four minutes. He stops, he writes, he stamps, he moves. Ten warehouses along the route. They will be in the canal for over an hour."

Efa watched the scene below. The foreman was arguing. The Compliance Officer was not listening. The escort soldiers were standing with the passive weight of men who had been ordered to stand and would stand until ordered otherwise. The column behind them had halted. Two hundred soldiers, standing on the towpath, waiting for a bureaucrat to finish writing a number in a book.

"Four minutes," Efa said. "For a crime that doesn't exist. For a fine on a violation he invented before he got here."

"Yes."

"And every Scrip-note he collects is worth fourteen percent of what it says on the paper."

"Yes."

Efa was quiet for a moment. Then she said, in the flat tone of a woman who operated a cargo press and understood the mechanics of compressive force: "Open the locks."

"Not yet. He's at the first warehouse. I need him at the tenth. Four hundred meters in. The entire column spread along the full length of the canal. No gaps. No section of the towpath unoccupied."

"You want them all in."

"I want them all in."

They watched. The Compliance Officer finished at the first warehouse. He moved to the second. Four minutes. The column advanced. The patrol boats followed, their crews looking bored, their Blight projectors powered down because there was nothing to project against. The district was quiet. The district was cooperating. The district was doing exactly what a punished district was supposed to do: accepting the shakedown, opening the doors, letting the fines accumulate.

Kanja counted warehouses. Second. Third. Fourth. Each stop identical. Each fine levied. Each foreman standing in a doorway watching a man in a grey coat write numbers that would cost their business months of revenue for infractions that had been invented on the march.

At the seventh warehouse, the foreman was a woman Kanja had never met. She stood in her doorway and listened to the Compliance Officer recite his authority and did not speak. The Compliance Officer wrote the fine. He tore the form from his book and held it out to her. She took it. She looked at it. Then she looked up at the walkway above the canal, three meters overhead, where she could not possibly see Kanja lying flat in the shadow between two plank sections, and she folded the fine into her apron pocket and closed her door.

She had looked up. She knew. Someone in the district had told her. Someone had told all the foremen. The doors were opening because the foremen knew what was coming and understood that the doors needed to open, the fines needed to be levied, and the column needed to advance to four hundred meters where the boy who had burned Forge-7 was waiting for the canal to do the work.

Eighth warehouse. Ninth. The column was stretched along three hundred and fifty meters of towpath. The patrol boats were spaced evenly through the canal, each one sitting deep in water that was already thickening with disturbed bottom sediment. The soldiers' boots were wet. The towpath surface, which had been dry at the northern entrance, was slick with the film of industrial residue that coated every horizontal surface in the Dredge-Line.

Tenth warehouse. The Compliance Officer stopped. He opened his fine-book. Four hundred meters.

Kanja stood up on the walkway. He was visible, for one second, to anyone below who happened to look up. No one looked up. He raised his right hand.

Fifty meters north, Pell Ostra saw the signal. She pulled the release cord on Lock Gate Seven. The shear-pin snapped. The gate dropped.

Fifty meters south of Pell, a dockworker named Torren Vace pulled the release cord on Lock Gate Three. The shear-pin snapped. The gate dropped.

One hundred meters east, on the cross-canal junction, Garren Hask pulled the release cord on Lock Gate Twelve. The shear-pin snapped. The gate dropped.

Three gates. Two seconds. 200,000 gallons of the Dredge-Line's upper canal, black with forge ash and fish oil and machine lubricant and the accumulated industrial waste of a working waterway, cascaded into the lower canal network with a sound like a building sitting down.

## SCENE 4: THE WEIGHT OF THINGS

The water came from three directions. North, south, and east, converging on the four-hundred-meter stretch of towpath where two hundred Sovereign Trust soldiers stood in formation and a Compliance Officer stood in a warehouse doorway writing a fine that would never be collected.

The first thing that changed was the sound. The canal, which had been still, began to roar. Not the roar of a river or a waterfall. The roar of fluid mass displacing air in a confined space, compressing the atmosphere above the canal surface, pushing a pressure wave ahead of it that hit the soldiers before the water did. The pressure wave smelled. It smelled of forge ash and decomposed fish and the chemical residue of a thousand industrial processes that had been flushing their waste into the upper canal for years.

The second thing that changed was the ground.

The towpath vanished. Not gradually. The water level rose three feet in under two minutes, and the towpath, which was an elevated walkway only eighteen inches above the normal canal surface, went from dry to submerged to gone. The soldiers, who had been standing on solid ground, were standing in liquid. Black liquid. Thick with silt and industrial particulate. Warm from the forge runoff. The consistency of heavy oil.

The third thing that changed was the soldiers.

They sank.

Five hundred times density. Seven hundred times density. Officers in full Dead Drakma plate, their armor adding mass to bodies that were already denser than the material world was built to accommodate. In clean water, on solid ground, this density was an advantage. In industrial sludge with no bottom harder than compacted mud, this density was a sentence. Their boots went first. Then their shins. Then their knees. The heavier the soldier, the faster the descent. A 500x infantryman in light armor sank to his calves and stopped, the mud's bearing capacity matching his mass at approximately eighteen inches. A 700x officer in full plate went to his waist in eight seconds and kept going, his armor's weight pulling him into canal bed material that had the structural properties of wet concrete and the smell of a rendering plant.

The patrol boats fared worse. Their propulsion systems, designed for clean harbor water, choked on the floating debris. Forge ash clogged the intake manifolds. Fish oil filmed the rudder assemblies. The boats lost steerage and began drifting into the canal walls, grinding their Dead Drakma hulls against the brick, the sound of metal on stone rising above the roar of the water. Three boats ran aground on the suddenly shallow canal floor, their keels buried in the same sludge that was swallowing the infantry. The crews, lighter than the soldiers but still confined to vessels that were no longer moving, could do nothing except watch the water rise and the formation dissolve.

From above, from the plank-bridges three meters overhead, Kanja's hundred fighters moved.

They moved in threes, as Maren had calculated. Three per beam section, staggered, crossing the planks at the controlled pace of people who worked at height for a living and did not hurry on narrow surfaces regardless of what was happening below. They carried cargo hooks and crane cables and the dock winches that every warehouse loading bay kept mounted to the gantry supports for moving heavy freight.

The work was methodical. A soldier, sunk to his thighs in canal muck, struggling to free himself, his armor working against him with every attempt to move, looked up and saw a dockworker on a plank overhead lowering a cargo hook on a crane cable. The hook caught the soldier's weapon harness. The winch engaged. The soldier was lifted bodily from the muck with a sucking sound that Kanja would remember for years, the sludge releasing him the way wet clay releases a brick from a mold.

The soldier rose. The cable carried him to the walkway level, dripping black muck, his armor smeared with industrial waste. Two fighters on the walkway received him. They stripped his weapon harness. They unbuckled his armor plates. They set him, disarmed and de-armored, on the walkway surface, and pointed north, toward the canal district's edge, toward dry ground.

"Walk," said the fighter closest to him. A woman. Efa Gol. Her voice was steady. "Your weapons stay. Your armor stays. You walk."

The soldier walked. He walked north along the walkway, unarmed, unarmored, covered in sludge, and behind him the next soldier was already rising on the crane cable, and behind that soldier the next, and the next, the work proceeding at the pace of a dock crew unloading cargo from a hold. Which was, in functional terms, what it was.

Forty minutes. One hundred dockworkers processed two hundred soldiers through a system designed by a crane operator and executed by people whose bodies understood the mechanics of lifting, moving, and placing heavy loads. The soldiers did not fight. Some of them tried, in the first minutes, to resist the hooks and the cables, to swing at the fighters on the planks with weapons they could not stabilize because their footing was gone and their training had not included "combat in waist-deep industrial sludge against enemies you cannot reach." The resistance ended quickly. Not because the dockworkers overpowered them. Because the sludge overpowered them. Every attempt to swing a weapon drove the soldier deeper into the muck. Every aggressive motion added kinetic energy that the canal bed converted to suction. Fighting back was sinking faster.

They stopped fighting. They let themselves be hooked. They let themselves be lifted. They let themselves be stripped and sent walking.

• • •

The Compliance Officer was on the roof.

Kanja had assigned this task to Callum Breck, because Breck was young and fast and light and because the image of a young father placing a Compliance Officer on a dry rooftop with his fine-book still in his hand was, in Kanja's assessment, the correct image for the aftermath. Breck had climbed down from the walkway as the water was rising, waded through the shallow end of the flood near the tenth warehouse where the sludge was only ankle-deep, and found Tavin Greer standing in the warehouse doorway with his shoes wet and his grey coat splashed and his leather portfolio clutched to his chest like a child holding a blanket.

"Come with me," Breck said.

"I am a Compliance Officer of the Bureau of Economic Stability and I am conducting a sanctioned enforcement action under Article Fourteen of the—"

"Come with me, sir. I'm going to put you somewhere dry."

Greer looked at the canal. He looked at his soldiers sinking. He looked at the dockworkers on the planks above, lifting armored men out of the muck with cargo hooks and the professional indifference of people who moved heavy objects for a living. He looked at Breck, who was twenty-two years old and standing in ankle-deep sludge and extending his hand with the patient courtesy of a man who dealt with uncooperative loads every day.

Greer took the hand.

Breck led him through the warehouse, up two flights of stairs, and onto the flat roof. The roof overlooked the canal. From the roof, the Compliance Officer had an unobstructed view of his column being disassembled by a workforce that treated the operation with the same efficiency they brought to their regular shifts.

Breck set a crate on the roof for the Compliance Officer to sit on. The crate was dry. The roof was dry. The view was comprehensive.

"Your fine-book," Breck said, pointing at the leather portfolio. "You can keep it. The fines aren't going to be collected, but you can keep the paperwork."

"This is insurrection," Greer said. His voice was steady. He was a bureaucrat, not a soldier, and bureaucrats processed events through institutional categories. What was happening below was, in his institutional vocabulary, insurrection. The word gave him something to hold.

Breck looked at him. Four months ago, Breck had been a dockhand who loaded cargo and went home to his wife and his daughter. Three weeks ago, he had walked the streets of Lower Portside at dawn nailing charcoal rubbings to doors. Now he was standing on a rooftop with a Sovereign Trust Compliance Officer while the canal below swallowed an army.

"It's not insurrection," Breck said. "It's a labor dispute."

He left the Compliance Officer on the roof with his fine-book and his crate and his view. On his way down, he stopped at the second-floor landing and took a piece of dock-marker chalk from his vest pocket. The chalk was the same grade used to mark cargo containers for routing. Breck held it in his hand for a moment, looking at it, thinking about his daughter, who would be named next week in a ceremony he had missed the preparation for because he had been helping an eighteen-year-old smith flood a canal.

When the Sovereign Trust's recovery team found Tavin Greer on the roof four hours later, the Compliance Officer was sitting on his crate with his fine-book open on his knees and the words THE TRENCH MONARCH written on his forehead in dock-marker chalk.

Breck had written them. He had not asked Kanja's permission. He had not consulted anyone. The name had come to him on the second-floor landing as he held the chalk and thought about a king's son who chose to work the docks and a father's daughter who would grow up in a district where the ground moved when it needed to move.

The Trench Monarch. The king of the low ground. The ruler of the places the Sovereign Trust did not want to walk. The name was not a title of grandeur. It was a title written in dock-marker chalk on a bureaucrat's forehead by a twenty-two-year-old father who had decided that the arithmetic of stolen labor was worth more than the architecture of obedience.

The name spread. By the end of the week, it was on walls.

## SCENE 5: THE CANAL AFTER

The Sovereign Trust did not send another column into the Dredge-Line.

The district settled into an autonomy that no one declared and no one challenged, because the cost of re-entering exceeded the value of the fines that could be collected and the institutional memory of two hundred soldiers walking home through Portside without their armor, smelling like a canal, was the kind of humiliation that accountants measured and generals avoided.

On the third evening after the flood, Kanja sat on the walkway above Lock Gate Seven with his legs hanging over the edge and the canal still draining below him. Pell Ostra had reinstalled the lock gates with new pins, standard tensile strength, and the canal was emptying at the rate of six inches per hour. In two weeks, the towpath would be passable. The twelve patrol boats, wedged into the canal walls and silted over, would take longer. The hulls were Dead Drakma and would survive, but the propulsion systems were destroyed and the interiors were filled with a substance that Pell described, with professional precision, as "everything the district would rather not discuss."

Garren Hask climbed the gantry ladder and sat beside him. The old smith moved carefully on the walkway. His knees were not what they had been, and the eighteen days of night work on the lock mechanisms had added a stiffness to his gait that he tried to hide and could not.

"The boy from the doorway," Hask said. "The one who asked when the column was coming. At the first meeting."

"I remember."

"He wants to know if there's going to be another meeting. Other people are asking. Not just the hundred. People in the district. People who weren't at the Anchor and Anvil."

Kanja looked at the canal. The water was dark, still carrying the silt and waste of the flood, but the surface was lower now and the walkway supports were visible again, crusted with the residue of what had passed through them.

"How many people?"

"I stopped counting at sixty. Efa Gol says Warehouse Twelve's entire night shift wants in. Pell has people from the lock maintenance crews asking her directly. Breck says the naming-ceremony crowd, thirty people, his wife's family, they all want to know." Hask paused. "The chalk on the Compliance Officer's forehead. That was Breck's work."

"I know."

"You didn't tell him to do it."

"No."

"The name. Trench Monarch. That's his, not yours."

"I know that too."

Hask sat with this for a moment. The canal made the small, settling sounds of water finding its new level. Somewhere in the warehouse district, a night-shift crew was working, their voices carrying over the rooftops in the fragments of a song Kanja did not recognize.

"Kanja." Hask used his name. Not "boy." Not "son." The name, spoken the way a lead hand speaks a coworker's name when the conversation is about to change weight. "What you did at the Forge was a job. Forty men, one building, one night. What you did here was a campaign. A hundred men, three weeks of preparation, an engagement against two hundred soldiers. And nobody died."

"That was the plan."

"The plan worked. The next plan will be bigger. The plan after that will be bigger than that. And somewhere along the line, the Sovereign Trust is going to stop sending shakedown columns and start sending something that does not walk home when you take its armor."

The old smith looked at Kanja with the expression of a man who had been working steel for thirty-one years and knew the difference between a tool designed for a single job and a tool designed for a lifetime of work.

"You need to decide what this is," Hask said. "A job or a trade. Because jobs end. Trades don't."

Kanja looked at the canal. He looked at the walkways. He looked at the plank-bridges that Maren had built, still in place, still lashed to the gantry supports with the double-wrap knots that crane operators learned in their first week. Temporary structures. Already becoming permanent through use.

"A trade," Kanja said.

Hask nodded. He did not say anything else. He climbed down the gantry ladder with the care of a man whose knees had been better thirty years ago and whose judgment had not, and he walked north along the towpath toward the district's edge, toward the dock-rows, toward the forge where he had worked for thirty-one years and would work tomorrow morning as if nothing had changed.

Everything had changed. The canal was draining. The name was on the walls. The Sovereign Trust's column had walked home without its armor and its dignity and the fine-revenue that its bureaucracy required for the deployment to be classified as a success. One hundred dockworkers had defended their district without killing anyone, using the district's own infrastructure as a weapon, and the lesson was not that they had won. The lesson was that the ground belonged to the people who understood it.

Kanja sat on the walkway above the draining canal and listened to the water and the distant night-shift crew and the sound of a district that had learned something about itself that it had not known three weeks ago.

At his hip, in boiled leather, the dark blade rested. It had heard the old smith's question and the boy's answer. It recorded both. It did not offer a verdict. The verdict would come later. The sword was patient. It had been patient for longer than the boy knew, and it would be patient for longer than the boy could imagine.

The Ledger was open. The second entry waited.

## CODA: THE LEDGER OF ONYX

Dredge-Line. Canal district. Black water. Industrial waste. The smell of forge ash and rendered fish and the chemical residue of twelve thousand families' labor flushed into a channel the Sovereign Trust considered municipal infrastructure. The boy knew better. The boy knew it was a weapon.

Three weeks of preparation. The lock mechanisms modified. Quick-release pins machined by a woman who has maintained these gates for twenty-two years and can diagnose a fault by the sound the crank handle makes. Seventeen plank-bridges positioned by a crane operator who calculates load ratings the way I calculate mass: precisely, or not at all.

The column entered. Two hundred soldiers. Five hundred to seven hundred times density. Twelve boats. One Compliance Officer. Grey coat. Clean shoes. Had never touched canal water. Had never visited the districts he fined. The boy let them walk. Four hundred meters. Ten warehouses. Ten fines levied for infractions invented before the column marched.

Gates opened. Three. Simultaneously. The water came from three directions. The towpath vanished. The soldiers sank. The heavy fell. The light rose. The boy's people walked the planks above, baseline density, light feet on light wood, and pulled the soldiers from the muck with cargo hooks and crane cables and the professional efficiency of a workforce that moves heavy loads every day of its life.

No casualties. Two hundred soldiers disarmed, stripped, sent home in canal sludge. Twelve boats grounded and silted. One Compliance Officer deposited on a rooftop with his fine-book and a name written on his forehead in dock-marker chalk.

The Trench Monarch. The name was not the boy's. The name was given by a twenty-two-year-old father who carried chalk in his vest and a four-month-old daughter in his reasons. The boy did not choose the name. The district chose the name. The name chose the boy.

First lesson of the Trench Monarch: do not fight the weight. Move the ground. The weight does the rest.

The old smith asked the question. Job or trade. The boy answered. Trade. The word weighs what it weighs. The Ledger records. The canal drains. The name is on the walls.
