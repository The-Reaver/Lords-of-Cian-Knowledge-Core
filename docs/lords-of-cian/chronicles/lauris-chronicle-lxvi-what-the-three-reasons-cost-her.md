# Lauris Chronicle LXVI: What the Three Reasons Cost Her

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1683`). Sixty-sixth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, the ninth entry of Strand K (Kares Prime / deep past) and the opening entry of
this wave's departure sequence -- the direct payoff to the hook Chronicle XXI (`MCD-1638`)
deliberately left open. Set roughly 180 years after Chronicle XXI, at age ~3,580, the far edge of
`MCD-174`'s decision window. Dramatizes the private articulation maturing into an actual decision:
the ~180-year research period `MCD-174` already locks, closing on the specific reasoning behind
choosing Cian (the Kareth War-Order, the Sealbound Directorate's inherited Ionic-Rite methodology,
and Living Drakma deposits approaching Kares Prime's own). No new named characters. No
contradictions.*

-----

*Archive fragment, Vask-of-Vasks, age ~3,580: "I have spent one hundred and seventy-nine years
answering the question I recorded at Aldreth. I did not expect the answer, once it finally arrived,
to feel less like a discovery and more like a debt coming due."*

I have transcribed a great many entries from Lauris's long middle centuries, and I will say plainly
that most of them do not reward close reading so much as patient accumulation -- a woman doing the
same excellent work, decade after decade, with almost nothing in any single entry to distinguish it
from the one before. This one is different, and I want to explain carefully why, because the
difference is easy to miss if one reads for incident rather than for the quiet turning of a mind.

She had gone, in the years following the question she recorded at Vask Aldreth, to the one place on
Kares Prime built to hold answers rather than to defend or heal or train: Vask-of-Vasks, the
original founding site, kept — as the Iron-Speakers' own bare-minimum accounting has it — as a
cultural center rather than an operational one, its halls given over almost entirely to archive.
I do not think this was an accident of convenience. I think she went there because it was the one
Vask whose entire purpose was memory rather than use, and she had, by her own account, begun to
suspect that the question she was carrying could not be answered by anything Kares Prime still knew
how to give her.

*

What she did there, across nearly two centuries, was not dramatic. She read. She had access, by
virtue of what she was, to archives most Karesians never petitioned to see in a lifetime — records
of the original diaspora's departure, one hundred and twenty-two thousand years gone by her own
count; trade-point logs stretching back further than any single Vask's living memory; the thin,
uneven record of what the outside world had become in the ages since Kares Prime had turned inward.
She read the way I have come to understand she does everything: without hurry, without a fixed
conclusion she was hunting to confirm, cataloguing what she found and setting it beside what she had
found the day before, waiting for a pattern to declare itself rather than forcing one into being.

I have counted, in the archive she kept of this period, no fewer than forty-one distinct
destinations she examined and set aside — worlds with populations that might have used her,
civilizations with archives that might have interested her, a handful of places whose only
qualification was that they were simply far enough away to constitute a genuine departure. She
discarded each one for reasons she records with the same flat precision she brings to everything:
insufficient reason, insufficient reason, insufficient reason, written so many times across so many
pages that I began, transcribing it, to feel the weight of a woman looking for something she could
not yet name and refusing to settle for anything less than it.

*

She found Cian in the hundred and sixty-first year of the search, in a trade-point log so old its
provenance had to be reconstructed from three separate fragmentary sources before she trusted it. I
will not pretend the discovery reads, in her own hand, as a moment of revelation. It reads, instead,
as the closing of three separate columns in a ledger she had been keeping open for a very long time.

The first column: a world holding the only Karesian-descended population she could find record of
anywhere in the archives — a diaspora line calling itself, in the fragment she found, the Kareth
War-Order, departed from Kares Prime one hundred and twenty-two thousand years before her own
search, at a different point in the same long decline her own civilization had since fully
completed. The second: the only documented institution she could locate anywhere running a
methodology descended, however distantly and however corrupted by ignorance of its own origin, from
the inherited Ionic-Rite tradition — an organization the fragment named the Sealbound Directorate.
The third, and I think in some ways the plainest to her, though she does not rank the three in her
own entry: Living Drakma deposits recorded at a density approaching Kares Prime's own, on a world
that had never been fully surveyed for what it held.

I have sat with this entry longer than most, because I believe it tells me something true about how
she has always made her largest decisions — not from longing, not from restlessness, but from the
same careful accumulation of witness that Selene named for her at age 1,840. She did not choose Cian
because it called to her. She chose it because, having read everything Kares Prime's archives could
offer her, it was the only answer that closed all three columns at once, and she had learned, long
before this search began, that an answer closing every column she had opened was the only kind of
answer she had ever fully trusted.

*

Her closing line for the entry, the one I have chosen to end this account with: *I have found the
place. I have not yet decided to go. I record the distinction deliberately, because I think the
second decision will cost more than the first, and I am not yet ready to know exactly what it will
cost until I have sat with it a while longer. I expect that will take time. Everything I have done
that mattered has taken time.*

She was right, though not, I think, in the way she expected to be. The deciding itself would prove
the smaller part. What it would cost her — the deliberation still ahead of her civilization's own
Iron-Speakers, and further ahead than either of them yet knew, the woman who had raised her — was
not yet visible to her on the night she wrote this entry, sitting alone in the one Vask built for
nothing but remembering.
