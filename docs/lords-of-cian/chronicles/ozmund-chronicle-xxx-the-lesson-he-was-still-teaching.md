# The Lesson He Was Still Teaching

I want to be honest about what this one is, because it isn't quite the same shape as the rest. It
isn't a single evening, or a single argument, or a single thing his father said that the General
carried around word for word for the rest of his life, the way he carried so much of what I've already
set down. It's closer to something he told me across several nights, in pieces, whenever some fresh
memory of Aethelgard surfaced in him unprompted — which happened more often than I think he expected it
to, for a man who'd been gone as long as his father had been gone by the time I knew him.

The anchor for it, as much as it has one, is a book. Not a House record — those were kept elsewhere, by
stewards, in the dry formal hand a country's accounts are always kept in. This was Aethelgard's own,
kept for himself in that same study with the strongbox in it, a private account of decisions he'd made
and, more often than the boy expected the first time he was shown it, decisions he'd later decided he'd
gotten wrong. The General said his father showed it to him only once, opened to a page in the middle
rather than the beginning, and told him plainly that it was never going to be finished — that he added
to it each time he learned he'd been wrong about something he'd once considered settled, which meant it
could only ever grow, never close, for as long as he kept governing anything at all.

The boy asked him, reasonably, what the point of an account was that never arrived anywhere. His father
told him it wasn't meant to arrive anywhere. It was meant to be a record of a mind still working rather
than a verdict handed down once and left to stand — that the day he stopped adding to it would be the
day he'd started believing he'd finished learning how to lead, which he considered the single most
dangerous thing a man in his position could ever let himself believe. "A finished account is a man
telling you he's stopped being surprised by himself," he said. "I don't trust anyone who's stopped
being surprised by himself. Least of all me."

The General told me he didn't understand, at the time, why that mattered as much to him as it plainly
did — why his father seemed almost relieved, rather than burdened, by the idea that the work was never
going to be done. He said it took him a long time, and a great deal of his own unfinished work, before
he understood it the way I think he wanted me to understand it too: that a name, a House, whatever you
want to call the thing a father hands down to a son along with all the rest of it, isn't a sum you
inherit once and then simply hold. It's a debt you keep paying into, generation after generation, and
the moment anyone decides they've paid it off in full is the moment it stops being worth anything at
all.

He still carries the coin. I've seen him do it — not the ritual his father built around it, not the
spinning and the waiting for temper to fall, which I understand he uses rarely now and only when he
genuinely needs it. Just the coin itself, turned over once or twice between two fingers in the kind of
idle moment most people fill with nothing in particular, the worn half-scrip his father carried before
him and his father's father carried before that, passed down through hands that used the same object to
teach three entirely different lessons. I don't think he even notices himself doing it most of the
time. I've never asked him about it. Some things, I've learned from him if from nothing else, are truer
left as a habit than turned into a story.

What I can tell you, having heard as much of this as I ever will, is that I don't believe the General
considers his father's lessons finished, even now, even this long after the man who taught them is
gone. I think he's still working some of them out in the middle of things I've watched him do myself —
in a debt paid without his name on it, in a formula rewritten rather than merely excepted, in an
argument he refuses to let anyone he commands sit through the way he once had to sit through his own. A
name, I've come to believe he thinks, isn't a thing a father finishes handing down before he dies. It's
a thing a son keeps learning how to be worth, for exactly as long as he's willing to keep paying into
it — which is, as far as I can tell, for as long as there's anything left of him to pay with at all.
