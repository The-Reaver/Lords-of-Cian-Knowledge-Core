# Daba Chronicle XXXVI: What He Would Not Trade

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1606`). Entry 36 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. The block's opening
entry in the sub-series' established genuine-failure discipline: dramatizes the real, unresolved cost
of `MCD-1567`'s "no single loss collapses a function the group depends on" doctrine when the loss in
question is a person rather than a function -- a courier is captured, and Daba refuses a rescue that
would expose three other cells to save her, a decision the Chronicle does not soften into a hidden
victory. Introduces Tamsin, Halvin, and Bren, collision-checked clean. Abad's approval: "rate 50

Tamsin missed her relay by eleven hours before anyone let themselves say the word.

She ran the northern spur -- three settlements strung along a drainage canal the Trust had stopped
patrolling closely after the third year of quiet, which was exactly why Daba trusted it to her. She
carried nothing written, the way every courier in 1804 carried nothing written, but she carried
something worse in a network built the way his was: she knew the names of two cell leads by face, and
the general shape of a third cell's rotation, because eleven hours is a short enough gap that a lead
runner sometimes has to improvise contact rather than wait for the drop.

Bren found this out from a boy who sold river eels near the canal locks, who had watched two Trust
enforcement wagons take a woman matching Tamsin's description out of a laundry yard two days prior,
struggling, not cooperating, which the boy reported like it mattered, and to Bren it did. Bren had
trained her. Bren was the one who came to Daba with it, and Bren was the one who had already, before
he arrived, worked out three different ways they might get her back.

Daba listened to all three. He always listened to all of it, every plan, all the way through, because
a plan cut off partway sounds worse than it is and a leader who stops listening early teaches his
people to stop bringing him hard things.

"None of them," he said, when Bren finished.

"She knows Halvin's face. She knows Corren's rotation. If they break her --"

"If they break her, they get two names and a schedule," Daba said. "If we go in after her, they get a
column of Trust wagons following whoever survives the attempt straight back to every cell that
attempt touches. That's not a worse outcome than losing her. It's a worse outcome than losing four of
her."

He watched that land on Bren the way it always landed, on everyone, the first time -- not as an
argument he'd lost, because there wasn't an argument to lose, the numbers were the numbers. It landed
as something closer to a physical blow, delivered by a man who did not flinch delivering it, which
Bren would remember for longer than he'd remember the numbers themselves.

Halvin, when he heard, did not argue at all. He asked one question, quietly, in the cellar they were
using that month: "Does she know that's the answer? Did we ever tell any of them that's the answer,
before they went out?"

Daba had told every courier who ever ran for him exactly that, in exactly those words, before their
first assignment, because he had decided years before that it was a cruelty to let someone risk
themselves for a doctrine they hadn't consented to. Tamsin had heard it. Tamsin had said yes anyway,
the way they all said yes, because the alternative -- a network large enough to spend lives buying
back its own -- was the alternative that had put 1,804 names into the ground in a single night eight
years before any of them were fighting anything at all.

Knowing she had agreed to it did not make it weigh less. He had learned, by now, that it never did.

She held for six days. What she gave up in that time -- and she gave something up, eventually, they
all did, and Daba had stopped pretending otherwise to the newer ones -- turned out to be a face and a
rotation and nothing more, because a face and a rotation were the only things she had to give, because
that was the entire design: no courier in 1804 carried more than they could survive losing under
questioning, precisely so that this exact failure, when it came, would cost the network a fraction of
what it would have cost a different kind of network. Halvin moved that week. Corren's rotation
changed. Nothing else broke.

Tamsin did not come back. The Trust's own record, which reached them nine weeks later through an
informant in the regional garrison, listed her death among four others, undated, unremarked, filed
under a case number instead of a name.

Daba added her name to a private list he kept and told almost no one about, in a hand small enough to
fit a great many names on a single page, and he did not tell Bren or Halvin that the list existed,
because he had also decided, years before, that some weight was his alone to carry, and that carrying
it visibly would only teach the people under him to be afraid of the exact discipline that was keeping
the rest of them alive.

He was right that the discipline worked. He never once let himself call the arithmetic a comfort.
