# What Twenty Years Sounds Like

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1787`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series, Chronicle LVIII, Draconis strand wave 3.*

---

The General told me this story so quietly, the one time he told it, that I had to lean in to be certain I hadn't missed a part of it. There was no incident in it worth the name. He told me that was exactly why he'd kept it.

It was some while after the border road, once Draconis had mended as far as that wound was ever going to let him mend, and the two of them had fallen back into the ordinary rhythm of a household that had, by then, seen them through so many years together that neither man any longer marked the passage of them consciously. It was a cold morning, the General told me, the kind that settled into old injuries before it settled into anything else, and he had come upon Draconis alone in the armory, working the stiffness out of his sword hand the way the General had seen him do a hundred times before without ever once thinking to look closely at what he was actually watching.

That morning he looked closely. He told me it wasn't any single thing that caught him — not the visible scar from the border road, which had long since stopped drawing his eye the way a fresh wound draws it — but the accumulation of small, ordinary signs he realized, all at once, he had been quietly cataloguing for years without admitting to himself he was doing it. The grey had come into Draconis's beard gradually enough that the General couldn't have named the season it arrived. His hand, flexing through the cold-stiffness that morning, took longer to loosen than it once had. He rose from a bench, crossing the armory to fetch a whetstone, with the particular deliberate care of a man who has learned, through some earlier mistake, exactly which motions his own knees will forgive him and which they won't.

The General told me he said nothing about any of it, and that Draconis, catching him watching, only smiled the small dry smile he kept for moments he understood without being told, and remarked, unprompted, that a man who'd spent as many winters standing outer watches as he had ought to expect the cold to start collecting on its debts eventually, and that he intended to keep working the hand loose each morning for exactly as long as it kept answering him when he asked it to. He said it lightly, the General told me — not as complaint, not as any bid for sympathy, simply as a soldier's plain accounting of a body that had given him thirty years of honest service and was, by now, entitled to a little slowness in return.

What struck the General, hearing it, was how little alarm the observation carried for Draconis himself, set against how much it carried for him. He told me he understood, standing in that cold armory, that he had spent so many years measuring the man against danger — knives, seals, beams, bolts — that he had somehow never once measured him against the ordinary, unstoppable fact of simply growing old, and that this, unlike every other threat the General had ever quietly stood ready to answer on his behalf, was the one he had no motion, single or otherwise, that could do anything at all against.

Draconis, for his part, seemed to find the General's silence more troubling than his own stiff hand, and asked him plainly what was wrong. The General told me he'd answered only that he'd been thinking Halyn's boy would be old enough for a proper blade soon, and that he hoped Draconis meant to be the one who taught him to hold it. Draconis had laughed at that, low and genuine, and said he intended to be exactly that, God and his own knees willing, and the General told me he had let the moment close there, unwilling to say the truer thing sitting just behind it — that he had, for the first time in all the years he'd known him, caught himself afraid of an ending that had nothing to do with any enemy either of them had ever faced together.
