# Daba Chronicle VII: The Weight of Being Small

By the time there were five of them, the argument had been building for weeks under everything else
they said to each other, and it finally broke open over a piece of news that had nothing directly to
do with any of them.

Word reached the ruined blocks that a resistance cell two districts over -- the Ashgate irregulars,
people called them, though Daba doubted they'd chosen the name themselves -- had grown to nearly forty
fighters in half a year, drilling openly in an abandoned foundry yard, confident enough in their
numbers to stop collecting recruits quietly and start collecting them by reputation. Three days after
the story reached Daba's people, the Trust burned the foundry yard to its foundations. Reports put the
dead at over twenty. No survivors were confirmed among the leadership.

Corvin heard the news and heard it wrong, or heard it right and drew the wrong lesson from it, which
Daba was coming to understand were not always different things.

"Forty wasn't enough," Corvin said, that evening, in the cellar they were using as a meeting place. He
was not a survivor of 1804 -- he had come to them three weeks earlier from a dockside gang that had
lost its own war with the Trust the previous year, carrying an anger that had not yet found a shape it
trusted. "If they'd had eighty, two hundred, they'd have had the numbers to hold the yard when the
Trust came for it. You keep us at five. Five people can't hold anything."

"Five people weren't supposed to hold anything," Daba said. "Five people are supposed to still be
five people tomorrow."

"That's not a strategy, that's hiding."

"It's the same strategy the Ashgate irregulars didn't have," Daba said, "and it's the reason they're
not five people anymore. They're none."

Corvin's jaw worked. Sael, sitting near the wall with her knees drawn up in the posture Daba had
learned was hers when she was listening rather than waiting to speak, said nothing yet, but he could
see her weighing it.

"You want us small forever," Corvin said. "Small doesn't win anything. Small just survives to watch
the next fire."

That landed the way Corvin meant it to land, and Daba let it sit in the room a moment before he
answered, because he understood the shape of the anger underneath it and did not want to argue past
it before acknowledging it. "I'm not interested in winning a battle the way the Ashgate irregulars were
interested in winning a battle," he said finally. "I watched what a fire that size does to something
that size. I was there. I know exactly what forty looks like when it's found and what twenty looks
like when it's counted afterward. I am not building something to be found."

"Then what are you building it for?"

"So that when it does get found -- and it will, eventually, some piece of it will -- what's lost is one
piece. Not the whole hand." Daba held up his own hand, fingers spread, and closed them one at a time.
"If they take this finger, I still have four. If they take the whole hand at once because I built it
as one fist instead of five fingers, I have nothing left to build with again."

It was Sael who finally spoke, quiet, working the argument through out loud the way she worked most
things through. "Forty people drilling in an open yard isn't forty separate threats. It's one big one,
wearing forty faces. The Trust didn't have to find forty things. They had to find one place."

Corvin looked at her, then back at Daba, and some of the heat went out of his shoulders, not because
he'd been beaten in the argument but because he'd been handed a version of it he could actually hold.
"So we stay small on purpose," he said slowly, testing the shape of the idea in his own mouth. "Not
because we're afraid to grow. Because growing the way they grew is the same thing as painting a target
and standing under it."

"That's the whole of it," Daba said. "We can be a hundred people someday. I want us to be a hundred
people someday. But a hundred people who've never stood in the same yard at the same time, who can
each lose the four around them and still have ninety-six left who never even knew their names to give
up under questioning. Not forty in a foundry, waiting to be found once."

Corvin didn't answer right away. He looked instead at the low cellar ceiling, at the five of them
spread around its edges rather than gathered in its center -- a habit Daba had insisted on from the
first week, and that Corvin, until tonight, had only ever followed because Daba asked him to.

"Small," Corvin said at last, like a man trying on a coat he hadn't expected to fit. "On purpose."

Nobody answered him. Nobody needed to. The five of them sat in their five separate corners of the
cellar a while longer, and for the first time since Corvin had joined them, the silence between them
felt like agreement rather than truce.
