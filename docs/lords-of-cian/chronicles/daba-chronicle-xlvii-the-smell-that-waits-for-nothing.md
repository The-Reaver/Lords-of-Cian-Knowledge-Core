# Daba Chronicle XLVII: The Smell That Waits for Nothing

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1617`). Entry 47 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes the
Rookery's memory (`MCD-1566`) surfacing unprompted on an ordinary day, decades after Daba Chronicle

years' flattening grief against a grief now lived with. Introduces Perrin, a senior 1804 cell

It was rain that did it that particular year, which surprised him, because for the better part of two
decades it had been smoke -- any smoke, cookfire or forge or a pipe lit too close to him -- and he had
made his peace with that specific ambush long enough ago that he no longer flinched at it, only noted
it, the way you note a familiar face across a crowded room. Rain was new. Rain hitting hot stone
released something oiled and mineral out of the ground for the length of a single breath before the
cooling took over, and standing in the doorway of a grain-store watching the first drops hit the yard,
he found himself back in a stairwell he had not physically stood in for more years than he let himself
count.

He did not move. That, he had learned, was the trick of it, if trick was even the word -- not to fight
the memory or hurry it along, but to let it finish arriving on its own schedule, the way you let a
wave finish breaking rather than trying to outrun it up the beach. It arrived the way it always did
now: not as panic, not as the flat animal need to count heads that had governed the first five years of
his life after the fire, but as a specific, almost tender catalogue of small things. The particular
pitch of Fennic's voice calling the lead line down. The exact phrase -- *ash-grey wins, everybody to
ash-grey* -- that had gotten nineteen children moving without a single one of them understanding they
were being saved. The weight of a six-year-old's arms locked around his neck in cistern water. He let
all of it pass through him standing in a doorway in the rain, dry-eyed, entirely present, and when it
had finished passing he was still a man standing in a doorway in the rain, breathing normally, waiting
for a supply count that did not care what year it currently believed itself to be.

"You went somewhere," Perrin said, from just inside the store, not asking it as a question, because he
had learned years ago that Daba answered questions about it worse than he answered simple statements
of fact.

"I did."

"Bad?"

Daba considered the honest answer, which was more complicated than the word allowed for. "No," he
said. "Not the way it used to be bad. It used to arrive like the fire itself -- all at once, nothing
left standing afterward. Now it arrives like weather. You feel it coming, you let it land, and then it's
finished landing, and the day goes on being the day." He came in out of the rain properly, shook the
water from his sleeve. "I don't think it ever leaves entirely. I've stopped expecting it to. I think
what changes is just how much of the day it's allowed to take with it when it goes."

Perrin, who had come to 1804 as a half-starved sixteen-year-old carrying a grief of his own that had
nothing to do with the Rookery and everything to do with a Trust reprisal three provinces distant --
a debt Daba had never once made him repay by comparison, never once weighed against the size of
eighteen hundred and four -- nodded slowly, the way he nodded at any piece of doctrine he intended to
keep. "Is that a lesson," he asked, "or just you talking."

"Both, usually. That's most of what I have to teach anyone, past a certain point." Daba counted the
grain sacks by eye, a habit so old he barely registered doing it, the number coming out exactly where
the tally sheet said it should, and something in him settled at the simple correctness of that,
unrelated to anything the rain had brought up and yet somehow answering it all the same. "You'll carry
something like this yourself, someday, if you haven't already started. Everyone who stays in this work
long enough does. The only thing I can promise you is that it gets quieter. Never gone. Quieter."

"That's not much of a promise."

"No," Daba agreed. "It's the honest one, though, and I've found those wear better over time than the
comfortable kind." He picked up the tally sheet, signed his initial to the corner of it, and the
ordinary business of the afternoon resumed around him -- sacks counted, a door latched against the
weather, Perrin already moving on to the next thing on a list neither of them had needed to say aloud
-- while somewhere behind his ribs, folded now into something closer to a keepsake than a wound, the
smell of hot stone in first rain went on meaning exactly what it had always meant, and would, he
suspected, go on meaning it for as long as he had breath to notice it with.
