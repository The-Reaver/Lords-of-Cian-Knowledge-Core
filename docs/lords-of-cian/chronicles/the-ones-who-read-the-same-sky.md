# The Ones Who Read the Same Sky

The enemy squadron broke off before either fleet came within striking range, which had never happened
before in the successor's memory of facing hostile ships under her own forecast. She'd called a
dangerous cross-current forming in the strait both fleets were converging on, invisible to the eye,
lethal to any vessel caught in it unprepared. She had expected the enemy to sail into it blind.

They didn't. They read it themselves, independently, and turned away at almost exactly the same
moment her own fleet did.

It took a captured officer, taken later in an unrelated engagement, to explain why. The enemy fleet
came from a coastal people with their own weather tradition, generations old, built not from any
method Sephtis had ever taught or that his successor had ever shared, but from a lineage of
fisherfolk readers going back further than either side's written records — an entirely separate
craft, arrived at by different eyes watching the same water for different reasons, that happened to
converge on the same true answer.

"They're not thieves," the successor said, once she understood it. "They didn't take anything from
us. They built their own version of the exact thing we built, out of the same sky, without ever
meeting us."

She asked to meet the enemy's own reader under a truce flag, an unusual request Kanja granted without
hesitation. The two women — rivals in every other respect, allied to fleets that had spent years
trying to sink each other — spent an afternoon comparing methods neither had ever shown an outsider,
and found, more often than either expected, that they were describing the same phenomena through
entirely different vocabularies.

"We could have killed each other today," the enemy reader said, at the end of it, "over water neither
of us was wrong about."

"Then let's not," the successor said. "Not over this, anyway. Whatever else your fleet and mine still
have to settle, this doesn't need to be part of it."

Neither side abandoned the wider conflict. But from that day on, both fleets gave the other's ships a
wide, deliberate berth whenever either read genuinely dangerous water, an unspoken truce over weather
alone that neither command ever formally acknowledged and neither ever broke.
