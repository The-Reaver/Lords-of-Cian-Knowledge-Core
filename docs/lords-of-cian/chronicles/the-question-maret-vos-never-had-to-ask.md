# The Question Maret Vos Never Had to Ask

Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters (reuses already-locked crew member Maret Vos).*

Maret Vos had been running the crane rig above the western shaft for eleven straight days by the time
she finally asked Kanja the question she'd apparently been sitting on since the siege began.

"Every other crew chief I've worked under tells you what to build and expects you to build it. You
keep asking us what the ground will actually hold before you tell anyone what to build at all." She
said it without complaint, more genuine curiosity than critique, watching him read a support column's
load-bearing capacity by hand before committing the crane to a lift he could have simply ordered.
"Slower way to run a siege. Why do it that way?"

"Because the ground doesn't care what I ordered. It only cares what's actually true about it, and if I
skip asking that question to save time, the ground answers it for me later, usually at the worst
possible moment to find out I guessed wrong." Kanja finished his read and nodded the lift forward, the
column holding exactly as he'd found it would. "I've seen what happens when someone builds from what
they expect to be true instead of what they checked. I'd rather be the slower crew chief than the one
explaining a collapse afterward."

Maret Vos considered that for the length of the lift, then offered something of her own — not a
challenge to his method, but an addition to it, born of her own decade running rigging on projects that
had nothing to do with war. "I've run cranes for engineers who thought asking the ground a question
meant it owed them an honest answer. It doesn't. Sometimes a column reads sound and fails anyway.
Sometimes it reads compromised and holds another twenty years out of spite. You can ask the right
question and still get an answer that lies to you."

"That's fair. I've had that happen too." Kanja hadn't considered it from quite that angle before —
not that his method was wrong, but that it wasn't as complete an answer as he sometimes treated it as
being. "So what do you do with that? Stop asking?"

"No. You ask anyway, because asking wrong is worse than asking and still getting fooled sometimes. But
you build in slack for the times the ground lies to you — a second support the calculations say you
don't need, margin the honest read didn't call for." She nodded toward the column he'd just cleared for
the lift, itself carrying more redundant bracing than his own load figures strictly required. "You're
already doing it. Just didn't have the words for why yet."

It was, Kanja realized, watching the lift complete without incident, the first time in eleven days
anyone on this crew had improved his own method rather than simply trusted it — not a rival engineer's
challenge, not a crew chief's order, but a fellow tradesperson's decade of hard-won caution folded
quietly into a system he'd have kept treating as complete otherwise.

"I've been asking the ground the right questions this whole siege," he told her, once the shaft's work
was done for the day. "I don't think I'd thought as carefully as I should have about what happens when
it answers me honestly and still gets it wrong."

"Most men who build things as well as you do never get told that. They just get complimented until
something fails and it's too late to hear it." Maret Vos began securing the crane rig for the night,
already thinking about the next lift. "You asked me a real question back, though, instead of just
thanking me for the thought. That's rarer than the redundant bracing."
