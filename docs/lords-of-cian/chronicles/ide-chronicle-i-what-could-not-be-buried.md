# Ide Chronicle I: What Could Not Be Buried

*Locked canon, Batch 79, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-342`). First entry in Ide's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Ase (`PH2-036`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`), Umoja Chronicle I (`MCD-335`), Yara Chronicle I (`MCD-336`),
Areíto Chronicle I (`MCD-339`), Guanín Chronicle I (`MCD-340`), and Borikén Chronicle I (`MCD-341`).
Narration is close-third on Ase throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Slots into no existing mainline
battle — original homage-era material set in Ide itself. Opens Chicago's own run of territory
Chronicles.*

---

Ase was three streets away when it happened, close enough to hear the crowd's silence change shape
but not close enough to reach the square in time. By the time she arrived, it was already finished,
and the only thing left for her to do was the thing she had spent her whole life learning how to
do: everything that came after.

The Magistrate had called it justice. A theft, he said, from the granary that fed half of Ide
through the lean months — and a man named for it in front of the whole square, tried and sentenced
inside the same hour, before anyone with a clear head could ask the one question that mattered,
which was whether he had done it at all. He hadn't. Ase would establish that within a day, from
nine separate witnesses who had seen the actual thief slip out the granary's east door while the
man now dead had been forty yards away, arguing with a fishmonger over the price of a morning's
catch. But by the time she'd gathered the ninth account, the Magistrate had already decided the
matter closed, and closed matters, in his experience, did not reopen themselves.

He was wrong about that. He had simply never met anyone like her.

She worked the way she always worked: unhurried, exact, refusing every kindness that would have let
her look away sooner. She wrote down the dead man's name in full, and the names of everyone who
watched him die, and the exact words the Magistrate had used to condemn him, and the time of day,
and the weather, and which of the granary guards had actually seen the real thief and said nothing
because saying something had never once, in living memory, changed what happened next. She wrote
until her hand ached and then she wrote more, because a fact half-recorded was a fact the Magistrate
could still argue with, and she intended to leave him nothing to argue with at all.

The stranger found her on the second night, in the back room of a print-shop that had agreed to run
her account only after she'd agreed, in turn, to let them do it anonymously, their own names kept
off the page. He had been in the square the first night. She remembered him only as a shape at the
crowd's edge, still while everyone else surged forward or fled back, the way a man stands who has
already decided this isn't a moment for him to change.

"You didn't try to stop it," she said, not as an accusation. A fact, recorded the way she recorded
everything.

"No." He didn't reach for an excuse. "I got there when you did. Maybe a minute before. A minute
isn't enough to stop a thing that's already been decided by everyone standing in that square except
the one man it was happening to." He watched her hands keep moving across the page. "You're not
writing this down to change what already happened."

"I can't change what already happened. Nobody can, least of all me." She didn't look up. "I'm
writing it down so that what happened stays exactly what it was, forever, no matter how many people
find it convenient to remember it differently starting next week."

The Magistrate's men came for the print-shop on the third night. Not for her — the print-shop's
owner had kept his word and kept her name off every copy, so the men came instead for the plates,
the ink, the stacked broadsheets waiting to go out at dawn, and burned all of it in the street as a
lesson to anyone else who might be thinking of printing something inconvenient. It was, by any
ordinary measure, a complete success. Nothing on those particular pages would ever reach anyone.

What the Magistrate hadn't accounted for was that the account had already left the shop three
different ways before his men arrived — one copy carried out under a fish-cart's false bottom by a
boy who owed the dead man nothing but a kindness once done him, one copy memorized whole by a
scribe's apprentice who could recite it back word for word a decade later if anyone ever asked, and
one copy, the stranger's own doing though he never said so afterward, folded small and sewn into the
lining of a traveler's coat bound for a town two days south that had never heard the Magistrate's
name and had no particular reason to protect it. Ase never asked him to carry anything. He simply
had, the same unhurried way he'd stood at the square's edge doing nothing anyone could point to
afterward and call help.

By the week's end the account was in five towns that weren't Ide, in the hands of people who had no
stake in the Magistrate's version of events and every reason to pass along a good, ugly, well-sourced
story. He sent men after the boy with the fish-cart. He never found him. He threatened the print-shop
owner with worse than burned plates. It changed nothing that had already left the shop days earlier.
By the time he understood the actual shape of what he was fighting — not one document he could seize,
but a fact that had already finished multiplying past any single place he could reach — the dead man's
real name, and the truth of what had actually happened to him, was simply and permanently part of the
record. No power on the Magistrate's part could unmake that now. No power on Ase's part could have
saved the man it was about. Both of those were true, and she had learned, long before this week, to
carry both without letting either one cancel the other out.

"You could have told me you'd need it copied that many times before it was safe," she said to the
stranger, once it was over — not thanks, exactly, closer to an accounting settled.

"Didn't know it myself, until I knew where the coat was headed." He didn't stay to see whether the
Magistrate's grip on Ide ever loosened because of what she'd written; that wasn't the part he'd come
for. "You did the part that mattered. I just made sure it had enough doors to go through."

He was gone by morning, the way he always seemed to be. He left nothing carved, no phrase repeated
hand to hand — only, weeks later, a fourth copy of Ase's account, in a hand she didn't recognize,
turning up nailed to the granary's own door where the Magistrate himself would have to walk past it
every morning on his way inside.

ability (`PH2-036`, "Named and Numbered") directly on the page for the first time, honoring both its

deliberately left unnamed, matching the Umoja/Areíto/Guanín/Borikén precedent for undetailed
antagonists. No new named characters are introduced; no new proper nouns requiring a collision
