# Lauris Chronicle LXXVIII: Ninety Seconds Under the Bow

*Locked canon, Batch 300, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1695`). Seventy-eighth entry in Lauris Letitia's own
Chronicle series, a Strand D entry giving full-scene treatment to Operation 11, the juvenile
Tide-Wraith engagement (previously only summarized in a paired rule with Operation 21 at
`MCD-1545`). Operation 11 opens the Established Hunter period (`MCD-179`: "Operations 11 through
22"), and this entry is written as her first contract of that period rather than her last of the
Apprentice Contracts, immediately following Chronicle LXXVII's Operation 10. Dramatizes the
jaw-severing and spine-pursuit termination technique already locked at `MCD-1545` in full combat
detail for the first time, and shows the earliest stirrings of the informal nicknaming `MCD-1539`
describes beginning across this period. One new minor named element, the Directorate vessel *the
Halfmoon Tide*, collision-checked clean. No contradictions with any already-locked rule.*

-----

*Archive fragment, Established Hunter designation LL-001-S-011: "Four rings of teeth closed on
water where I had been standing a half-second before. I did not count the half-second as luck. I
had already decided where I would be standing after it closed."*

Operation 11 reached Lauris within the same month her Apprentice Contracts formally closed, and I
think it worth recording plainly that the Directorate, whatever grief or ceremony her tenth contract
might have deserved, extended her none of it. She was Senior External Asset now, and the contract
waiting for her was, by any measure, considerably larger than anything the Apprentice period had
asked of her.

The Voskharen Trench's juvenile Tide-Wraith had killed eleven fishing crews across a single season,
surfacing without warning from feeding zones no vessel's sonar had reliably charted, and the
Directorate's answer, developed over three prior failed engagements, was bait: a hardened vessel,
*the Halfmoon Tide*, built heavier and slower than any working fishing boat specifically to survive a
strike long enough for its passenger to respond to one.

Lauris stood at the bow for the better part of two days before the creature came.

*

I have asked her, more than once, what those two days were like, standing at the rail of a vessel
built around the expectation that something roughly thirty-eight meters long and weighing some two
hundred and eighty thousand kilograms would eventually try to take it from beneath. She has told me,
consistent with everything else I know of her, that she found the waiting itself unremarkable --
that her attention across those two days was spent almost entirely on the water itself, its
temperature gradients, its current patterns, the particular quality of stillness that precedes a
large body moving beneath a surface rather than through open water.

It breached directly under the bow on the second evening, four rings of jaw closing on the space
where she had been standing a half-second before she was no longer standing there. I record what
followed as she has recorded it to me, because I believe the plainness of her own account does more
justice to what her body did than any embellishment of mine could manage: she met the closing jaw at
its first tooth-ring with the Spine of Dagon, drawn and swung in the same motion that carried her
clear of the strike, severing cleanly through cartilage and bone the creature had never once, across
eleven prior kills, needed to test against anything capable of answering it.

The Tide-Wraith did not surface again. It dove, hard and fast, back toward the depth its own biology
trusted as safety, and Lauris went with it -- not pursuing from behind, which her own instructors at
Karth-Ven had trained her was the posture that got pursuers killed against anything built to flee
downward, but walking, deliberately, along the length of its own descending spine, using the
creature's own diving motion to carry her toward the one point her Directorate briefing had marked as
decisive: the primary motor ganglion, seated just aft of the skull, that governed every subsequent
motion the beast's massive body could make.

She severed it in a single motion. The dive stopped being a dive and became, instead, simple falling
weight, and the whole engagement, from the first closing jaw to the creature's body settling still
against the Trench floor, had run, by the vessel crew's own timed account, ninety seconds.

*

I want to record the crew's reaction at some length, because I believe it is the true substance of
this Chronicle, more than the engagement itself. Eleven fishing crews had died to this creature across
a single season. *The Halfmoon Tide*'s own crew of nine had spent two days bracing for the same fate,
armored and grim in the particular way of men who have accepted a mission's danger without yet
believing they will survive it. Ninety seconds after the water finally broke, every one of them was
still alive, unharmed, and standing on a deck that had not so much as taken a structural strain worth
logging.

One of them -- I have not been able to determine which, and Lauris herself does not recall -- said
it first, low, to the man beside him rather than to her directly: that she had walked down the
thing's own back like it was a road it had built for her. I record this not because the phrase itself
survived -- it did not, not formally, not the way "the Petite Catastrophe" would survive from Operation
22 eleven contracts later -- but because it is, so far as either of us has ever been able to trace,
the first time any Directorate crew found itself reaching for language to describe what they had
just watched her do, rather than simply reporting that the contract had been completed.

*

I asked her, once, whether she noticed that moment -- the first small, informal reach for a name that
was not her designation -- happening around her. She told me she did not, not at the time, and that
she would not fully register the pattern until several operations later, when the names had
multiplied past what any single crew's private remark could account for.

"I was thinking about the water," she said. "I have always thought the crew understood something
about me before I understood it about myself. I have never minded that. I think it is, in its own
way, the truest kind of report -- the one written by people who were simply there, and had no
Directorate form to fill out telling them how."
