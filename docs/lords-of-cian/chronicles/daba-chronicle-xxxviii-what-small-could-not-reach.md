# Daba Chronicle XXXVIII: What Small Could Not Reach

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1608`). Entry 38 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes the
concrete, personal cost of `MCD-1567`'s doctrine-over-mass design when it is applied correctly and
still is not enough -- 1804 identifies a settlement under active Trust reprisal early enough that a
larger force might have saved it, and Daba deploys only what the doctrine allows, which is not
enough. Introduces Pallow's Reach and Serel, collision-checked clean. Abad's approval: "rate 50
Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

---

They knew about Pallow's Reach nine days before the Trust column reached it, which was, by any honest
accounting, more warning than 1804 usually got about anything. A source inside the regional garrison
had overheard enough of the planning to be certain: a reprisal, three hundred strong, moving against a
farming settlement suspected -- likely correctly -- of feeding grain to a resistance band operating in
the hill country above it. Nine days was enough time to plan almost anything. It was not enough time to
change what 1804 was.

Serel brought the report to Daba and then, before Daba had said a word, brought the argument he already
knew was coming. Serel had run reconnaissance for six years and had never once, in that time, asked
Daba to break the doctrine. He asked now.

"We could pull every fighting cell within four days' travel," Serel said. "Eighteen people, maybe
twenty. Enough to matter against three hundred, if we take the column somewhere it doesn't expect to
be taken -- the causeway before it, not the village after."

"Eighteen people in one place, for four days, converging on a single target the Trust will investigate
thoroughly once it's over," Daba said. "That's not a rescue. That's a map of half our fighting strength,
delivered to them for free, the moment any one of the eighteen is identified afterward."

"There's a settlement of four hundred people about to be burned."

"I know the number."

He did know it, and knowing it did not change the other number, which was the one that mattered more
in the arithmetic 1804 had built itself around: eighteen committed fighters, cross-trained,
irreplaceable in any short span, set against a Trust reprisal force that would burn Pallow's Reach and
still have two hundred and eighty men left over to hunt whoever had tried to stop it. There was no
version of the engagement Daba could construct where 1804 won it outright, and every version he could
construct where 1804 tried anyway ended with the network smaller, more visible, and Pallow's Reach
burned besides -- because eighteen people, however well trained, do not stop three hundred, they only
give three hundred a fight worth remembering them by.

What Daba authorized instead was small enough to feel, to Serel and to everyone who carried it out,
like an insult measured against what was coming. Four people. A week's warning delivered on foot to
the settlement's own elders, quietly, through a route that would not be traced back. A cache of tools
better suited to digging than farming, left where a resourceful person might find a use for them.
Nothing that looked like an army, because nothing that looked like an army was available, and anything
that looked like an attempt at one would have cost more than it saved.

Pallow's Reach burned anyway. Some of it. The elders used the warning to move perhaps half the
settlement's people into the hill country before the column arrived -- old routes, mostly, that 1804
had not taught them so much as reminded them of, because people who have lived somewhere for
generations generally know their own ground better than any outside doctrine gives them credit for.
The other half did not move, or moved too late, or chose to stay and fight for a harvest they could not
carry with them, and the Trust column did what Trust columns did to settlements it had already decided
were guilty.

Serel came back from the aftermath and did not, this time, bring an argument. He brought a number, the
way Daba himself always did, because Serel had learned that much from him even in disagreement: roughly
half. Roughly half of four hundred people had lived because of nine days' warning and four people
carrying it. Roughly half had not, because four people and a week were what the doctrine could afford,
and the doctrine had never once claimed to be able to afford more.

"We could have saved all of them," Serel said. It was not quite an accusation. It was worse than that
-- it was simply true.

"We could have tried to save all of them," Daba said, "and lost the eighteen people who are the only
reason any settlement gets a nine-day warning next time." He did not say it as though it settled the
matter, because he had stopped believing, some years before, that it did. He said it the way he said
most hard things by then: as the arithmetic he had chosen to live inside, on a day when the arithmetic
had cost exactly what it always threatened to cost, and had not, this time, felt like enough.
