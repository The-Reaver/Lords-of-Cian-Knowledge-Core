# The Storm They Didn't Make

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Sovereign Ghost of the Great Sea Alias Chronicle XVI,
first entry in the sixth wave. Rebellion era, age 21, some months after Ghost Harbor (`MCD-235`).
Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The delegation came out under a truce flag three villages deep — fisherfolk from a stretch of coast
the Trust barely bothered to patrol, rowing a leaking skiff alongside *The Audit* until someone on
deck finally threw them a line rather than let them drown proving their point.

Their elder did the talking, once he'd stopped shaking from the climb. "Three years now, no storm's
touched our coast bad enough to lose a boat. Not since your fleet started running these waters. We
came to ask you to do it again, for the crossing season. Hold the weather back the way you always
have."

Kanja crouched to meet him at eye level rather than let the man crane his neck up at a legend. "We've
never held a storm back. Not once. Not this fleet, not any fleet — that's not a thing ships do."

"Then how—"

"Dol Maren reads weather the way a smith reads metal. Cloud color, the way the swell runs a day
ahead of the wind, which months this stretch of coast actually gets hit and which ones just look like
it might. We route around the worst of it because we know when the worst of it's coming. That's not
holding a storm back. That's not standing in front of it when it arrives."

The elder's face did something complicated — relief fighting disappointment fighting a stubborn need
to keep believing what had kept his village calm for three years. "The old ones say the ghost fleet
tames the sea."

"The old ones are wrong, and if your village believes it hard enough to stop reading its own sky
before the crossing season, that belief is going to drown someone the year we're not here to watch it
happen." Kanja held his gaze until the man nodded, reluctant. "I'll give you something better than a
myth."

He had Dol Maren brought up from below, and for the better part of an hour she walked the elder and
two of his sons through what she actually watched for — the specific cloud banks that meant a crossing
should wait, the swell pattern that meant it was safe, marks on a hand-copied chart they could keep and
pass down themselves. No mystery in any of it. Just attention, repeated for years until it looked like
magic to anyone who hadn't done the looking.

"This works whether we're anywhere near your coast or not," Kanja told them as they climbed back down
to their skiff, chart wrapped in oilcloth against the spray. "That's the whole point of giving it to
you instead of letting you keep believing it belongs to us."

The elder held the chart the rest of the way to shore like something more fragile than rope and wood
had any right to be.
