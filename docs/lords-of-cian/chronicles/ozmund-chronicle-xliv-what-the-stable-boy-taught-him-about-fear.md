# What the Stable Boy Taught Him About Fear

This is one the General told me himself, which was rare enough on its own that I remember the exact
evening — a quiet one, no crisis pressing on either of us, the kind of night he occasionally allowed
himself to talk about the years before either of us mattered to anyone. He told it the way a man
tells a story he's kept for himself a long time and finally decided was safe to give away. I've never
told it to anyone else until now.

Cobb was a stable boy, a year or so older, the son of a groom who'd worked the Verehimu horses for as
long as anyone remembered and would go on doing so long after either boy had grown past needing to be
watched. The General said they'd known each other the way children of wildly different stations
sometimes manage to, in the gaps adults don't think to police — the stable yard being, of all the
grounds of the House, the one place nobody much cared whether the heir was somewhere he technically
shouldn't be, so long as he came back for his lessons on time.

What he remembered clearest, he told me, wasn't anything Cobb said. It was a horse — a young gelding,
newly broken, that had thrown its previous rider badly enough that the whole stable staff had taken to
handling it with a wariness bordering on outright avoidance. Cobb had been given the job anyway,
because someone had to be, and the General said he'd watched him approach that stall morning after
morning with his hands shaking badly enough to see from ten feet away, and go in regardless, every
single time, because the horse needed handling and there was no one else assigned to do it.

He asked him once, directly, the way only a child asks a question an adult would have known better
than to ask, why he kept going in if he was that afraid. The General said Cobb had looked at him like
the question genuinely puzzled him — not offended, just honestly uncertain how to answer something
that seemed, to him, self-evident. He said being afraid didn't have anything to do with whether the
horse needed feeding. The fear was just a thing that happened to him on the way into the stall. It
wasn't a reason not to go in.

The General told me that was the first time in his life anyone had ever described courage to him as
something separate from not being afraid — that every lesson he'd had up to that point, formal and
otherwise, had treated fear as a thing a man eventually outgrew or overcame, a weakness with an
expiration date. Cobb hadn't outgrown anything. He went into that stall shaking every single morning
for the better part of two months, by the General's own count, until the gelding settled enough that
the shaking stopped being necessary — and the General said what struck him, even at that age, was
that Cobb had never once treated the shaking as something to be ashamed of, or hidden, or fixed. It
was simply the cost of the work, paid in full every morning before anything else got done.

He told me he'd thought about that stall more times than he could count, in rooms Cobb never knew
existed and against things Cobb never had to imagine — that somewhere underneath every command he'd
ever given a soldier who had every right to be afraid of what was coming, there was still a boy his
own age walking into a horse stall with his hands shaking, doing the work anyway, and never once
mistaking the shaking for a reason to stop. He said it was, as far as he could tell, looking back, the
first time anyone had ever taught him anything true about being a man rather than about being a
Verehimu — and that the boy who taught it to him had never had any power to speak of, and had never
once needed any.

He didn't know, when he told me this, what had become of Cobb. He said he'd meant, more than once
over the years, to find out, and that something — he was never specific about what — had always kept
him from actually doing it. I never asked him to explain that further. Some men keep a door closed
not because they're afraid of what's behind it, but because they'd rather it stay exactly as they
left it.
