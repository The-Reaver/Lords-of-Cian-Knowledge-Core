This is one the General told almost apologetically, as though he worried it would sound like too
small a thing to be worth my writing down at all. I told him I'd decide that myself, once I'd heard
it, and by the end he seemed to agree it was worth the telling, even if he never quite stopped
sounding a little embarrassed by how much of it had stayed with him.

Correspondence review was, in the ordinary run of House business, the least glamorous duty Aethelgard
handed his son to learn the weight of governance by — a fortnightly stack of petitions, appeals, and
grievances filed by tenants and smallholders across the Verehimu lands, sorted first by the clerks'
office and then, in theory, read and acted upon by whichever senior Verehimu had the time. In
practice, the General said, the stack was enormous and the time was rarely found, and the clerks'
office had long since developed its own quiet triage for surviving that fact: petitions judged
substantial were forwarded up promptly; petitions judged minor, unclear, or simply poorly written were
set aside for "further review" that came, more often than the office would ever have admitted aloud,
much later or not at all.

He found Sella's petition on a night he happened to be working later than usual, three folders down
in a stack the clerks' office had marked, months earlier, as awaiting clarification. It was two pages,
written in a hand that had clearly cost its author real effort — careful, deliberate letters from
someone unused to writing at length — laying out, without much rhetorical polish, that her late
husband's tannery had been reassessed the previous year at a valuation nearly double what the
business had ever actually earned, on the strength of an inspector's report she believed had confused
the property with a larger tannery two holdings over sharing a similar name, and that the resulting
tax debt was now large enough that she expected to lose the workshop, and with it the only income she
had left, within the year.

The General said what struck him first wasn't the substance of the claim — he had, by then, read
enough petitions to know that most contained at least a plausible grievance somewhere inside them —
but the note, in a different hand, clipped to the front: *insufficient grounds for reassessment,
petitioner may resubmit with supporting documentation.* No documentation had been requested. No one
had written to tell her what documentation would satisfy the request. The petition had simply been
read once, found inconvenient to verify, and returned to the pile to wait for a follow-up that the
clerks' office had never actually asked her to provide.

He didn't summon her. He didn't raise the matter with the clerks' office, or with the Seneschal, or
with his father — the General was careful, telling me this, to say that he'd thought about doing all
three and decided against each one in turn, because any of them would have made the correction about
him, would have turned a woman's tax assessment into a story about the heir's personal intervention,
and he'd already begun to understand, by then, that the kind of help that announces itself tends to
cost the person receiving it something they never agreed to spend. Instead he rode out to the tannery
himself, alone, on an ordinary afternoon with no title attached to the visit, and looked at the
property with his own eyes, and confirmed within an hour what a competent inspector could have
confirmed in the same hour a year earlier — that Sella's tannery and the larger one two holdings over
were, in fact, two entirely different operations that happened to share a founder's family name three
generations back, and that whoever had filed the original assessment had simply pulled the wrong
figures from the wrong ledger.

The correction itself, once he had the facts in hand, took him one letter, filed through the ordinary
assessor's office under the standing authority he already held to request reassessment reviews — no
mention of the earlier note, no mention of where the correction had originated, nothing that would
have told anyone reading the file afterward that it had reached a desk higher than a clerk's. Sella
received a revised assessment within the month, at something closer to the tannery's real worth, and
as far as the General ever knew, went the rest of her life believing the correction had simply been
the system working the way it was meant to, eventually, on its own.

He told me he never met her again after that single afternoon at the tannery, and that he'd deliberately
made sure of it — that showing up a second time, once the matter was settled, would have turned a
correction into a debt she'd feel obliged to carry toward him, and the whole point, as far as he'd
ever been able to explain it even to himself, was that she shouldn't have to carry anything at all.
What he said stayed with him longest wasn't the ride out, or the hour spent comparing ledgers in a
cold tannery yard, but the plain fact that there had been, by his own rough count, several hundred
other petitions in stacks just like that one, waiting on desks just like his, that no one had happened
to be working late enough to find.
