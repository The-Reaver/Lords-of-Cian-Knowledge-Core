# The First Time She Asked

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1804`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXXV — sixth entry in the Val Mirel strand's third wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not
present in this entry. Ozmund is roughly twenty-one. Mirrors Chronicle LXXIV from the opposite
direction — the first moment Val Mirel consciously treats Ozmund as a fellow tactician rather than a
student. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructed from a story
Ozmund told him years later. No new named characters.*

---

The General told me this story with a particular kind of quiet pride I only heard from him a handful
of times in all our years together, and when I asked him once, much later, why this one in
particular still sat with him the way it did, he told me it was because it was the first time his
mother had ever asked him something rather than told him something. Twenty-one years, he said, of
being taught. This was the first morning he was asked.

She had come with a genuine problem this time, not a lesson dressed as one — a Seventh Wing matter,
sensitive enough that he never learned, telling it to me, the full shape of it, only that it concerned
a disputed reading of enemy movement along a border she wasn't permitted to name to him even now. She
laid it out for him the way she might have laid it out for an officer of her own command: the terrain,
the reported sightings, the timing of two engagements that didn't sit quite right against each other,
and then she stopped, and looked at him, and asked him plainly what he made of it.

He told me his first instinct was to defer — twenty-one years of being her student had built the
habit deep enough that his mouth had nearly formed the words *I don't know, tell me what you see*
before he caught himself and understood, watching her face, that she was not looking for that answer.
She was waiting. Genuinely waiting, the way she waited on a ridge above a grain road, for him to
actually think rather than to ask her to think for him.

He took his time with it — he told me that was itself something he'd learned from her, the patient
refusal to be hurried into an answer before he trusted it — and worked through what she'd given him the
way she had once taught him to work through the dry wash above the grain road at fifteen: what moved,
what didn't, what a patient enemy would want a watcher to see and what it would want hidden. He told
her, eventually, that the two engagements didn't sit right against each other because they weren't the
same force at all — that whoever had reported them had assumed continuity where none existed, and that
the real question wasn't what the enemy intended along that border but how many separate parties were
operating on it without each other's knowledge.

She was quiet for a long moment after he said it. He told me he'd braced, in that silence, for the
gentle correction he still half expected even then — the assumption, worn into him over two decades,
that whatever he offered would need refining by someone who simply knew more than he did. What she gave
him instead was a single nod, and then, without any further comment on the reasoning itself, she began
discussing with him what she intended to do about it, as though his answer had simply become the
ground both of them were now standing on together rather than a student's guess awaiting her judgment.

He told me it took him the better part of that conversation to understand what had actually happened.
She was not testing him. She had a genuine problem, and she had come to him — her son, not some officer
of her own command — because some part of her had already decided, before she ever laid the terrain out
for him, that his read on it was worth having. He said it was the strangest, most quietly overwhelming
feeling of his life to that point: not pride exactly, though there was plenty of that too, but the
sudden, disorienting sense of having crossed some threshold he hadn't known was there, from a person
his mother protected and taught into a person his mother consulted.

"She never told me she thought I was ready for that," he said to me, years later. "She simply started
treating me as though I already was, and let me discover, from the inside, that she was right. I think
that was always how she taught — not by telling you what you'd become, but by quietly beginning to act
as though you already had, until you had no choice but to grow into the space she'd already made for
you."

I never once, in all the years I knew him, saw him defer to anyone's judgment before he'd tested it
against his own first. I understood, hearing this story, precisely the morning that habit had actually
begun.
