# The Math He Didn't Say Aloud

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1848`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle CXIX, sixth entry of the coming-of-age strand's second wave, set strictly pre-
Fulfillment-Ceremony (`MCD-025`), Aethelgard alive throughout. A quiet meditation on the disparity
between his own Karesian-inherited long lifespan (`MCD-016`/`CC-016`, extending the already-locked
89,003-year-old Val Mirel Kareth, `MCD-101`) and the ordinary human spans of Osric and Cobb, the two
people this wave has shown him growing closest to — resolved not with despair but with a deliberate
choice about how to love people he will very likely outlive. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per
`VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`. No new named characters.*

---

Of all the stories the General ever gave me, this is the one I have come closest to calling his saddest,
though he corrected me the one time I called it that to his face, and told me I had misunderstood it
entirely. I give it to you now the way he eventually made me understand it — not as grief arriving early,
but as a young man doing arithmetic no one had asked him to do, and finding, at the end of it, something
sturdier than sorrow.

He told me it came on him gradually, the way most of his real understandings did, rather than in a
single blow. He had grown up knowing, in the abstract, distant way a child knows facts that have not yet
been tested against anyone he loves, that his mother's line carried years beyond ordinary counting — that
Val Mirel had commanded a Seventh Wing for longer than House Verehimu itself had stood, and that whatever
part of her had passed to him carried, presumably, some measure of that same long road. It was an idea he
had held the way a boy holds any inherited fact too large to feel: true, and yet weightless, until the
evening he found himself watching Osric's hands on a ledger and noticed, for what he told me was truly
the first time, that they had begun, faintly, to shake.

He said the arithmetic arrived almost against his will. Osric was not an old man yet, not by the
household's own reckoning, but he was not a young one either, and the General told me he stood in the
granary doorway doing sums he had never once let himself do before — how many more autumns of steady
counsel, how many more evenings like the one in the dark among the grain sacks, set against a span of his
own that had no clear far edge to it at all. He told me the number that frightened him was not large. It
was small. It was the plain, unavoidable fact that whatever years remained to Osric would be, to Ozmund
himself, no more than a single early chapter of a life that had not yet properly begun, and that he would
very likely go on, afterward, for a length of time Osric could not have imagined without some effort, long
after the steadiness that had shaped so much of his own boyhood had gone quiet.

He told me he thought of Cobb too, and that this was somehow the harder thought of the two — Osric he had
known always as an adult, a fixed point, but Cobb was his own age, a boy who raced him in the paddock
grass and would, in the ordinary run of things, grow old and slow and eventually stop entirely while
Ozmund, by every indication his blood gave him, would not have changed at all. He said he did not know,
standing with that thought for the first time, whether it was a cruelty built into him or simply a fact
with no moral weight attached to it whatsoever, the way a river is neither cruel nor kind for outlasting
the banks it passes between.

What he did with it, he told me, was the part he wanted me to understand plainly, because he said most
people who first hear the shape of the thing assume it must end in some kind of withdrawal — a young man
deciding, reasonably enough, that loving people he would outlive was simply too costly a proposition to
keep choosing, and pulling back from all of it before the cost could compound. He told me he had
considered exactly that, for perhaps the length of a single restless night, and had rejected it by
morning, and the reasoning he gave me for the rejection was the plainest thing he ever told me in all our
years of friendship: that a shorter life did not make Osric's steadiness worth less, and a longer one
did not make his own grief, when it eventually came, worth more. The disparity in the years was real. It
simply was not, he had decided, a fact large enough to be allowed to decide anything else for him.

He told me he never spoke the arithmetic aloud to either of them — not to Osric, not to Cobb, not for
years to come to anyone at all besides me, on a night I no longer recall the reason for. It was not a
secret kept out of fear, he was careful to say. It was simply not a burden either of them had asked to
carry, and he had already, by then, spent a good deal of his short life learning the difference between
a weight worth sharing and one worth quietly bearing alone. What he gave them instead, in the years that
followed, was not distance. It was the opposite of distance, chosen with open eyes and a private, settled
understanding of exactly what it would eventually cost him — which he told me, in the end, was the only
kind of love he ever fully trusted as genuinely his own: not the kind that assumes there will always be
enough time, but the kind that chooses fully anyway, knowing precisely how little of it, relatively
speaking, was ever going to be granted to the people standing closest to him.
