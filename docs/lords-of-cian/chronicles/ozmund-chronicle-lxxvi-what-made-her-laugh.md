# What Made Her Laugh

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1805`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXXVI — seventh entry in the Val Mirel strand's third wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive and present, in good
humor, for the first time in the strand. Ozmund is roughly twenty-four. A deliberately lighter,
warmer-toned entry varying the strand's register — ordinary domestic ease and dry humor rather than
lesson, crisis, or grief. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructed from a story Ozmund told him years later. No new named characters.*

---

I will say plainly that this is not the kind of story I expected to be given, of all the things the
General ever told me about his mother, and I think that surprised him too, telling it to me — he
laughed, partway through, in a way I'd rarely heard from him, and told me he hadn't thought of this
particular afternoon in years, and that he was glad, suddenly, to have the excuse.

She had come for what was, by then, becoming almost an ordinary kind of visit — nothing urgent behind
it, no dispatch, no wound, simply a handful of days between postings that she'd chosen, for once, to
spend doing very little at all. Aethelgard was in residence too that week, a rarer thing than either
of them, and the General told me the three of them had fallen, almost by accident, into an evening
meal together that none of them had planned with any particular purpose, the kind of ordinary family
supper he told me he could count on one hand across his entire boyhood.

What he remembered most clearly, he said, was his father attempting — with the particular
earnestness of a man who has never once succeeded at it and refuses to admit that to himself — to tell
a joke he'd heard from a visiting delegation earlier that month, something about a river-trade
concern's ledger-keeper and a shipment of counted fish that had arrived one short. The General told me
his father had never, in the whole of his life, told a joke correctly — he always arrived at the
punchline a sentence too early, or explained the setup so thoroughly that the ending landed with
nothing left to land on, and everyone at Verehimu tables had long since learned to laugh anyway, out
of genuine affection rather than genuine amusement.

Val Mirel, he told me, did not laugh at the joke. She sat through the whole of it with an expression he
could only describe, telling it to me, as scholarly — the exact patient attention she gave a raiding
band's movements or a millrace's silted bend, turned now on his father's badly mangled comic timing as
though it were a genuine tactical problem worth solving. And then, once Aethelgard had finished,
visibly pleased with himself despite having ruined the ending twice over, she asked him, entirely
straight-faced, whether the fish had perhaps simply deserted, having correctly assessed the ledger-
keeper's competence.

The General told me his father laughed so suddenly and so completely that he nearly upended his own
cup, and that it was, in his considerable experience of both his parents, the only time he ever saw his
mother make a joke of her own rather than simply enduring or observing someone else's. What struck him
even more than the joke itself, he said, was how she delivered it — with exactly the same flat,
unhurried stillness she brought to everything else, so that for one confused half-second his father
hadn't even been certain she was joking at all, and the confusion itself became part of what made it
land.

He asked her, later that evening, once his father had gone to see to some late business, whether she
was always that funny and simply never showed it. She told him, and this was the part he gave me
twice, laughing both times he told it, that a Seventh Wing tactician learned very early that a joke
delivered with visible enjoyment was a joke that told the room too much about what she found
unguarded, and that a joke delivered exactly like an order was a joke almost no one ever caught in
time to laugh at properly — which she considered, on balance, the better kind.

"I think that was the evening I understood my mother actually enjoyed my father," he told me, still
smiling, years later. "Not endured him, not simply loved him out of long habit — enjoyed him, the way
you enjoy someone whose particular foolishness you've decided, privately, is one of your favorite
things about them. She would never have said it in those words. She didn't need to. I watched her
choose, deliberately, to let one joke land exactly when it would do the most good, and I understood
that was its own kind of love, dressed up as dry wit so it never had to announce itself as anything
softer."

I asked him, once, whether she ever made another joke in front of him after that. He told me she had —
rarely, and always in that same flat, unhurried register, and that he had come, over the years, to
treasure each one precisely because they were so few. A discipline of humor, he called it once, the
same way everything else about her was a discipline. I believe that was exactly right.
