# Xaragua Chronicle I: The Line That Did Not Break

*Locked canon, Batch 64, 2026-09-05 (`MCD-334`), superseding `MCD-331`. First entry in Xaragua's own
Chronicles, its protagonist Ogoun Xarey (PH2-002), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest,
arriving partway through and departing before the story's resolution is his to claim. Narration is
close-third on Ogoun Xarey throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion (Onyx's designated scope per VB-020 is Kanja
chapters specifically, and this is not one) -- the four Voice Bible Pillars (VB-001-005) still govern tone
and language. Draws on Ogoun Xarey's already-locked profile (PH2-002, PH2-017: Mino his combat mentor,
Chui an officer under his command) but slots into no existing mainline MCD- rule -- this is original
homage-era material, not an extension of a locked Cian battle.*

---

A line holds or it does not. There is no honorable version of the second thing.

Xaragua's southern approach had been theirs for six generations before it wasn't, and eleven days
before it would be again if the coastal redoubt held until dawn. Ogoun Xarey had not slept in three
of those days and did not plan to start now. He stood at the redoubt's forward stakes with salt wind
peeling the smoke sideways off the watch-fires, and behind him eight hundred people who had already
decided, individually and without being asked twice, that they would rather die on this ground than
be walked off it again.

Mino stood at his left shoulder, the way she had stood at it since before he could hold a blade
steady in the wind. She did not speak unless the moment called for it. This one didn't, yet.

Chui came up the stake-line at a run, breathing hard, boots black with the low-tide mud. "Third
column's moving early. They know we're thin on the east face."

"Then the east face gets thinner nowhere but where it needs to." Ogoun Xarey did not raise his
voice. He never needed to; the men around him had learned to listen for the register underneath
it instead. "Pull twenty off the center. Not the redoubt gate. The center holds itself as long as
the gate holds."

"Twenty's not enough against a column."

"Twenty's what we have to give. So it's enough, because it has to be." He looked once at the dark
water past the stakes, the shape of ships he couldn't yet count riding low on the horizon. "Tell
them what I've told every line since the first one. We don't ask the ground to forgive us for
losing it. We ask it to remember we didn't."

Chui went. Mino stayed a moment longer.

"You're spending the center to save the gate," she said. Not a question. She had taught him the
doctrine; she was owed the courtesy of watching him apply it correctly.

"The gate is eight hundred people's only way off this spit of land if the night goes wrong. The
center is only ground." He did not look at her when he said it, because some sentences a man says
better to the dark than to the person who raised him to be able to say them at all. "Ground grows
back."

The man arrived sometime after that, in the gap between the first horn and the second, and Ogoun
Xarey would think, later, that he had not so much noticed him as noticed the *absence* around
him -- the specific quiet of men who did not know what they were looking at and had decided, on
instinct, not to test it. He was not dressed for Xaragua's coast. He carried nothing that
announced what he was. He simply stood at the stake-line as if he had always been standing there,
watching the horizon with the particular stillness of someone doing arithmetic no one else in the
line could see the terms of.

"You're not one of mine," Ogoun Xarey said. Not a challenge. A fact needing confirmation before it
could be filed anywhere useful.

"No."

"Then you picked a strange night to visit."

"I heard there was a man on this coast who never negotiates with the tide." A pause, weighed the
way a stranger weighs a first sentence to a room he doesn't yet own. "I wanted to see if that was
temperament or doctrine."

"Does it matter which."

"It does if I'm about to stand next to it."

Ogoun Xarey studied him a moment longer than the night could really afford. Something in the way
the stranger held the ground -- not like a soldier claiming it, not like a guest apologizing for
being on it, but like a man who had stood on worse ground than this and simply hadn't found a
reason yet to leave it -- earned him the next sentence instead of a blade.

"Doctrine," Ogoun Xarey said. "Temperament gets people killed for reasons that don't outlive them.
Doctrine gets remembered by the ones still standing after."

"Then I'll stand where the doctrine needs another body, and not one word further than that."

He did not ask for command. He did not offer strategy the line hadn't already built for itself. When
the third column broke against the thinned center exactly where Ogoun Xarey had bet it would, the
stranger was already there, absorbing what needed absorbing, giving no orders, taking none, a
weight added to a line that had already decided its own shape before he arrived. Chui would say
after that he fought like a man settling an old debt to gravity itself, that the ground seemed to
argue less with him than with everyone standing near him. Ogoun Xarey noticed only that the center
held eleven minutes longer than the doctrine had priced it to, and that eleven minutes was, by the
thinnest possible margin, the difference between the gate closing on eight hundred people and the
gate closing on considerably fewer.

Dawn found the redoubt still theirs. The third column did not come again that week, and by the time
it might have, the ships on the horizon had already decided the coast wasn't worth the count of
their own dead it would take back.

The stranger was gone before the fires were properly out, the way certain kinds of debt-payers
always are -- not thanked, not needing to be, gone in the direction of whatever coast next needed a
body added to a line already decided. He left nothing behind that Ogoun Xarey could point to and
name. Only, days later, a rumor moving mouth to mouth among the eight hundred who'd held the
gate -- that a man with no name and no flag had stood in the center where the twenty were thinnest,
and had not once, in eleven minutes of the worst of it, asked anyone standing near him to be
anything other than exactly as stubborn as they already were.

Ogoun Xarey kept that rumor the way he kept every other cost the line had paid that night. Not as a
debt he intended to collect. As a fact the ground would remember, same as it remembered everything
else that had refused to break on it.
