# What the Keeper of the Talisman Remembered

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Iron Bastard Alias Chronicle XVIII, closing the
sixth wave. Rebellion era, age 25. Not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material — a reflective
entry. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The old artificer who had first calibrated the Aegis-Talisman to Kanja's own hand, years before any
Directorate report had a name for what he'd go on to do with it, found him during a rare quiet week
between engagements and asked, without much preamble, to see the lens work one more time before age
took her hands entirely.

She was not surprised by what it had become. She was surprised by how little, in one sense, had
actually changed.

"I built it to invert a shield's function," she said, turning the Talisman over in hands that
trembled now where they hadn't years before. "Read instead of block. Everyone who commissioned it
wanted a better way to sense danger before it arrived. Nobody asked what else a tool built to listen
that carefully might end up hearing, once someone patient enough picked it up."

"You didn't build the doctrine," Kanja said. "You built the instrument. I built the doctrine out of
the years it took me to learn what the instrument was actually capable of telling me, if I was
willing to listen longer than a single warning signal."

"That's the part I remember best, if you want the honest truth. The early calibrations. You'd hold it
against a wall for an hour at a time, learning nothing anyone could see, while every other soldier I'd
ever fitted equipment for wanted results in minutes." She handed it back to him. "I thought I'd built
you a better shield. I think, now, I actually built you a better set of ears, and you're the one who
figured out what to do with them."

"Both, maybe. A shield that only blocks is a wall. One that also listens is a conversation. I'd
rather have the conversation."

She watched him go, the Talisman settled back against his forearm the way it had settled a hundred
times since, and thought — not for the first time, and not without a certain private pride — that
whatever she'd intended when she'd first shaped the lens, the instrument had long since become
something its maker could only recognize, not fully claim.
