# Uhuru Chronicle I: The Patience That Cost Him

*Locked canon, Batch 82, 2026-09-09 (`MCD-345`). First entry in Uhuru's own Chronicles, its
protagonist Ofin (`PH2-044`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but
granted no command, no intervention, and no resolution credit, matching the established convention
from Xaragua Chronicle I (`MCD-334`), Umoja Chronicle I (`MCD-335`), Yara Chronicle I (`MCD-336`),
Areíto Chronicle I (`MCD-339`), Guanín Chronicle I (`MCD-340`), Borikén Chronicle I (`MCD-341`), Ide
Chronicle I (`MCD-342`), Kwan Chronicle I (`MCD-343`), and Jibaro Chronicle I (`MCD-344`). Narration
is close-third on Ofin throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Completes a first Chronicle entry for all
five Chicago territories. Slots into no existing mainline battle — original homage-era material set
in Uhuru itself.*

---

The council chamber had voted the review board's funding down for the eleventh time before Ofin
understood, fully and without illusion, that he was no longer fighting a disagreement. Eleven votes
was not a disagreement. Eleven votes was a wall a specific, organized bloc of men had built brick by
brick, session by session, for the sole purpose of making certain that a fair-housing enforcement
board Kwan's own broker had once staked his name on — the agreement that closed one long, costly
open-housing march years before Ofin ever sat behind this desk — would exist forever on paper and
never once in fact.

He resubmitted it a twelfth time the following week. He had learned, long before he ever ran for
anything, that a wall built one brick at a time comes down the same way, and that the only real
question was never whether it would hold. It was whether he would still be standing when it stopped.

"They're going to vote it down again," his chief clerk told him, not unkindly, the way a man tells
another man something he already knows because someone has to say it out loud eventually. "Same
twenty-nine. Same reasons. They've had three years of practice being right about it."

"They've had three years of practice voting no. That's not the same thing as being right." Ofin
signed the resubmission without looking up. "They will keep having the exact same practice for as
long as I keep asking. That's the whole of my strategy, such as it is. I intend to still be asking
after they've grown tired of saying no."

The stranger had been in the outer office three nights running by then, a quiet, unannounced
presence at the edge of the late-hour work Ofin's staff had stopped questioning months ago — men
came and went from City Hall at all hours during the long grind of the council's siege, and one more
face that carried coffee and never asked for anything back drew no particular notice. Ofin noticed
him anyway, the way a man notices anything steady in a season where almost nothing else is.

"You're not one of mine," Ofin said, on the third night, not looking up from the appropriations
language he'd rewritten for what felt like the fortieth time.

"No."

"Then you're spending your evenings in a very unglamorous place for no reason I can see."

"Didn't come for the glamour." The stranger set the coffee down and didn't linger over an
explanation he clearly didn't feel he owed. "Came to see what it actually looks like, up close, the
part of this the newspapers never print — not the speech, not the vote count. This. A man rewriting
the same three paragraphs at midnight because rewriting them is the only weapon he's got left that
the other side hasn't figured out how to vote against yet."

"It isn't much of a weapon."

"Didn't say it wasn't a weapon. Said it wasn't much to look at. Different thing." The stranger
settled into a chair against the wall, unhurried, the way he did everything, and said nothing further
that night, or the next.

The thirteenth vote failed by one fewer margin than the twelfth. The fourteenth failed by three fewer
than that. Ofin did not celebrate the shrinking margins the way his staff did, quietly, in the
hallway where he couldn't hear them — he had learned that celebrating a wall for cracking was a good
way to stop noticing exactly how much of yourself the cracking had actually cost. He had started
sleeping less that year than he had any year of his life, and had told no one, including the
physician his wife had quietly begun insisting he see, precisely how little.

The court-ordered redistricting that finally broke the council's hostile bloc arrived, when it
arrived, almost as an afterthought to the fight itself — a legal mechanism doing in one ruling what
three years of resubmitted paragraphs had already worn most of the way down on its own. The
seventeenth vote, the first with a genuinely different council seated in front of him, passed by
eleven votes rather than failing by eleven. The review board that Kwan's broker had once staked one
public sentence on, years before, in a different ward entirely, was real now, funded, seated,
sending its first case files out within the month.

"You did it," the clerk said, in the hallway, the closest thing to celebration Ofin allowed anyone
in his own office.

"We did it. I only ever did the part that was mine, which was refusing to stop." Ofin looked, for
just a moment, older than the date on his own birth certificate said he ought to. "That's not a
small part. It's just not the whole of it, and I'd rather everyone in this building remember that
before the histories decide it was."

The stranger was gone by the time the board's first case file went out, the way he always seemed to
be. He left nothing carved, no phrase repeated hand to hand — only, weeks later, a single unsigned
note on Ofin's own desk, in a hand no one in the office recognized, reading nothing but a question:
*how many more walls are left, and how much of yourself do you have left to spend on them?*

Ofin read it twice. He did not answer it, not out loud, not to anyone. He put it in the drawer where
he kept the things he intended to think about later, when there was time, and went back to the next
paragraph that needed rewriting.
