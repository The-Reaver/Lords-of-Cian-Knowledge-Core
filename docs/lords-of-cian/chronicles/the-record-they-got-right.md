# The Record They Got Right

*Locked canon, Batch 262, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1327`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LXXXI, wave
27, closing the wave. Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241). A counterpart
entry to "The Ballad That Outgrew the Truth" (MCD-881), the alias's first accurate oral record. Not
a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The woman had followed the campaign for six settlements now, always at the edge of things, never
asking for anything, filling a worn ledger of her own with a hand that wrote faster than most
people spoke. She wasn't a smith and had never claimed to be one. She called herself a keeper of
accounts, though the accounts she kept had nothing to do with tallies or debts — names, dates,
exact figures, the precise sequence of who had done what and when, corrected on the spot whenever
a settlement's own retelling started to drift toward something more flattering than the truth had
actually been.

"You could make it a better story," Kanja said, the first time he actually sat with her and read
a few pages, watching her cross out a settlement elder's claim that the campaign had arrived at
the very moment of the district's greatest need, replacing it with the plainer fact that they'd
simply been due to pass through on schedule. "Most people would."

"Most people already do," she said, not looking up from the page. "There's a ballad two tours back
that has you calling down lightning. I've heard three different versions of the Furnace-district
numbers since I started this, each one rounder than the last. Somebody has to write down what
actually happened, plainly, while there are still people alive who remember it well enough to
correct me if I get it wrong."

He asked her, eventually, why it mattered to her specifically — why an accurate record was worth
six settlements' worth of unpaid, unglamorous walking. "Because the exaggerated version makes it
about you," she said. "The plain version makes it about the method. Anyone can learn a method. Nobody
can learn to be a legend, and I don't think that's actually what any of this was supposed to teach
people in the first place."

She never asked him to endorse the record, and he never offered to. It existed alongside the
ballad, alongside the myth, neither one erasing the other — a plain, precise counterweight sitting
quietly in a worn ledger that would outlast every exaggerated verse the moment anyone cared enough
to actually check.

"You'll finish this someday," Kanja said, watching her write. "What happens to it then?"

"Someone will read it who needs the plain version more than the song," she said. "That's all it
ever needed to be for."
