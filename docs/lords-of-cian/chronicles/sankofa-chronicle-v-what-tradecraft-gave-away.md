# Sankofa Chronicle V: What Tradecraft Gave Away

*Locked canon, Batch 229, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1025`). Fifth Sankofa territory Chronicle, third to touch
the forged-letter/pamphlet conspiracy from Chronicles II and IV (`MCD-360`, `MCD-1023`). Protagonist
Baálé (`PH2-021`), not a Kanja Chronicle. Per the pacing agreed in Batch 225: this is the deepening
"crack" entry, not the reveal -- the conspiracy risks real exposure for the first time without naming
its author. Kanja is deliberately absent from this entry -- a first for the sub-series.*

---

Four months of silence followed the pamphlets — no further wave, no gloat, nothing — and the silence
unsettled Baálé more than another escalation would have. He'd started to say as much to Kra on the
evening a man he'd never seen before found him alone on the clinic's back stair at dusk and told him,
without preamble or theater, that he had come to kill him.

It was, Baálé understood within the first exchanged blow, nothing like Kojo's attack three Chronicles
before. Kojo had come over territory, in daylight, in front of witnesses, wanting to be seen doing
it. This man came alone, at dusk, to the one door in Sankofa no crowd ever gathered at, and he fought
like someone trained specifically for this and only this — economical, silent, uninterested in words
after the first ones. He was also, Baálé registered somewhere under the working part of his mind that
never fully stopped calculating, not fighting like a man who expected to survive the attempt. He was
fighting like a man who'd been told the target rarely let attackers live long enough to matter.

He was wrong about that, the same way every one of them eventually was. The man went down hard in the
fourth minute, disarmed and pinned, very much alive — and "The Turn" closed over him the way it always
did, the fight draining out of his body and something else moving in to fill the space it left, the
same total, involuntary loyalty that had claimed Kojo on a daylight street a year before.

What made this one different came a breath later, when Kra — who had heard the noise and come
running with two others — stopped hard at the mouth of the alley behind the stair, staring up at a
low roofline where a second shape had been crouched, sighted, and ready, and was now gone.

"There was someone up there," Kra said. "Aimed. I watched him not take it."

They found no one on the roof and no trail worth following past the second street over — whoever it
had been moved like they'd planned the exit as carefully as the position. But the shape of it was
plain enough once Baálé had a minute to stand still and think it through: the man on the stair had
been the blade meant to draw him into "The Turn's" one condition, face to face, close enough to bind
— and the second man on the roof had been there to make sure that condition never got the chance to
matter, a shot that didn't need Baálé to survive an exchange at all. It should have worked. Whoever
gave the order had understood the ability's own limitation better than almost anyone outside Sankofa
ever had.

It hadn't worked because the man with the shot lined up chose, in the end, not to take it.

"They finally tried to kill me instead of just scaring everyone around me," Baálé said later, in the
clinic's back room, with the bound man — who gave his name, when asked, as Yao, and answered every
other question the same flat, cooperative way "The Turn" always produced — seated across from him
under Kojo's watch. "That's not nothing. Letters and pamphlets don't risk a man's life. This did.
Whoever's behind this is running low on patience, or running low on other options, or both."

"Or they thought this time they'd finally solved you," Kra said.

It was Yao, unprompted and without any sense that he was handing over anything of value, who gave
them the piece that actually mattered. Asked how he'd been contracted, he described it the way a man
describes an ordinary transaction: a message left for him at a dead drop behind a grain merchant's
stall, instructions folded a particular way — three folds, corner to corner, the last one tucked
rather than creased flat — with the specifics of the job written inside in a hand he'd never seen and
a chalk mark on the merchant's back wall the following morning confirming the drop had been collected
and the job accepted.

Baálé had gone very still well before Yao finished describing the fold.

He had seen that fold before. Not recently — years before, in the worst weeks of his life, on paper
that had nearly gotten him and Kra both killed by turning two allied organizations against each other
with words neither side had actually written. The COINTELPRO letters had used exactly that fold,
three corners in and the last one tucked rather than creased, a signature so specific and so useless
to anyone without direct knowledge of the originals that it had never once made it into anything
either side ever said publicly about the ambush afterward.

"Someone taught him that fold," Baálé said, holding the memory of it against what Yao had just
described until the two sat perfectly, impossibly matched. "Or someone who used that fold on us
taught someone else. Either way, this isn't new people copying old tricks they read about somewhere.
This is the same hand, or a hand that learned directly from it."

"The letters that almost killed us," Kra said slowly, "were never solved. Whoever wrote them was
never caught."

"I know." Baálé set the memory of the fold down the way he'd have set down anything too sharp to
hold for long. "I've known that for years and made my peace with not knowing. I'm not sure I get to
keep that peace much longer."

He did not say a name, because he did not have one — only a method, a fold, and the cold, settling
certainty that the thing which had nearly ended him and Kra both at the very start of everything had
never actually ended at all. It had waited. It had adjusted its patience across a decade of silence
and a season of paper before finally, for the first time, risking a hand where he could see it — and
losing that hand, this once, only because someone standing behind it had, at the last possible
second, chosen not to fire.

Baálé kept the fold. He did not go looking for what it meant yet. But he stopped, that night, calling
the thing behind the letters and the pamphlets a shape without a face, and started calling it,
privately, by the only word that fit what four months of silence and one botched assassination had
just told him: unfinished.
