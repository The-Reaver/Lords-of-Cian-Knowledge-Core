Six weeks on, the thing that struck Daba most was how ordinary the work had become. Not the grief — the grief hadn't gone anywhere, had simply learned to sit quietly in a corner of most hours instead of filling all of them — but the days themselves, which had resolved into a shape not so different from the ones before the fire: counting stores, mending what could be mended, keeping children fed and occupied by people who had, against every reasonable expectation, kept showing up to do it. They had a room now, borrowed above a cooper's shop two districts from the tannery, and on a particular grey evening Daba found himself in it with Sana, Torvin, and Odalys, doing nothing more urgent than sorting a crate of salvaged tools by what still had an edge and what needed the grinding wheel.

Odalys had the ledger open beside her, out of habit more than need — she still carried it everywhere, had taken to calling it the only thing in her life that had never once lied to her — and it was open, as it often was these days, to the last page of names. Mika sat cross-legged near the stove teaching two smaller children a hand-clap game that was not, quite, Coalstep, though Daba recognized the bones of it and said nothing.

"Eighteen hundred and four," Odalys said, not reading it so much as saying it the way you'd say a name you'd worn smooth from handling. She had taken, in the weeks since, to saying the number aloud sometimes for no occasion at all, the way another person might touch a scar to be sure it was still there. "I keep waiting for it to feel like less of a weight. It doesn't. I don't think it's supposed to."

"No," Torvin agreed, from the corner, working a whetstone over a chisel with the same patient thoroughness he'd once given the tannery's water lines. "But a weight can be carried two ways. You can let it press you flat, or you can pick it up and decide what it's for."

Sana looked up from the tools. "What's it *for*, though. It's not for anything. It's just what happened."

"Is it," Daba said. He hadn't meant to speak — the thought had been sitting in him for weeks the way the idea in the crossing had sat unfinished, and it chose that moment, in that unremarkable room, over a crate of dull knives, to finish itself. "A fire that size doesn't happen by accident twice without someone deciding it's cheap to repeat. They wrote *undetermined origin* because the number cost them nothing to erase on paper. But it didn't erase. It's sitting right there in Odalys's book, and it's sitting in all of us whether we say it out loud or not. So maybe it's not a weight at all. Maybe it's the one thing they can't take back from us, because it already happened, and it's already ours to keep. A number like that could be a wound forever. Or it could be the one thing that makes sure nothing like it happens to a settlement this size again — because nothing this size ever gets a chance to burn all the way down before someone with that number behind them is already gone, already scattered, already too small and too far apart to catch."

The room was quiet for a moment that wasn't uncomfortable, only full. Odalys closed the ledger, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child rather than shut it.

"You're not talking about grieving anymore," she said.

"No," Daba admitted. "I don't think I have been for a while."

Nobody in that room gave it a name that evening — there was no roster to found, no oath sworn over the crate of half-sharpened knives, nothing so clean as a founding. There was only Torvin nodding slowly over his whetstone, and Sana going quiet in the particular way she went quiet when a thought was rearranging something in her rather than passing through, and Mika's hand-clap song still going in the corner, bright and entirely unaware of the weight the number carried three feet away from her. But something had shifted its footing in the room all the same, the way a floor settles once, permanently, under a weight it has finally agreed to bear. The number stayed a number a while longer. It would not stay only that forever.
