# The Engineer Who Came to Disprove Him

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle XXI, closing wave 6.
Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral
third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

The Trust engineer arrived three weeks into the campaign's coastal leg with a commission from a
superior who wanted the "metabolizes punishment" reputation formally debunked — a measured,
credentialed report proving the rebuild-speed claims were exaggeration, propaganda, or simple
survivorship bias from a handful of lucky sites, something a nervous garrison command could point to
and stop losing sleep over.

He measured everything. Beam-cure times, joint tolerances, the actual headcount at each rebuild
against the reported figures, the intervals between a burning and the first new timber going up. He
cross-checked settlement accounts against each other for inflation. He was, by his own private
admission, thorough almost to the point of rudeness, and he expected, going in, to find the gap
between legend and fact that every report he'd ever written on this kind of reputation had eventually
found.

"You're welcome to keep measuring," Kanja told him, midway through the third week, unbothered by the
engineer trailing the crew with his tally board. "I'd rather you measure it honestly than take my word
for any of it."

"That's an unusual answer from someone with a reputation to protect."

"I don't have a reputation I need you to protect for me. I've got a method that either holds up or it
doesn't. If your numbers say it doesn't, I'd genuinely like to know that before the next site burns,
not after."

The numbers, when the engineer finally finished compiling them, held up in every measurable respect —
rebuild times faster than any conventional projection could explain by material availability or crew
size alone, a consistency across sites that ruled out any single lucky outlier. He wrote the report
exactly as the data demanded.

It was reassigned before it reached the officer who'd commissioned it, filed instead under a
classification that ensured almost no one would read it. The engineer kept his own copy of the raw
figures anyway, in a private ledger he never submitted to anyone, and told no one on either side what
it actually said — not from fear, he told himself, but because he had learned, watching the numbers
resolve the way they had, that some conclusions were true regardless of who was permitted to hear
them.
