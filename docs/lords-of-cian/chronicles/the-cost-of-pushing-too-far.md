# The Cost of Pushing Too Far

*Locked canon, Batch 160, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-503`). The Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle X, first
entry in the fourth wave. Rebellion era, age 29, the Gale Straits. Not a territory Chronicle. New
standalone material — the first genuine loss on Kanja's own side in this alias's run. Narrated in
neutral third-person prose. No new named characters.*

---

Sephtis's prediction gave a narrow window for a decisive strike, tighter and riskier than any timing
the fleet had used before — a chance to end a Directorate squadron's operations for good, if the
fleet committed fully rather than holding the usual margin of caution. Kanja chose to commit fully.

One escort vessel, pushed harder into the storm's early edge than its hull could safely bear, didn't
make it through. Six crew were lost.

"The prediction was accurate," Sephtis said afterward, no defensiveness in it, simply an honest
account laid beside the cost. "The storm arrived exactly when I said it would. The risk wasn't in the
prediction. It was in how close to the edge you chose to sail against it."

"I know exactly where the risk actually sat. That was my choice, not a failure of your forecasting."
Kanja didn't deflect the responsibility onto the doctrine itself. "I've built this entire approach on
using the storm's timing as an advantage. Today I pushed that advantage past what one of my own ships
could actually survive, chasing a decisive outcome instead of respecting the margin that's kept this
fleet's own people safe through every engagement before this one."

"Does that change how the fleet uses the timing going forward?"

"It has to. A decisive strike isn't worth six of our own people if a more patient version of the same
plan would have cost us nothing." Kanja reviewed the engagement's full account, the squadron's defeat
recorded honestly beside the escort's loss rather than let the victory crowd out the cost. "I've asked
a great deal of this crew's trust in my judgment across every use of this doctrine. Today that trust
cost six of them their lives because I mistook aggression for decisiveness. I don't intend to make
that mistake again."

The fleet held a wider margin in every subsequent engagement, the decisive strike's success weighed
permanently against what it had actually cost to achieve — a hard lesson absorbed rather than
smoothed over into another clean victory.
