The forge Daba stood in that evening belonged to a cell he had never personally visited before, in a settlement he could not have found without a guide, and the young man working the bellows was one he had never met and whose name he had deliberately not been told before he arrived, the same courtesy the network now extended to him as automatically as it once had only to strangers. He watched the young man finish a hinge-plate — competent work, not remarkable, the kind of steady unremarkable competence that told you more about a teacher than a flash of brilliance ever could — and only afterward, when the piece was cooling and the conversation had turned to easier things, did he ask the question he'd come to ask.

"Who taught you the temper line?"

"A woman two settlements south," the young man said. "Never told me her name, and I never asked. She said she'd been taught by a man in Fenmark, and he'd been taught by someone called Coen." He looked up, uncertain whether the name meant anything, unaware he was saying it to the one person alive for whom it meant nearly everything. "You know him?"

"I know him," Daba said, and did not say more, because saying more was not what the moment required, and because the young man's own not-knowing was, itself, the entire point standing in the room with them both.

He did the accounting on the walk back, the way he had taken to doing it every year or two, less because the number itself mattered and more because the shape of the number told him things a single conversation couldn't. Coen, from Fenmark, had by now taught four people directly, each of whom had gone on to teach at least one more, none of whom had ever compared notes with any of the others, none of whom had ever met Sabeth or her own now-considerable line running east through the river towns, and neither line had ever crossed paths with the six smaller branches that had grown, more slowly and more quietly, out of five other cells Daba had seeded in those same early years and mostly, deliberately, stopped personally tending. Somewhere in a settlement he had never visited, a forger he had never met was teaching a technique he himself had learned three hands removed from the man who had taught it to Daba, who had learned it, in turn, from a boy of eighteen sharpening a machete on a cliffside with no formal training in anything and an instinct for warfare that ran in his blood before either of them had a word for why.

That was the part that still, after all these years, caught in Daba's chest a certain way when he let himself sit with it. He had built this specifically so that it would not need him — so that a raid on any single forge, any single teacher, any single settlement, would find a competent stranger with a modest stock and nothing whatsoever connecting them to the man who'd started it, three or four or now five hands back up a chain nobody on the ground could see the top of. He had built it, in other words, to survive his own absence, his own capture, his own death, without ever once being tested by any of those things — and the proof that it was working was precisely this evening's quiet ordinariness: a young man who had never heard his name, doing steady unremarkable work, entirely unaware that the man watching him finish a hinge-plate was the reason the technique existed on Cian at all.

He thought, walking home under a sky that gave nothing away, of Wren Talsen's glove — the private line scored into its inside seam, meant for no eye but hers, an accounting that lived and would go on living whether or not anyone ever again connected it back to Sabeth, or to him, or to the boy who had taught them both without ever asking to be remembered for it. A central armory, he thought, could be burned to the ground in a single night and take the whole of what it held down with it. What he had built instead had no ground to burn. It had only hands, teaching hands, scattered wide enough now that he could stand in a forge three names removed from his own and watch it holding, unremarkably, entirely on its own.
