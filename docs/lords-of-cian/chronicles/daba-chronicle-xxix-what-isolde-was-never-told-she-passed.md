# Daba Chronicle XXIX: What Isolde Was Never Told She Passed

Isolde Wrenna first asked to join 1804 the way most people who eventually joined it first asked -- indirectly, through a favor done for someone who knew someone, in her case a physician's assistant's debt to a woman Nessa had quietly kept fed through a hard winter years before. She did not know Nessa's name. She did not know 1804's name either, only that something in the district moved grain and medicine to the people who needed it most without ever asking to be thanked, and she wanted, plainly and without much art in how she said it, to be part of whatever that something was.

Nessa liked her within the first hour of meeting her, which she told Daba honestly and which he took, just as honestly, as no reason at all to shorten what came next.

"She's not one of ours," he said. "Not the Rookery's. Not anyone who buried what we buried. That's not a mark against her. It just means we owe her nothing yet, and she's owed us nothing either, and trust that isn't owed has to be built the slow way or it isn't real trust at all."

The slow way took the better part of a year, and Isolde, for most of it, was never told she was being tested at all. She was given small tasks first -- carrying a sealed message three streets over, minding a supply cache for an afternoon, nothing that could hurt anyone badly if she proved careless or worse. Each task was watched, quietly, by someone she never identified as watching. She carried the message without reading it, though the seal would have been easy enough to lift and reset. She minded the cache without ever once mentioning its location to the friend who walked her partway home. Small things, none of them dramatic, all of them logged.

The test that mattered came later, and it was Nessa's own idea, built from a technique 1804 had used since its first year without ever quite naming it as a technique: a piece of false information, plausible and specific, given to Isolde alone and to no one else in the district, concerning a shipment route that did not exist. If the route ever surfaced anywhere it shouldn't -- in a Trust patrol's sudden interest, in a rival smuggler's boast, in a stranger's careless question -- it could only have come from her, and there would be no argument left to make on her behalf.

Nothing surfaced. Three months passed, and the false route went nowhere at all, and Isolde never once asked why the task she'd been given for it had quietly dissolved into nothing, because by then she had learned, without being taught the lesson directly, that 1804's work often simply stopped mattering the way it had seemed to at first, and had stopped finding that strange.

"She held it clean," Nessa told Daba, "and she never even knew it was a test worth holding."

"That's the only kind worth trusting," Daba said. "A person who knows she's being watched behaves for the watching. I need to know how she behaves when there's no one to perform for."

He met her himself, finally, on an ordinary evening that she would not learn until much later had been chosen for exactly that ordinariness -- nothing at stake, nothing to prove, a conversation rather than an initiation. He asked her only one question that carried any real weight, and asked it plainly, the way he asked most things that mattered.

"If I told you the whole of what we are, right now, and you decided afterward you wanted no part of it -- what would you do with what you'd learned?"

She thought about it longer than he expected, which he took, silently, as a point in her favor before she ever spoke a word.

"I'd forget it as hard as I could," she said finally. "Not because you asked me to. Because I've spent a year watching what this district looks like with you in it, quietly, and I don't want to be the reason it stops."

He told her the name that night -- the whole of it, the tragedy the number stood for, the doctrine built from it -- and watched her sit with the weight of eighteen hundred and more dead the way every genuine new member sat with it, in silence first and questions after. She did not ask to be trusted with more than she'd earned. She asked, instead, what she could be taught, and Daba, listening to the question, thought that this -- not the year of small unwitnessed tests, though those had mattered too -- was the last and truest proof he'd been watching for all along.
