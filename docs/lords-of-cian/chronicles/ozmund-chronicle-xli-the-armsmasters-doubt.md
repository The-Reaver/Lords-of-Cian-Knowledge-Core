# The Armsmaster's Doubt

Berrin Hollis had trained three generations of House Verehimu men-at-arms and buried two commanders
he'd outlived by the time I found him, an old man with a bad hip and a worse temper, still running
drills twice a week on a yard he swore had gotten smaller since his knees got worse. He didn't want
to talk about the General at first. He said everyone who came asking wanted the same story — the
Spike, the arena, the crown he'd thrown back in everyone's face — and none of them wanted the part he
actually remembered, which was a boy who couldn't hold a proper guard to save his life for the better
part of a year. I told him that was exactly the part I wanted. He looked at me a long time before he
believed it, and then he talked for three hours without stopping once.

He'd been given the boy at eleven, on Aethelgard's own order, with instructions that amounted to
almost nothing — teach him the forms, teach him discipline, and above all teach him nothing that
depended on strength he might or might not have, because Aethelgard wanted a son who could fight as a
man fights, not a son who'd been handed an advantage he hadn't earned and told to call it skill.
Hollis said he'd taken the assignment without much thought, another heir to run through the standard
progression, and had his first real doubt about the boy inside a week.

It wasn't that Ozmund was slow. Hollis was firm on that point, unwilling to let me write it any other
way — the boy learned the forms as fast as any student he'd had, faster than most. It was that he
learned them wrong on purpose, or wrong in a way that looked deliberate even when Hollis couldn't
prove it: a guard held a half-second too loose, a strike pulled a hand's width short of where it
should have landed, footwork that kept him perpetually one step further back than the drill called
for. Hollis had trained enough young nobles to know the shape of a boy coasting on his name, and for
the better part of that first year he was certain that was exactly what he was looking at — a future
lord humoring an old man because his father had told him to show up, holding back not out of fear but
out of the quiet, bone-deep certainty that none of it would ever actually matter to him.

He told the boy so, once, flatly, in front of the whole yard — that he could see a student pretending
to try, and that pretending was worse than not trying at all, because at least a lazy man was honest
about it. He said he expected the boy to go running to his father over it. Instead Ozmund said
nothing, finished the session, and came back the next morning two hours before the yard normally
opened and was already working the same form, alone, when Hollis arrived to unlock the racks.

That became the pattern for the better part of a year — the boy present for every scheduled session,
holding the same careful, deliberately incomplete guard Hollis had learned to read as evasion, and
then absent from wherever anyone thought to look for him until the early mornings gave him away. Hollis
started arriving earlier himself, just to see how early the boy actually started, and never once beat
him to the yard. He told me he came to understand, slowly and without ever being told outright, that
he wasn't watching a boy who didn't care. He was watching a boy working twice as hard as any student
he'd ever had to prove something Hollis didn't yet have the shape of — that whatever came easily to
him wasn't going to be the thing he let define what he was worth.

The doubt broke, Hollis said, on an ordinary afternoon eleven months in, when Ozmund finally executed
a full disarm exactly as drilled — full extension, full commitment, nothing held back — and put Hollis's
own practice blade in the dirt in front of the whole yard. Hollis remembered being more startled by his
own reaction than by the disarm itself: not pride exactly, not yet, but the sudden, total collapse of
a year's certainty that he'd been reading the boy correctly. He asked him afterward, alone, why it had
taken so long to see what the boy had apparently always had in him. Ozmund's answer, which Hollis
repeated to me word for word because he said he'd never forgotten it, was that he hadn't been holding
back the technique. He'd been holding back everything else — everything that would have let anyone,
including himself, mistake a natural gift for the reason it worked.

Hollis kept training him for another eight years after that, through what he called, without
exaggeration, the most demanding student he'd ever had — not because the boy was gifted, though by the
end he plainly was, but because he never once again let a session pass where Hollis could point to
something he hadn't fully committed to. He told me he'd trained sons of lords who wanted to be told
they were exceptional, and one who wanted, more than anything else Hollis had ever seen in thirty
years on that yard, to be told only that he'd earned it.
