# The Testimony That Contradicted Itself

*Locked canon, Batch 256, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1148`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXIV, wave 22. The first entry where two sincere witnesses give honestly incompatible testimony and the ledger's own discipline finds neither one lying. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The husband said the looms had run six days a week since spring. The wife, testifying an hour later without having heard him, said five, and both of them said it with the flat, unhesitating certainty of people who had lived the number rather than guessed it. Ezio took both accounts down exactly as given, the way he took everyone's, and did not point out the conflict to either of them before they left the tent.

It would have been easy, and faster, to assume one of them was protecting something — a debt, an absence, a reason the sixth day mattered to hide. Ezio had seen that shape before, the padded claim, the convenient blank spot. This didn't read the same. Neither account wavered under the small, ordinary follow-up questions that usually cracked a comfortable lie. Both of them answered instantly, from memory, not performance.

He ran the mill's own production logs against both figures separately rather than trying to average them into something that satisfied neither. What he found wasn't a contradiction so much as two true accounts of two different halves of a year: the mill had run six days through spring and summer, then quietly dropped to five with the autumn light, a schedule change the owner had never posted or announced, absorbed into the workforce's routine so gradually that no single worker had noticed the exact week it happened. The husband, on the early shift, remembered the season he'd worked hardest. The wife, further from the looms by then with a newborn keeping her home two mornings a week, remembered the season closer to when she'd last counted carefully.

"They're not disagreeing," Ezio told Kanja, laying both testimonies side by side with the log between them. "They're each right about a different year."

Kanja read it twice before he said anything. "Most people would've made one of them wrong to make the numbers simpler."

"Most people aren't paid to be right instead of simple." Ezio wrote the finding as two figures, not one — wages owed at the six-day rate through the season it held, wages owed at the five-day rate through the season it changed, the exact week of the shift pinned down to within three days by cross-referencing a delivery record neither testifier had known existed. Neither the husband nor the wife had to be corrected, apologized to, or told they'd misremembered. Both left with a number that matched what they'd actually lived, because the arithmetic had been patient enough to hold two true things at once instead of forcing them into one.

The owner, confronted with a finding built from his own unannounced schedule change, didn't dispute a figure of it. What he argued, weakly, was that no one had ever asked him directly when the hours changed. Ezio's answer was in the ledger already: he hadn't needed to ask. The mill's own delivery logs had told him, once he'd stopped assuming the conflict meant someone was lying and started assuming it might mean two people were simply standing in different weeks.
