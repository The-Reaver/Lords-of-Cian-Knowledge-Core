This is the last of what the General ever told me about the years before I knew him, and he told it
to me only once, very late, in a year when he'd already begun saying things out loud that he'd
clearly been carrying silently for a long time. He said he wasn't sure it was a story at all. It was
closer, he said, to something a person remembers the shape of without remembering when they first
heard it — the way you know a lullaby before you can remember being sung to.

He had a nursemaid in his infancy named Ysbel, old even then, who had served three Verehimu
generations before his and outlived enough of them to have opinions about the House that nobody senior
enough to silence her ever quite got around to silencing. Aethelgard trusted her absolutely and never
once asked what she said to the children left in her care, which the General thought, telling it to
me, was probably the only reason any of it survived long enough to reach him at all.

Ysbel never called it the Crown-Scar. Nobody in the nursery did — that was a title for the hall, for
formal portraits and the marks of House inheritance read aloud at ceremonies, and Ysbel had no more
use for it than she had for most things spoken in halls. She called it, instead, the Waking Weight,
and she said it the way you'd name a thing you respected and didn't entirely trust, which the General
said was exactly right, though it took him the better part of two centuries to understand why. She
would trace two fingers along the mark on his shoulder while he slept — he only knew this because she
told him herself, years later, an old woman by then, half-amused that he didn't remember it — and say,
not quite to him and not quite to herself, that the Waking Weight never slept because it was owed
something the rest of him hadn't finished paying, and that a boy who carried it would spend his whole
life discovering, a little at a time, exactly what that debt was and to whom.

She had a story to go with it, half legend and half nursery rhyme, worn down by however many Verehimu
children had heard some version of it before him — that Drakmund himself, the first to carry it, five
thousand years gone by the time Ysbel was old enough to have heard it from someone who was old when
she was young, had never called it a gift either, not once, not in any account that survived him. The
House's own formal history called it an honor, a mark of favor from whatever hand had placed it. Ysbel's
version, the nursery version, said only that Drakmund had described it, late in a very long life, as
something that watched him more faithfully than he had ever watched it — that it listened harder than
it was listened to, and gave less than it took, and that he had never once in five thousand years
managed to teach it otherwise.

The General told me he hadn't thought about Ysbel's version in decades by the time he told it to me,
and that he wasn't sure, even then, whether he believed an old woman's nursery superstition carried
any real truth in it at all, or whether it was simply the kind of story people tell about power they
don't understand, dressed up as wisdom because uncertainty is easier to live with if it rhymes. He
said he'd stopped needing an answer to that question a long time before he stopped thinking about it.
What he remembered most clearly, he said — more clearly than the words themselves, which had blurred
with retelling the way all nursery things do — was Ysbel's hands on his shoulder in the dark, careful
and a little wary, touching the mark the way you'd touch something that was, in its own quiet way,
touching you back.
