The ship had a limp.

She had felt it since noon. A hitch in the stern each time the swell lifted the quarter, a small hard sound under the long ones. Iron on iron, once, and then the swell let go of the hull and the sound was gone.

She stood at the rail with the evening sun low on her face and listened to the sea tell her the rest. A long swell out of the south-west with a short chop riding on it. Higher than it had any right to be on a sky this milky. The wind would back before morning. By the last watch there would be weather.

Behind her, a lamp-wick scraped.

The hand called Wystan stood at the lantern locker with a rag, trimming the stern lantern she hung over her hammock. He was a harbor-watch man from Windbreak, twenty-six, rigger's hands, the left thumb crooked from a bad splice. He had hauled stone on the mole all that autumn under the fleet's masons, and the day the last cap-stone was seated he had asked the bosun whether the Captain's ship took hands. The bosun had said it did.

Three months aboard. Her lantern trimmed each dusk. Her water-skin full each dawn. A bowl set on the rail beside her at the evening meal. Nobody had told him to do any of it.

He felt her looking. His face came round to her and held.

She knew the look. A good many harbors had given it to her.

"Wystan."

"Yes."

"The rudder tackle. Something is chafing it. Go down and serve it."

He wiped his hands. "Which turn?"

"Find it."

"By when?"

"Dawn."

He took the lamp from the locker and went aft along the deck, and the hatch grating by the helm took his light and swallowed it. She heard the ladder, and the low bump of his head on a beam, and a word under his breath.

Hamund came up beside her at the rail and said nothing for a while.

"It was served at Windbreak," he said. "Every turn. I watched the carpenter do it."

"Something is chafing."

He looked at her. She watched the sea.

"He's gone down alone," Hamund said.

"Yes."

"With a lamp, in the stern flat."

"Yes."

Hamund turned his face to the water. After a while he went forward to the benches and sat with his back to the mast.

• • •

She walked.

Mainmast to taffrail. Taffrail to mainmast. Forty strides each way on the deck, the chains swaying at her knees, the planks cool through her boot soles. The hatch lay aft of the helm, a square of grating the width of a man's shoulders, and every ninth turn her path took her across it.

The first time, the sound came up through the bars. A tock. Then a tock. A serving mallet on a wrapped line, slow and even, a man working the twine round a rope one turn at a time.

The sun went down. The lamps came on along the rail. She walked.

On the sixth pass the tock stopped, and she waited three strides for it to start. It did not. She went on to the mast and turned and came back, and on the seventh pass it was going again. On the eighth it stopped and did not start, and she walked the whole length of the ship twice before it did.

She did not look down through the grating.

All of it hung on that. A man working in the dark worked for the rope or for the face that had sent him. From the deck she heard a mallet. The mallet did not say which.

Her three had never been given work in the dark. They had turned their backs to her in the eastern passage at Chain Harbor and faced the garrison, and no one had told them to.

She turned at the taffrail and walked on.

Edda crossed her path at the second watch with a wedge of hard cheese, put it in her hand, and went below. She ate it on her feet.

The wind backed an hour after midnight, as she had known it would. It came cold. The chop steepened on the swell and began to slap at the quarter, and spray flew aft over the rail in handfuls. The helmsman, a grey-bearded man with forearms like capstan bars, shifted his feet on the planks and hung his weight on the tiller. The ship moved under her boots like an animal that had begun to expect something.

The tock came up from below, slow and even.

• • •

It came in the last watch, before there was any grey in the east.

The sea told her first. A flat hard note under the swell, a new thing, a line of wind that had weight in it, coming up from the south-west at a speed no sail was ready for. She had the length of three breaths.

"Helm," she said.

The helmsman looked at her. His face held. His hands tightened on the bar.

"Hold her head to it."

The first of it struck the quarter with a noise like a wall falling.

The ship heeled and came back, and the stern lifted on a black hill of water and slid off its back. The tiller bar slammed over in the helmsman's hands. He went with it, his feet leaving the planks, and the ship swung broadside to the sea with her rigging screaming.

Below the deck, in the dark under the helm, a bang. Iron taking a load all at once. Then a sound she knew, the sound of a thing about to let go.

She had her hands on the bar before the next sea came.

It hit the rudder from the side. The kick came up the bar like a mule's, through the helmsman's arms and into hers, and she took it. All of it. The jar went into her shoulders and her spine and came back up the bar heavier than the sea had sent it, and she put it into the bar and hauled. The helmsman roared and hauled with her.

The rudder bit.

The stern swung. The bow came up through the black water, shedding, and the ship's head came round to the sea with a groan from every timber, and held.

The next sea broke over the quarter and ran off her shoulders. She had the bar. She had it for the next one and the one after, the chains swinging and snapping at her knees, the links warm and singing.

Below the helm there was a sound again, small, from the flat. A voice, speaking to the iron. One word, over and over.

"Hold. Hold. Hold."

It held.

• • •

The squall passed through in the time it takes to eat a meal. The wind backed again and dropped. The sea went on slapping at the hull and the sky lightened from the east, a long grey bar, then a long pink one.

The helmsman went forward to the benches, his hands shaking, and sat down.

The hatch grating lifted.

Wystan came up the ladder on his hands and knees. He was black to the elbows. Tar in his hair, tar on his face where he had wiped it with the back of his wrist. A cut on the crooked thumb, bleeding into the tar. He knelt on the deck and breathed.

His face came round to her and held.

"The serving was sound," he said. "I went over every turn. Twice."

She stood with her hands at her sides.

"The chafe was at the eye, by the lower strap. The bolt had worked loose. Every swell was walking the nut back a thread. I ran it up hard and seized it with twine so it could not turn."

"I said the tackle."

"Yes." He looked at the thumb. "I would not have found the strap if you had not sent me for the tackle."

She looked at him a while.

"Show me."

• • •

The aft flat was four feet high. She went down the ladder and along a beam on her knees and her hands, in the dark, in the smell of tar and wet rope and the tide-mark of old bilge.

She could not walk in it.

She knelt at the strap and was still. The weight went out of her arms and shoulders. Down. Out through her hands. She felt it go, a little at a time, the way heat goes out of iron left in the rain.

Wystan knelt beside her and held the lamp up to the lower strap.

The strap. The bolt. The nut run up hard, and a seizing of tarred twine wound round it, neat as a bandage. Beside it on the pintle, a bright scar in the old metal where the bolt had walked.

She put her hand on the strap. Cold. Solid. It had not moved when the sea came.

"You heard it," she said.

"The swell lifted her and it rang. In the quiet." He glanced at the beam over their heads. "I heard you walking. Above. All night. I thought you were the watch."

"Yes."

"You walked past the hatch every ninth turn."

"Yes."

He did not ask why.

"Why did you go down when I said?"

His face in the lamplight was black and open and tired, and it held on her.

"You asked a man who knew rope to go and look at one," he said.

She turned that over twice, the way she turned a chain to find the weak link. She went round it as far as it would go.

It was whole. It was whole all the way round, and it told her nothing.

"Good," she said.

She went back along the beam on her knees. The ladder. The hatch. The deck and the cold air and the light.

The weight came back into her arms as she stood. She stood a moment longer than she needed to, feeling it come.

"Keep my lamp," she said.

He had climbed after her and stood a pace off with the work lamp in his hand. He looked at her, and his face held, and he nodded once.

He went forward to the cook's fire with tar on his hands. Hamund met him halfway and handed him a rag without a word, and the two of them stood looking at the thumb.

She walked aft to the helm. She put her hand flat on the tiller bar. It lay quiet. It had stopped moving some time ago.

Then she turned and walked the deck again, mainmast to taffrail, the chains swaying, the sun coming up on her face.
