# The Reading He Could No Longer Make Alone

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. The Storm That Walks Alias Chronicle LII, wave 18. Long Mask era, many years after the school's founding; Sephtis very elderly. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. Names Sephtis and his successor; no new named characters.*

---

The fever had passed through him three months earlier and left his left hand unsteady in a way that
hadn't gone away. He could still read a sky as well as he ever had. He could no longer hold the chalk
steady enough to mark the chart himself, and for a man who had spent sixty years insisting that the
mark on the chart was the promise, not the thought behind it, that was very nearly the same thing as
losing the craft outright.

He had said nothing about it to anyone but his successor, and only because she'd caught him failing
twice before he found the words to explain it.

"I can still see it," he told her, the morning the fleet needed a call he could not physically put
down on paper. "The storm's there. Two days out, veering north-northeast, weaker than it looks from
here. I know exactly what I'm reading. I just can't make my hand agree to write it down where anyone
else can trust it."

"Then I'll write it," she said. "You call it. I'll hold the chalk."

It was not, either of them understood immediately, the same as her making her own call and him
confirming it, the arrangement they'd fallen into naturally over the years since her training ended.
This was something closer to what it had been at the very start, decades earlier, except reversed —
his voice, her hand, the authority still entirely his even though the mark on the chart would be
unmistakably hers.

They did it in front of the fleet rather than privately, because Sephtis insisted on it.

"If I hide this," he said, "the first time someone finds out on their own, they stop trusting every
call I've ever made, wondering how long I've been failing quietly. If I show it plainly, they see
exactly what it costs me to keep doing this, and they trust the call more for it, not less."

He called the storm. She wrote it, in her own hand, under his direction, in full view of the crew. The
storm arrived exactly as he'd read it — two days out, veering north-northeast, weaker than the sky had
made it look.

"You didn't need my hand," he said afterward, quietly, to her alone. "You never really have. You just
let me believe you did a little longer than was strictly true, so I wouldn't have to feel this before
I had to."

"I let you believe it," she said, "because it was true for exactly as long as you needed it to be, and
not one day longer than that. That's not a kindness I'm ashamed of."
