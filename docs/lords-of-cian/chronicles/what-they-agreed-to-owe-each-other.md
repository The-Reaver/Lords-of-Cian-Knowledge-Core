# What They Agreed to Owe Each Other

*Locked canon, Batch 262, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1333`). The Lord of Embers Alias Chronicle LXXXVII, wave
29, closing the wave. Rebellion era, age 27, the Rolling Foundry Campaign (MCD-241). A labor/
economic-governance entry establishing a formal apprentice compensation structure. Not a territory
Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

It was an apprentice, not a journeyman, who finally raised the question nobody in the campaign's
leadership had thought to ask out loud — a girl three months into her training, blunt in the way
only someone new enough to not yet know which questions weren't supposed to be asked could manage.
"Everyone talks about what we're learning," she said, at an evening meal the senior smith's
successor happened to be sitting at. "Nobody's ever said what we're owed for the work we're already
doing while we learn it."

It hadn't been a deliberate oversight so much as an accident of how the method had grown — food,
shelter, and training had always been given freely, and in the campaign's early, smaller days that
had felt like more than enough. Thirty-one settlements and thousands of apprentices later, the
successor realized, sitting with the question, that "more than enough" had quietly become an
assumption nobody had ever actually tested against what the apprentices themselves needed.

"She's not wrong to ask," the successor told Kanja that night, laying the problem out plainly. "A
fourteen-year-old sending part of a wage home to a family that's struggling isn't the same as a
fourteen-year-old getting fed and taught. We built a teaching structure. We never built a labor
structure to sit alongside it."

They built one together over the following weeks, with apprentices themselves at the table for
every part of it rather than a rule handed down from above — a graduated stipend tied to skill
progression rather than raw hours worked, care built in for anyone supporting family beyond
themselves, and a standing council of apprentices with real say over how disputes about it got
settled, modeled loosely on the same guild-council structure that had already taken in the
Open-Forge Standard.

"You could have just decided this yourself," the girl said, once the structure was finished and
posted plainly where every apprentice could read it. "Nobody would have argued."

"I could have," Kanja said. "It would have been my idea of fair instead of yours. You're the one
actually owed something here. It only counts as fair if the people it's meant for helped build
it."

The structure held past the campaign's own eighteen months, carried onward the way the Open-Forge
Standard had been, into settlements that had never hosted a single forge from the tour itself —
proof, the successor said later, that the method had always been about more than iron, and had
finally caught up to saying so plainly.
