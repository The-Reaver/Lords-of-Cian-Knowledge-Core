*Archive fragment, present-day: "He thanked me tonight for something I did three years ago that he
has only just worked out I did on purpose. I told him it was nothing. It was not nothing. It was
simply not yet his to carry."*

I will allow myself, in this account only, a small departure from my own usual discipline, since
the rule I am about to describe is one I set for myself rather than one anyone else imposed: I do
not often let warmth into how I narrate this family's story. Clinical distance has served the
account better than sentiment would have, across every chapter I have written for Ezio and for
Lauris both. But there is a name that has always been an exception to my own rule, and this evening
is as good an occasion as any to say plainly why.

The dinner itself was unremarkable by design — the three of us, though I count myself present more
as recorder than participant on evenings like this, in a room with no strategic value and no
audience beyond the people already in it. Ezio had spent the better part of the meal in a lighter
mood than I had seen from him in some months, the specific ease of a man who has just noticed,
belatedly, a kindness someone extended him years earlier and never once called attention to.

"The incident at the eastern warehouse district," he said, some way into the evening, not quite a
question. "Three years ago. You positioned yourself between the engagement and my own vantage
point for the entire encounter. I assumed, at the time, that this was simply where the tactical
picture placed you."

"It was where the tactical picture placed me," Lauris said, which was, I noted, entirely true and
entirely not the whole of the answer.

"You arranged for the tactical picture to place you there."

She did not deny it. She has never, in my experience, found much use in denying a thing once
someone has correctly worked it out, whatever effort she went to originally to keep them from
working it out sooner. "Yes."

He considered this for a moment with the particular stillness I have come to recognize as him doing
careful arithmetic on something that matters to him more than his own face lets on. "You have been
doing this for some time."

"Since roughly the second month," she said. "It became habit before it became a decision I still
actively make. I do not think of it, most days, as anything other than where I am supposed to
stand."

What she did not say, and what I record here only because I believe the account is genuinely
incomplete without it, is the fuller shape of the arrangement: that her own conspicuous,
undisguised capability has spent years absorbing the crew's threat assessment and the enemy's
alike, so thoroughly that no engagement has yet forced Ezio to reveal what his own true capability
actually is. It is not concealment exactly, the way she has described it to me privately on other
occasions — it is redirection, a debt collected not from what she hides but from what she is so
visibly willing to be seen doing instead.

Ezio did not press her for the fuller accounting that evening, and she did not offer it. He said,
instead, after a silence that held nothing uncomfortable in it, only "Thank you," in a register I
have rarely heard him use — not the courtesy he extends as a matter of course, but something closer
to a debt he had only just realized he owed and intended, plainly, to keep owing rather than
attempt to immediately settle.

"It was nothing," she said.

It was not nothing. I have narrated enough of both their lives by now to say so with confidence,
and I will allow myself, this once, the small indulgence of saying it here rather than leaving it
only implied: whatever else either of them has built across the years of this crew's story, I do
not think either one of them has ever quite found the vocabulary for what they are, plainly, to
each other, and I have come to suspect neither one particularly minds the absence of the word, so
long as the thing itself continues, unremarked, exactly as it has for years.

My dear Ezio. I have never once had to explain to him why I write it that way. I suspect, reading
this account someday, he will understand it a little better than he did before this evening — and
I suspect, knowing her, that Lauris would prefer I leave it at that.
