# The Secret He Should Have Told Them

*[DRAFT — awaiting batch/rule-ID assignment]. Captain Alias Chronicle XLVI, wave 16. Rebellion-era
aftermath, later years. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose. No new
named characters beyond the already-locked Garren Hask and Corren Halst.*

---

Kanja had known for eleven days that a Directorate patrol pattern near the eastern anchorage had
shifted, and he had said nothing about it to anyone but Corren Halst, quietly, asking him to adjust
the watch rotation without explaining why. He had told himself it was a small thing, not yet
confirmed, not worth the weight it would put on people already stretched thin. On the twelfth day a
supply skiff ran the old route instead of the adjusted one, was spotted, and its two crew barely
made it back with an arrow through a sail and nothing worse — this time.

Garren Hask found him afterward going over the manifest a third time, checking names that were, in
fact, all still there.

"Corren says you knew," Hask said. It wasn't a question.

"I knew the pattern had shifted. I didn't know it well enough to be sure. I didn't want to spend
everyone's nerves on a guess."

"That's not your call to make alone. Not anymore." Hask said it without heat, which somehow made it
land harder than shouting would have. "You built this crew on us telling each other the truth even
when it's not finished cooking yet. Corren gets to decide if a half-sure guess is worth losing sleep
over. So do I. So does every hand on that skiff. You deciding for us what we can carry — that's not
protection. We've been carrying worse than uncertainty since before you knew our names."

Kanja set the manifest down. "I was wrong to keep it. I wasn't trying to manage you. I was trying to
spare people something, and I told myself that made it different from just not trusting you with it.
It wasn't different."

"No. It wasn't." Hask sat across from him, some of the edge going out of his voice. "You've built
half of what holds this crew together on admitting when you're wrong the second you know it. Costs
you nothing to admit this one too."

He called the senior hands together that evening — Halst, Hask, a dozen others — and told them
plainly: the pattern shift, how long he'd sat on it, and why he'd been wrong to. Nobody made it easy
for him, and nobody made it harder than it needed to be either. By the end of it, a standing rule
existed that hadn't before: anything he knew that could change how a watch was stood got said aloud,
confirmed or not, finished or not, the moment he knew it. Not because the crew doubted his judgment
about danger. Because they'd stopped letting him carry alone what was never only his to carry.

"Half-sure is still worth more to us than a surprise," Halst told him, packing up the map table.
"Especially from you. People trust what you tell them precisely because you don't dress it up. Don't
start deciding for us which parts we're allowed to hear plain."
