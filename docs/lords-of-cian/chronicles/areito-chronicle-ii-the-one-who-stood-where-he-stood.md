# Areíto Chronicle II: The One Who Stood Where He Stood

*DRAFT -- pending Abad's approval, not yet locked canon. Batch 93 candidate (`MCD-356`
candidate). Second entry in Areíto's own Chronicles, its protagonist Kwame Ade (`PH2-004`), not a
Kanja Chronicle. Kanja appears only as a guest, present but granted no command, no intervention, and
no resolution credit, matching the established convention. Narration is close-third on Kwame Ade
throughout, not Onyx of Oblivion. Directly pays off the explicit, unresolved hook left at the close
of Areíto Chronicle I (`MCD-339`): Kanja's parting line there, referencing `PH2-004`'s own stated
vulnerability -- "the one who'll actually land a blow on you someday...is someone who's just as
certain as you are." Introduces one new named character, Adeyemi -- collision-checked against the
full live ledger (zero prior hits), distinct from the already-locked Xaragua supporting-cast figures
Kwabena (`PH2-014`) and Kwaku (`PH2-016`), neither of whom this reuses or resembles.*

---

He came seven years after the barbershop, and he came alone, and he came without anything sharper
than his own two hands, which was how Kwame Ade knew before the man had said a single word that
this one was different from the three who'd come before him.

"Adeyemi." Kwame Ade said the name the way a man says a word he has been waiting a long time to use
again. "I heard you were out."

"Two years now." Adeyemi stood in the doorway of the print shop the way he'd once stood in the
doorway of a cell they'd shared for the better part of four, unhurried, unarmed, entirely certain of
his welcome to take up exactly as much space as he intended to take. "Took me most of that to decide
whether I was coming here to talk or to finish something. I still don't fully know which this is."

"Sit anyway."

"I'd rather stand. Man makes better decisions on his feet."

Kwame Ade set down the block he'd been setting type into and gave him the whole of his attention,
which he gave to very few people anymore. "You were the only one in that cell who came out believing
the exact same thing I did. Word for word, near enough. That the doubt in us was the enemy, and a
man who killed it all the way through would never be moved again by anything smaller than his own
certainty."

"I still believe that. Every syllable of it." Adeyemi's voice didn't rise. It never had, even then.
"Which is why I can't make peace with what you did with it. You took the exact thing we built
together in that cell — the same fire, the same four years, the same nights neither of us slept
because we were busy becoming men who couldn't be broken again — and you spent it on a city full of
strangers instead of the six men who forged it with you. You called that growth. I've had two years
outside to turn it over, and I still call it the same thing I called it the week you left: a
betrayal wearing the exact clothes conviction wears, so close to the real thing that even you
couldn't tell the difference."

"Everyone else who came to say that brought help."

"Everyone else who came doubted, somewhere underneath it, whether they had the right to be the one
who said it. That's why you stopped them without lifting a hand — they were only half inside their
own reasons. I'm not doubting anything. I built the same wall you built, in the same room, out of
the same stone. I've had seven years to make sure not one brick of it moved." Adeyemi's hands, still
open, still empty, came up level with his own chest — not a fighter's stance, something closer to a
man showing another man exactly what he was about to do before he did it. "I think that's the only
thing in this world that can still touch you. I think I might be it. I came to find out, and I
didn't see any honest way to find out except to try."

The stranger was near the back of the shop, had been for most of the hour, sorting a stack of
finished handbills into a crate no one had asked him to fill, present the whole while without once
inserting himself into the space between the two men. He did not move now either.

Adeyemi crossed the room without hurry and threw one blow — not wide, not honest-and-wild the way
the barbershop man's had been seven years before, but exact, unhesitating, thrown by a hand that had
spent seven years deciding it was allowed to throw it.

It landed.

Kwame Ade went back a full step, a hand to his own ribs, blood already finding its way between his
fingers where nothing had ever drawn blood from him before, and for the first time in longer than he
could measure, felt something in his own certainty answer with something that was not simply
standing there unmoved.

Adeyemi didn't swing again. He stood with his fist still half-raised, breathing hard, staring at the
blood on Kwame Ade's hand with an expression that had nothing of triumph in it at all.

"There it is," Kwame Ade said, quietly, still upright, still holding his own ribs. "You were right.
About all of it, maybe. I don't know yet. I'll have years to work that out, same as you had years to
work out the question you just answered." He looked at the raised fist, still hanging in the air
between them. "Finish it, if that's what seven years decided."

Adeyemi lowered the fist slowly, like a man setting down something heavier than his own arm had told
him it was. "I came believing I'd know, the moment it landed, whether I meant to go all the way
through with it. I don't. That's new. I haven't been unsure of anything in seven years, and I'm
unsure of this, standing right here with the blood already drawn." He stepped back toward the
doorway. "Maybe that means the wall in me isn't finished being built after all. Maybe it means some
part of what you did in that room wasn't only betrayal. I don't know which yet, and I'm not going to
lie to you and pretend the not-knowing feels like anything but its own kind of falling."

"Then go find out. Same as I did."

"I might come back once I have." Adeyemi paused at the door, not looking back. "I might not need
to."

He was gone before the shop's bell had finished settling on its hook.

The stranger crossed the room only once the door had shut, and only to press a folded length of
clean cloth into Kwame Ade's hand — no words, no question of whether it was wanted, gone again to
the back of the shop before Kwame Ade had finished pressing it to the wound.

"You could have stopped that," Kwame Ade said, holding the cloth against his own side.

"I could have. You didn't need me to." The stranger didn't look up from the handbills. "You needed
to know if it was true. Now you do."

Kwame Ade looked down at the blood already slowing beneath the cloth — the first mark anyone had
ever left on him that he hadn't chosen to let land — and found, underneath the pain, something he
hadn't expected to feel at all: not fear, not even the particular grief he'd carried for seven years
about the six men that cell had forged him alongside. Something closer to relief, that the wall he'd
built himself into had turned out, after everything, to still have one true door in it.
