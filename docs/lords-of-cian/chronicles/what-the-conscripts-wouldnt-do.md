# What the Conscripts Wouldn't Do

*Locked canon, Batch 106, 2026-09-10 (`MCD-367`). Bane Alias Chronicle III. Rebellion era,
within the "Bane" window, a skirmish distinct from the Black Trench and from "The Trap Built From
His Own Shape" — not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material: not part of the already-locked
Twenty-Two Victories list. Narrated in neutral third-person prose, no Onyx coda, consistent with the
pre-`VB-026` early-Rebellion state. No new named characters — the conscript, the sergeant, and the
line are all unnamed.*

---

He was nineteen, which made him the same age as the man they'd sent his line to hold against,
though nobody in the unit had put those two numbers next to each other out loud, because saying it
made something true that was easier to carry unsaid.

His sergeant had fought at the Black Trench and survived it — one of the nine hundred or so who had,
depending which casualty count you believed, and the sergeant believed none of them, because he'd
counted the dead with his own hands afterward and the number never came out the same way twice. He
did not talk about it. He talked instead, the night before, about the line itself: a shallow
causeway crossing a salt flat, no cover, no fallback, the kind of ground a commander only chooses
when he has decided the men holding it are worth less than the two hours their deaths would buy. Nine
hundred conscripts, most of them younger than the sergeant, spread thin across half a mile of nothing,
waiting for a column that Directorate intelligence said Bane was leading in person.

"You don't fight him," the sergeant said, checking the same buckle on the same strap for the fourth
time in an hour. "Nobody's fighting him tonight. We're fighting the two hours it takes him to walk
through us to whoever he's actually coming for. That's the whole job. Buy two hours. Don't be a hero.
Heroes are how you get counted twice — once for the fight and once for the funeral."

The boy did not ask what happened to the men who had tried to be heroes at the Black Trench, because
he already understood, from the way the sergeant's hands kept finding that same buckle, that the
answer was not a number he wanted spoken aloud the night before his own line.

Word came down the causeway before the column itself did — not a runner, not a signal flare, just a
kind of silence that moved faster than either, passing man to man along the shallow ditch they'd dug
themselves into, each one going quiet a few seconds after the man beside him. By the time it reached
the boy it had no content left, only weight: something was coming, and the something already knew
how this ended, and everyone ahead of him down the line had somehow understood that at the same
moment, without a word being exchanged that he'd actually heard.

He saw him only once, and only at distance — a shape at the head of a column still a quarter mile
off, walking rather than marching, unhurried in a way that read wrong against nine hundred men lying
flat in a ditch with everything they owned aimed down the causeway at him. The boy would spend the
rest of his life unable to describe what made that unhurried walk different from ordinary confidence,
only that it was, and that the difference reached him across a quarter mile of open salt flat before
a single shot had been fired in either direction.

To his left, a man three positions down stood up out of the ditch, set his weapon on the ground in
front of him, and started walking backward, away from the line, not fast, not panicked, just done —
the way a man walks away from a job he's decided, calmly and completely, he is no longer willing to
do. No one stopped him. The sergeant, two positions further down, watched it happen and said nothing,
and that silence told the boy more than any order could have: the sergeant wasn't holding the line
together through discipline anymore. He was watching to see how much of it there was left to hold.

By the time the column closed to within a hundred yards, perhaps a third of the causeway's defenders
had done the same thing — weapons down, walking back across the flat in a straggling, unashamed
retreat that no officer tried to punish, because the officers were doing arithmetic of their own and
coming to the same number. The boy stayed. He would tell people afterward that he stayed out of fear
of moving as much as fear of staying, which was probably true, and probably not the whole of it.

The column never reached the causeway's midpoint. Somewhere short of it, out of the boy's hearing, an
order came down a different channel entirely — the commander who'd chosen this ground had apparently
found, in the two hours since dusk, a reason to no longer need it held, and the whole engagement
simply stopped being an engagement, dissolving into men on both sides walking away from a fight that
had already decided itself before either line came close enough to touch.

The boy never learned what the actual reason was. He suspected, without ever confirming it, that
there hadn't been one — that whatever the sergeant's two hours were supposed to buy had already been
bought or lost somewhere else entirely, and this stretch of salt flat had simply been a number on a
map that stopped mattering. What he remembered, decades later, telling it to men too young to
remember the war themselves, was not the column, or the order, or even the walk. It was the third of
the line that had stood up and set their weapons down before a shot was fired — not broken men, not
cowards, but men who had looked down a quarter mile of open ground and understood, all at once and
without being told, that the fight was already over and they simply hadn't been informed of the
result yet.

"We didn't lose our nerve," he'd say, when people used that phrase for it, because they always did.
"We ran out of a fight that wasn't there to begin with. There's a difference, even if nobody who
wasn't on that flat ever believes it."
