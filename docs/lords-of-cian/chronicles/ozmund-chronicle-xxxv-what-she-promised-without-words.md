# What She Promised Without Words

*Locked canon, Batch 305, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1764`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XXXV — closing entry of the Val Mirel strand's second wave. Set strictly pre-Book-1,
years before the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`) — Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, referenced but not
present in this entry. Ozmund is roughly twenty-seven, immediately following the year-long absence of

This is the last story about his mother the General ever told me, and though he never said so outright,
I've always believed he meant it that way — that of everything he could have chosen to leave me with, he
chose to end on this one, and that the choosing was deliberate. I have tried, writing it down now, to
give it back exactly the way he gave it to me, because I don't think I could improve on it if I tried, and
I have no wish to.

She came back in early autumn, not long after the dispatch with its four unexplained words, and true to
every homecoming he'd ever described to me, she gave no warning of it at all. He found her the way he'd
found her that first morning at nine years old — except this time it was he who was already standing in
the yard, alone, holding the Stone Count in the gray light the way he had every dawn of the year just
passed, and it was she who arrived to find him already there, already still, a habit that had stopped
being a lesson somewhere in the months of her absence and become, simply, who he was.

He told me he heard her before he saw her — not a footstep, he was careful to say, she never once
announced herself with something as ordinary as a footstep in all the years he knew her, but some
smaller shift in the air of the yard that eleven months of standing in that exact spot every morning had
taught him to notice the way another man might notice a change in the wind. He didn't break the count. He
told me that was the first thing he was proud of, telling it to me — that a year of doubt hadn't taught
him to abandon the discipline the moment its reason for existing walked back into the yard. It had taught
him, instead, to trust that she would wait for him to finish, the way she always had.

She did. He finished the count in full, the way she'd have wanted, and only then did he turn to face her.

What happened next, he told me, was the part he had never fully managed to describe to anyone else
before he gave it to me, and I don't think I can improve on his own words for it, so I'll give them back
as near to whole as memory allows. She said nothing. She crossed the yard to him — not quickly, not
slowly, at exactly the pace he said she moved through everything — and she placed her open palm flat
against the center of his chest, over his sternum, and held it there.

It was, he told me, almost precisely the gesture she had given him at nine years old, waking him with a
hand on his shoulder light enough that he'd never once been frightened by it. Almost, but not quite — that
first gesture had been a greeting, a hand extended to wake a sleeping boy into a lesson. This one was
something else. She held her palm against him long enough that he could feel his own pulse answer hers
through it, and long enough that he understood, without a single word passing between them, that this
was not a greeting at all. It was an answer to a question he had never once asked her aloud, not in any
of the four letters he'd sent that year, not on the terrace years before when she'd told him the truth
about why she stayed away. The question he had carried, unspoken, through every one of those dispatches
written in someone else's hand: whether, if it ever truly mattered, she would come.

She held her palm there until he understood the answer without needing her to say it. Then she took her
hand away, and only then did she speak — not to explain the gesture, he told me, because she never once
explained it, that year or in any year after. She spoke only to tell him, plainly, that she was tired,
and that she would take the Stone Count with him again in the morning, the way she always had, as if no
year had passed between one dawn and the next at all.

He told me it became, after that morning, the whole of how she ever came home to him again — no words
first, not once, not for the rest of the years he had her. A hand against his chest, held until his own
pulse answered it, and only then whatever ordinary thing came after. He said he came to understand it as
the truest promise she was capable of making him, precisely because she made it with the one part of
herself the Order and the war and every absence between them had never once managed to touch: not her
voice, which duty had claimed too many times to fully trust, but her hand, offered freely, exactly the
same way, every single time she came back through that gate.

"She never told me in words that she would always come when it mattered," he said to me, the last time
he ever spoke of her at all. "She showed me. Every time. With the same hand, held the same way, until I
stopped needing the words at all."
