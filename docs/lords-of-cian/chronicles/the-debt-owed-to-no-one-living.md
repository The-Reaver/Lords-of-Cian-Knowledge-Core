# The Debt Owed to No One Living

*Locked canon, Batch 216, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-945`). The Trench Monarch Alias Chronicle LII, wave 18. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The dredge worker had died the ordinary way most of them did, a rope giving way under a load it should
have held, and left behind exactly two things worth fighting over: a small plot of dry ground above
the flood line, and eleven days of back pay a tally sheet already confirmed he was owed. Neither
amount was large. The fight over them was.

His elder daughter had cared for him through the last two years of failing health and believed the
plot and the pay both belonged to her, plainly, for reasons nobody disputed. His younger brother
believed the same, on the grounds that he'd helped raise the dead man's own children when their mother
died young, and that family debts didn't stop mattering just because the man who'd have settled them
was gone. Neither of them had asked Kanja to get involved. A neighbor had, mostly out of exhaustion at
watching two people she liked tear at each other over a dead man's leftovers.

It wasn't a wage dispute. There was no owner lying, no district shorting anyone, no reputation for
honesty to defend against an outside threat. It was two people who'd both genuinely loved the same man,
each convinced the other had forgotten what that love was supposed to mean in practice.

Kanja didn't bring the tally method to it, not really — there was no figure in dispute, nothing to
verify against a manifest. What he did was closer to what he'd watched the old dredge hand at Ferrow
Cut do for decades without ever needing a ledger for it: he sat with each of them separately first,
said almost nothing, and let them say everything they hadn't said to each other yet.

The daughter, it turned out, wasn't afraid of losing the plot. She was afraid the years she'd spent
caring for her father would be treated as owed rather than given — as though love only counted if it
came with a deed attached. The brother wasn't afraid of losing the back pay. He was afraid that being
cut out of the settlement meant being told, after everything, that he'd never really been family at
all.

Kanja never suggested a split. He simply told each of them, plainly, what the other had actually said
to him, without editing it into a stronger or weaker claim than it was. It took most of a day. By the
evening the daughter had the plot, freely offered by her uncle before Kanja ever proposed it, and the
back pay went toward finishing a modest stone marker both of them agreed on together.

"I didn't do anything you couldn't have done yourselves," Kanja told them afterward. "I just wasn't
either of you, so it was easier to hear it from me first."

Corren Halst, hearing the full account later, pointed out that nothing about it would ever make it
into a pamphlet — no owner shamed, no wage recovered from anyone but the dead man's own estate,
nothing that looked like the reputation at all.

"Most of what actually holds a family or a district together isn't the part anyone writes down,"
Kanja said. "I learn that every time I end up somewhere I didn't expect to be needed."
