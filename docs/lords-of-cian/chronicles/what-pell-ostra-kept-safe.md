# What Pell Ostra Kept Safe

Years before she was packing the accelerant charges that would open the Black Trench's ravine or
scaling the Scrip-Forge stockpile at Ash-Wharf, Pell Ostra talked to chemicals the way other people
talked to friends, and had never once been trusted with anything more important than a shipment of
lamp oil.

The raid on the Marrow Landing counting-house wasn't supposed to reach the records room. It reached
it anyway, a Compliance detail moving faster than anyone had accounted for, straight toward six
months of independently verified tally books that represented the only proof the wage corrections
had ever happened at all.

Pell Ostra was in the records room when the detail broke through the outer door, alone, with nothing
in her hands but the lamp-oil satchel she'd been carrying for an entirely unrelated reason.

She didn't run. She thought about the oil, and the room's single narrow window, and the six months of
ledgers stacked exactly where anyone searching would look first, and did something nobody had asked
her to do and nobody would have thought to ask a lamp-oil courier to attempt.

By the time the detail reached the records room, the ledgers were gone through the window, passed
hand to hand down a chain of workers she'd never met and would never fully identify afterward, and
the room itself was burning just enough — oil on the outer shelving alone, nothing near the window,
nothing that would spread past what she'd already decided it should reach — to make the search look
finished before it had properly started.

"You set a fire in a room full of the only proof we had," Kanja said afterward, when the ledgers were
accounted for and the smoke had cleared enough to assess what was left. "That could have gone
considerably worse."

"I know exactly how oil burns. I've known since I was nine years old." Pell Ostra said it without
apology, already assessing the damage with the same flat attention she'd used to set it. "I didn't
burn what mattered. I burned what made the search look finished. There's a difference, if you
understand fire instead of just being afraid of it."

"Most people would have run."

"Most people don't know what I know about a room this size and this much oil." She looked at him
directly for the first time since he'd arrived. "I've never been trusted with anything before today.
I'd like to keep being trusted with something, if the ledgers are worth what I think they are."

They were. It would be years before her talent for reading exactly what a fire, a charge, or a
structure could be made to do found its full expression at Ash-Wharf and the Black Trench, but the
instinct — reading what a room's own materials could be turned into, rather than fearing them — was
already fully formed the day she chose to burn a records room to save it.
