# The Trap Built From His Own Shape

*Locked canon, Batch 106, 2026-09-10 (`MCD-366`). Bane Alias Chronicle II. Rebellion era,
several months after the Battle of the Black Trench (`MCD-232`), still within the "Bane" window —
not a territory Chronicle. New standalone material: not part of the already-locked Twenty-Two
Victories list. Narrated in neutral third-person prose, no Onyx coda, consistent with the
pre-`VB-026` early-Rebellion state. No new named characters — the Directorate officer is unnamed,
matching the precedent set by the Undersecretary in "The Pivotal Piece."*

---

The officer who built the trap had survived the Black Trench by being three ravines away when the
walls came down, which was either luck or the only kind of competence the Directorate still trusted
after losing eleven Suppression companies to a single man in four years. He had spent the months
since studying the engagement the way a locksmith studies a lock he's failed to pick: not the battle
itself, which was chaos and fog and a body count nobody wanted read aloud twice, but the *shape* of
it. The collapsed exits. The chosen ground. The nine hours he'd stayed inside a sealed space with an
enemy he could have simply avoided.

His conclusion, delivered to three separate review boards before anyone above him would fund it: Bane
did not fight to win space. He fought to remove it — from himself and from everyone else in the
engagement, all at once, until the only variable left standing was which side could survive without
room to maneuver. You could not out-maneuver a man who had already decided maneuvering was over. You
could only build the same trap faster than he could, and detonate it first.

So he built one. A dry wash between two ridgelines in the Korren foothills, narrow enough to funnel a
column, timbered along both slopes with enough Dead Drakma charge to bring down forty feet of rock on
either side inside eleven seconds of a lever pull — his own hand on that lever, not a subordinate's,
because he had watched what happened to men who trusted someone else with the moment that mattered.
He seeded the approach with exactly the kind of intelligence Bane's people were known to chase:
a supply convoy, real enough to bleed for, routed through the wash at an hour calculated to put the
column's center mass under the heaviest charge the instant the lever came down.

It should have worked on the shape of the man the reports described. It did not work on the man.

He came through the wash forty minutes before the convoy's scheduled hour, alone, on foot, walking
the center line at a pace that matched nothing in any of the after-action timing tables the officer
had built the whole operation around. He did not scout the ridgelines. He did not send anyone ahead
to draw fire. He simply walked in, stopped roughly at the wash's narrowest point — near enough to the
charge lines that the officer's hand found the lever without being told to — and looked up at the
slope where the officer was lying in wait, as if he had been handed the coordinates in advance and
was only now confirming them against what he could see with his own eyes.

"You moved the timber two days ago," he said. Not a question. Just loud enough to carry up the slope
without shouting. "Fresh cut smells different than old cut, even under this much dust. I noticed it
from the ridge road an hour before I came down."

The officer's hand did not leave the lever. It also did not pull it. He would say later — to the two
review boards that survived long enough to hear the account, and never again after that — that he had
run this exact moment in his head perhaps two hundred times in the preceding weeks, had war-gamed
every version of it he could construct, and in every single one of those two hundred versions the
lever came down and the wash closed and the account ended in a body count instead of a conversation.
He had built the mechanism specifically so that hesitation was impossible — eleven seconds, one motion,
no reconsideration built into the design on purpose. And he found, with the actual lever actually
under his actual hand, that he was not deciding anything at all. The decision had already been made
for him, somewhere he hadn't been present for, and what he was doing now was simply the part where he
found out what it was.

"You built this to be my trap," Bane said, still looking up at the slope, still not moving toward
cover, still not treating the eleven seconds as a countdown he needed to survive. "That's the part you
got right. You just built it backward. A trap only works on someone who doesn't know it's there. I've
known it was there since the fresh-cut timber. The only thing that was ever actually undecided here
was whether you'd pull it anyway, knowing that. And I think you already know you're not going to."

The lever stayed where it was. Forty feet of rock stayed where it was. The convoy, when it came
through the wash forty minutes later exactly on schedule, drove past an abandoned ambush position and
an officer sitting against the ridge rock with his hands empty, and past no one at all where the enemy
column was supposed to be, because there had never been a column — only one man, walking in ahead of
his own reputation to confirm, in person, that the reputation had already done the work the charges
were built to do.

The report the officer filed afterward was never fully declassified. The line from it that did reach
the Brand-Line, copied hand to hand through channels no Directorate audit ever traced back to its
source, read simply: *He wasn't disarming the trap. He was informing me I'd already decided not to use
it.*
