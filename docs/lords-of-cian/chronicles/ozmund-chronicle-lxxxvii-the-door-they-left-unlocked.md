# The Door They Left Unlocked

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1816`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXVII, House politics strand wave 3, eighth entry.*

---

I found Wendell Rowe the way I found most of the smaller sources for these stories — by asking
whoever I'd already spoken to whether they knew of anyone else. Bevin gave me his name, half
apologetically, saying he wasn't sure the old man would have much worth telling. Rowe, by then long
retired from House Verehimu's service and living modestly on a pension in a river town some days
south of Karkosa, proved him wrong within the first quarter hour.

He'd kept the night watch at House Verehimu for the better part of thirty years, the kind of duty,
he told me, that teaches a man more about a household's true character than any daylight service
ever could, because the things people do when they believe no one senior is awake to see them tend to
be a good deal more honest than the things they do in company. Rowe said he'd walked those halls
through three different eras of the House's fortunes, and that of everything he'd observed in thirty
years, the thing he remembered most clearly wasn't any single event but a habit — his own habit,
formed gradually enough that he couldn't tell me exactly when it started, of leaving one particular
side door, near the old kitchen stair, unlocked through the small hours rather than bolted the way
every other watchman's instructions required.

He explained it plainly, once I asked. Ozmund, as a young man, had taken to walking at odd hours —
Bevin's own story, of the cold ovens and the sleepless boy, was only the first of what turned out to
be a long-running habit, one Rowe said he'd noticed within his first year on the night watch and
never stopped noticing after. The General never explained the walking to anyone, so far as Rowe knew,
and Rowe never asked, which he considered, telling it to me, the most important part of the whole
arrangement. What he did instead, once he'd worked out the pattern well enough to trust it, was
simply stop locking that one door at the hours he judged Ozmund most likely to want it — not out of
any instruction given, which would have meant someone above him had to know and approve the change,
but out of his own quiet, unauthorized judgment that a young man who clearly needed to walk shouldn't
have to explain himself to a locked door in his own family's house at two in the morning.

He told me he'd never once mentioned the arrangement to Ozmund directly, and as far as he knew,
Ozmund had never once asked why that particular door was reliably open when every other exit from the
house was properly secured. Rowe said he suspected the General had worked it out for himself easily
enough, being the kind of man he was, and had simply extended him the same courtesy in return — never
acknowledging it, never thanking him for it in any way that would have turned an unspoken kindness
into a debt either of them had to carry formally. It went on for the better part of fifteen years,
Rowe told me, an arrangement maintained entirely through two men's mutual, unstated agreement not to
discuss it, until Rowe's own retirement finally ended it without either of them ever having spoken a
single word about why the door behaved the way it did.

What struck Rowe most, he said, wasn't the arrangement itself, which he described almost sheepishly,
as though thirty years hadn't been quite long enough to convince him it wasn't some minor breach of
duty. It was a single evening, perhaps eight years into the habit, when he'd been caught in the act
of leaving the latch undone by another senior member of the household staff, a woman who could easily
have reported an unlocked door as exactly the security lapse it technically was. Rowe said he'd
braced for trouble and instead watched the woman glance at the door, glance at him, and simply nod
once and walk on without a word — because she, too, it turned out, had noticed the same pattern over
the same years, and had long since drawn the same conclusion Rowe had: that a household this size
kept a great many small secrets on behalf of people it trusted, and that this was, by any measure
Rowe could think to apply, among the least costly and best-earned of them.

He told me, near the end of our conversation, that he'd thought more than once, in the years since,
about what it meant that an entire household had come, gradually and without any single person
deciding it, to extend a young man that particular kind of unspoken trust — not obedience, which any
household gives its ranking family by custom, but the quieter, harder-won trust of people choosing,
individually and without instruction, to make his life a little easier in ways no one above them had
asked for and no one below them would ever be thanked for. He said he still thought, all these years
later, that it was the finest thing he'd ever done in thirty years of service, and that he'd have done
it again in a heartbeat, door or no door, if the man on the other side of it had ever needed it kept
open.
