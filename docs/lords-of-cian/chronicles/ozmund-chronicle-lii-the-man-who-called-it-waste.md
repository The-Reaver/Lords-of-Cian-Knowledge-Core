# The Man Who Called It Waste

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1781`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series, Chronicle LII, Draconis strand wave 3.*

---

The General told me most men who questioned Draconis's devotion to him did it the way the guardhouse had, years before — quietly, among themselves, in rooms he was never meant to overhear. This was the one time, he told me, that someone said it to Draconis's face, in front of him, and did not have the sense to lower his voice while doing it.

The man was a garrison commander up from the coast, attached for a season to a joint muster the two Houses had agreed to run against a shared stretch of border trouble, senior enough in his own service to think his rank traveled with him into another House's yard. He'd watched Draconis for the better part of a week by then — watched him turn down a forward command he was plainly suited for so he could stay attached to the inner escort instead, watched him fuss, in the commander's own word for it, over a young lord's seating and sightlines at a war council the way no soldier of his caliber ought to trouble himself over anything. He said it over supper, in front of enough officers that it couldn't be unsaid afterward, the way the guardhouse's own senior man had said his piece years before — that a soldier of Draconis's gifts spending himself guarding a boy who'd never drawn a blade in anger was the plainest waste of a career he'd watched in twenty years of service, and that he wondered, loudly enough to be heard three tables down, what exactly a young lord did to buy that kind of devotion from a man who by rights should have known better.

The General was not meant to hear it. He told me he'd been passing the mess hall on some unrelated errand, close enough by accident to catch the shape of it through the open door, and that he'd stopped there in the corridor rather than either walking in or walking on, because something in him needed to hear how Draconis would answer a question that came close enough to the truth to frighten him without ever actually touching it.

Draconis didn't raise his voice. The General was particular about telling me that, more than once — that whatever answer came, it came level and unhurried, the same register the man used for giving a roster or correcting a recruit's stance, as though the question itself had cost him nothing to hear. He said that he had spent the better part of his career learning exactly what a man was worth by watching what he did when no one was measuring him for it, and that by that measure the boy in question had never once, in all the years Draconis had known him, given him cause to regret a single hour spent in his service. He said the commander was welcome to call that waste if he liked; he had called it something else himself for twenty years and saw no reason to trade the word now on another man's say-so. He said it, the General told me, the way a man states a fact about the weather — not defensive, not proud, simply correcting a figure that had been entered wrong.

The commander pushed once more, the General said, asking outright whether Draconis had ever considered that a clever enough young lord might know exactly how to keep a good soldier tethered to him for reasons that had nothing to do with the soldier's own judgment. Draconis let a silence sit long enough that the whole table felt it before he answered that one, and when he did, the General told me, it was the closest to sharp he ever heard the man get in that entire exchange: that he had buried enough men who'd followed worse commanders for worse reasons to know the difference between a leash and a choice, and that he'd made his own thirty years ago and saw no profit in explaining it twice to a stranger passing through for a season.

The General told me he stood in that corridor a long while after the talk at the table moved on to something else entirely, and that what stayed with him wasn't the insult, which he said he'd have shrugged off easily enough on his own account. It was the plain, uncomfortable accuracy buried inside a question asked in bad faith — that a stranger with no reason to look closely had seen, in a week, the exact shape of the thing the General himself had spent decades not looking at directly: a devotion built on something the man giving it didn't fully understand, offered to someone who had never once corrected the misunderstanding. He told me he'd wanted, standing there, to walk into that hall and put the truth on the table himself, if only to prove the commander wrong about the leash. He didn't. He told me the reason he gave himself that night was the same reason he always gave himself, and that for the first time it sounded, even to him, less like an answer and more like a habit he'd stopped examining.
