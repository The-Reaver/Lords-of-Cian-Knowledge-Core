# Daba Chronicle XLVIII: The Bet Daba Wasn't Supposed to Win

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1618`). Entry 48 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. A deliberate entry
of warmth and humor -- proof, against the doctrine-over-mass gravity running through most of the
series, that 1804 is a home with real affection and lightness in it, not only a grim instrument.
Reuses Mika and Perrin (`MCD-1616`/`1617`); introduces Tessin, a newly recruited junior member,
collision-checked clean. No other new named characters. Abad's approval: "rate 50 Chronicles in
batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

The wager, as Tessin explained it to anyone who would listen, was simple: she would get a real laugh
out of Daba -- not the short exhale through the nose that passed for one, not the almost-smile he
allowed when a plan went well, but an actual, audible, undignified laugh -- before the next supply
cycle turned over. Stakes were first pick of the good blankets for a month, an old cell tradition
nobody could name the origin of and everyone honored anyway, and by the second week of trying, six
different people had quietly put something into the pool against her.

She was thorough about it, if nothing else. She tried the obvious jokes first and watched them die on
contact. She tried mangling tactical terminology on purpose, calling a chokepoint a "throat-spot" with
a perfectly straight face until Perrin corrected her twice before catching on. She tried a truly
committed pratfall down a set of steps that were not, in fact, slippery, timed for a moment when Daba
happened to be watching, and received in return only a raised eyebrow and the dry observation that she
had just demonstrated, with unfortunate clarity, exactly the kind of predictable movement a Trust
scout would learn to anticipate within a week. Mika, watching this exchange from across the room, did
not even try to hide that she was laughing at Tessin instead.

By the last day of the cycle, Tessin had given it up as a lost cause and was simply doing her assigned
work, which was counting and recounting a delivery of dried fish against a tally that refused to match
no matter how many times she ran it, muttering numbers under her breath with the particular fury of
someone who has decided the fish themselves are conspiring against her personally. Daba, passing
through to check the store himself, watched her lose count for the fourth time, restart from one, lose
it again at almost the exact same point, and declare to the room in general and no one in particular
that she was fairly sure one specific fish was actively moving itself between piles out of spite.

He laughed.

It came out of him without warning -- a real laugh, sudden and full and entirely undignified, gone as
quickly as it arrived but unmistakably, irretrievably real, and the room went so quiet in the wake of
it that Tessin actually turned around to see what had happened, as though something might be broken.

"That," Mika announced, into the silence, "counts."

"That wasn't even a joke," Tessin protested. "I was serious. I think the fish actually is moving."

"Doesn't matter," Perrin said, already counting coin out of the pool with the resigned efficiency of a
man who had bet against her and lost. "The wager never said it had to be your joke that did it. It just
said you had to get the laugh."

Daba, recovering himself, found he did not particularly mind having been outmaneuvered by a technicality
he hadn't known he'd agreed to. "For what it's worth," he told Tessin, "the fish isn't moving. You keep
starting your count from a different sack each time. It's not the fish that's inconsistent."

"That's worse," Tessin said. "That means I've just been losing to myself for four days."

"Welcome to the work," Daba said, and something in the flatness of his own delivery set the room off a
second time, Mika laughing outright now, Perrin shaking his head, Tessin throwing up her hands in a
kind of triumphant defeat and declaring that she'd take the blankets regardless of whether the joke had
technically been intentional.

She got the blankets. Nobody argued the point. And for the rest of that cycle, whenever a delivery
tally came up short, someone in the store would mutter, entirely deadpan, that the fish was moving
again, and Daba -- who had spent the better part of his life keeping a face steady enough that no
enemy could read it, who had built an entire doctrine on never once being predictable -- found that
this, at least, reliably got him every single time.
