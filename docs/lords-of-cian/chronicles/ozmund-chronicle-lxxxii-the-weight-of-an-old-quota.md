# The Weight of an Old Quota

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1811`). Ozmund Verehimu Character Chronicle series,
Chronicle LXXXII, House politics strand wave 3, third entry.*

---

The General told me this one carefully, the way he told anything involving numbers, as if he
suspected — correctly, in my case — that his listener's attention would need tending along the way.
He needn't have worried. It was, once I understood what he was actually describing, one of the
quieter demonstrations of the man I ever heard from him, precisely because nothing about it looked
like a demonstration of anything at all.

House Verehimu's grain tithe, the General explained, had been set by formula rather than by
individual assessment for nearly sixty years — a scheme his own grandfather had introduced, in its
time a genuine improvement over the arbitrary, case-by-case levies it replaced, calculated as a flat
percentage of each holding's declared acreage rather than its actual yield. It was meant to be fair
in the way rules are meant to be fair: the same percentage, applied evenly, regardless of who held
the land. What his grandfather hadn't accounted for, and what sixty years of quiet drift had made
considerably worse, was that acreage and yield had stopped tracking each other cleanly a long time
before Ozmund ever inherited responsibility for the ledgers that recorded either. The House's largest
holdings, worked with better equipment, better irrigation, and generations of accumulated soil
improvement, produced far more grain per acre than they had in his grandfather's day. The smaller
holdings, worked by families without the capital to improve their land the same way, produced
considerably less. The same flat percentage, applied to both, meant the largest and wealthiest
holdings paid a smaller effective share of what they actually harvested than the smallest and
poorest ones did — a fact, the General said, that no one had designed on purpose and no one, in sixty
years, had quite gotten around to noticing on purpose either.

He found it the way he found most things worth finding, he told me — not through any grand
inspection, but by working late over a set of yield reports that had nothing to do with taxation at
all, and noticing, almost as an aside, that the numbers sat oddly against what he already knew of the
tithe formula. He was twenty-five by then, Quartermaster Aldous Prynn had held the House's stores and
levies for eleven years by that point, and the General said his first instinct, on bringing the
discrepancy to Prynn directly, was met with exactly the response he'd half expected: not hostility,
but the particular weary caution of a man who has kept a system running competently for over a
decade and has learned to be suspicious of young men who arrive with a better idea before breakfast.
Prynn didn't dispute the numbers. What he disputed was the wisdom of touching a formula that had, for
sixty years, avoided the far worse alternative — individual assessment, which invited favoritism,
disputes, and endless argument over whose land was truly worth what.

The General said he understood the objection, and didn't dismiss it, and spent the better part of a
month working alongside Prynn rather than around him to find a formula that solved the actual problem
without recreating the older one it had replaced. What they settled on, eventually, was neither a
flat percentage nor a full individual assessment, but a banded scale — several fixed tiers, set
against yield-per-acre averages recalculated every few years rather than fixed once and forgotten,
so that the largest and most productive holdings paid a somewhat higher rate and the smallest a
somewhat lower one, without any single family's tithe ever depending on one assessor's private
judgment of their particular field. It was, the General admitted, a more complicated system to
administer than the one it replaced, and he'd needed Prynn's own decade of practical experience to
keep it from collapsing into exactly the kind of dispute-generating machine Prynn had originally
feared.

He didn't announce the change as a correction of anything. When it went to Aethelgard for formal
approval, the proposal was presented, at Ozmund's own insistence, as jointly authored — Prynn's
practical refinements alongside Ozmund's original observation — and the General told me that had been
deliberate, not modesty for its own sake, but a recognition that a reform delivered as "the heir
fixed what the Quartermaster got wrong" would have cost Prynn something real, while one delivered as
two men solving a shared problem cost him nothing at all. Aethelgard approved it within the week,
remarking only that he'd never noticed the drift himself and was glad someone finally had.

The General said the new scale held, without significant revision, for the rest of the years he had
any way of knowing about it, and that what stayed with him longest wasn't the formula itself but the
particular quality of Prynn's manner toward him afterward — not gratitude, exactly, which would have
implied a debt owed, but something closer to the easy collegiality of a man who has decided you're
worth working alongside rather than around. He said that was worth more to him, in the years that
followed, than any single reform could have been on its own.
