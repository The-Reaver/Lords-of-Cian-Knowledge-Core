# The Numbers That Forgave No One

*Locked canon, Batch 203, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-893`). The Blue-Collar Titan Alias Chronicle XLVIII, wave 16. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

Two workers died when a support timber failed without warning in a section that had passed inspection
twice, been shored to code, and shown no sign of strain to anyone who'd examined it in the six days
since it went in — and by the time Kanja reached the site, the crew had already split into two camps,
one certain it was sabotage, the other certain someone had cut a corner and was hiding it.

He spent two full days confirming it was neither.

"I want it to be something," he told the crew chief honestly, laying out the failed timber's grain
alongside the installation records, the load logs, the inspection notes from both passes. "Sabotage,
I can find and stop. A cut corner, I can find and correct. What I'm finding instead is a timber that
was sound by every measure we had, installed correctly, inspected correctly, carrying a load well
within its rated tolerance — that failed anyway, because of a flaw buried inside the grain that no
inspection anyone's ever devised would have caught before it broke."

"That's not an answer anyone's going to accept."

"I know. I don't think it's supposed to be an answer that satisfies anyone. I think it's just the
true one." Kanja set the timber down, the fracture pattern he'd traced dozens of times over two days
still telling him the same unwelcome thing. "There's a difference between an answer that comforts
people and an answer that's honest. This is the second kind, and I'm not going to manufacture the
first kind just because it would sit easier."

The families wanted someone to answer for it, and there was no one — not the men who'd installed the
timber, who had followed procedure exactly; not the inspectors, whose tools had no way to see a flaw
sealed inside solid wood; not Kanja himself, who had checked the section three days prior and found
nothing a hundred more careful engineers would have found either. He said as much to them directly,
without hedging, without the comfort of a villain to offer in place of the truth.

"You're telling us it was bad luck," one grieving relative said, the words landing like an accusation
even though nothing in her voice suggested she thought he was lying.

"I'm telling you it was a flaw nobody could have found, in wood that passed every honest test we have
for finding flaws. I know that's not the same as luck being fair, and I know it doesn't give you
anyone to be angry at who actually deserves it." Kanja didn't look away from her. "I'd rather give you
that, hard as it is to hear, than give you a name to blame that isn't true just because a true name
would be easier to carry than no name at all."

There was no resolution that made the deaths sit easier — no fraud uncovered, no correction that
prevented a recurrence beyond doing exactly what the crew had already been doing, no comfort in an
explanation that amounted, in the end, to the honest limits of what care and craftsmanship could
promise anyone. Kanja recorded the finding plainly, without softening it, and let it stand as what it
was: the hardest kind of true account, offered because a false one would have been easier for no one
but him.
