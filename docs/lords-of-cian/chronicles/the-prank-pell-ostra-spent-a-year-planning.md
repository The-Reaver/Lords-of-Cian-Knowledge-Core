# The Prank Pell Ostra Spent a Year Planning

*Locked canon, Batch 222, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-996`). Captain Alias Chronicle XLIX, wave 17. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

It started, Pell Ostra would later admit, with a joke Kanja made in passing about how nothing on the
ship ever surprised him anymore — that between the Trinity's senses and a decade of reading rooms
for danger, there wasn't a corner of The Audit that could still catch him off guard. She hadn't
argued. She'd just quietly decided he was wrong, and spent the next year proving it.

The plan, when it finally went off, was almost insulting in its simplicity: a rigged bucket of bilge
water over the forward hatch, the kind of prank any dockhand might pull on a green recruit. What made
it work was everything underneath it — eleven months of Ostra deliberately mistiming her own
demolition charges by fractions of a second whenever Kanja was nearby, training his read of her
timing to expect a lag that wasn't real, so that on the one day it mattered, his instinct told him
the bucket's trigger mechanism was already spent when it wasn't.

He caught the bucket water across the shoulder in front of half the crew.

For a full three seconds nobody breathed. Corren Halst, who'd been recruited into the conspiracy
eight months earlier purely to keep a straight face during briefings, later said it was the longest
silence he'd ever stood through in a war.

Then Kanja started laughing — not the contained, dignified sort the crew was used to seeing from him
even in genuine good humor, but the kind that bent him over the rail and wouldn't stop, that pulled
Efa Gol and half the deck watch into it before anyone had decided it was safe to join.

"A year," he finally managed. "You spent a *year* recalibrating my read on your timing."

"Eleven months," Ostra said. "I'm efficient."

"I built my read on you off real data. You poisoned it on purpose."

"You said nothing on this ship could catch you off guard anymore. I don't like being told something's
finished when it isn't." She was already re-coiling the trigger line, entirely unbothered, as if she'd
merely closed out a demolition contract rather than humiliated a man who could bend metal with his
bare hands. "Took long enough. I was starting to think I'd have to actually blow something up to get
your attention."

"Please don't."

"No promises."

He didn't retaliate — not that week, not that month — and the crew noticed, and took it as its own
kind of answer: that a man who could have turned the whole ship upside down looking for a way to even
the score instead let it stand as hers, uncontested, a debt he had no intention of repaying. Halst
argued for years afterward that this, more than any battle, was the moment half the newer hands
stopped being quietly afraid of him and started being merely fond of him instead — that a man capable
of being outplanned by his own demolitions officer, and delighted by it, was not the kind of man
reputation alone could explain.

Ostra never did tell him it took eleven months and not a year. She liked the number better the way
she'd first said it, and some inaccuracies, she decided, were worth keeping.
