# What He Carried From His Own Father

*Locked canon, Batch 304, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1740`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle XI — the Aethelgard strand's closing entry, set strictly pre-Book-1, years before
the Fulfillment Ceremony (`MCD-025`). Aethelgard Verehimu is alive, a lord and not yet a king; Ozmund
is old enough to be told real family history rather than a child's version of it. Narrated by Red
Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`, reconstructing a story told to him long after
Aethelgard's death, matching the strand's established retrospective register. Dramatizes Aethelgard's
own relationship to Drakmund Verehimu — the "Evil King," the original Crown-Scar recipient
(`MCD-138`), exiled by Aethelgard himself and still alive in the Shattered Kingdoms per the

begun in Chronicle VII (`MCD-1736`) and reused in Chronicle VIII (`MCD-1737`). No new named
characters.*

Of everything the General ever told me about his father, this was the one story that wasn't really
about Aethelgard at all, though it took me years of knowing him to understand that. It was about the
man who made Aethelgard the way he was — and the General only ever told it to me the once, on a night
I won't describe, because it isn't mine to describe, except to say that it was a hard night for him
and this was the story that came out of it.

He said his father almost never spoke of Drakmund. He knew the shape of the fact the way any child of
that House knew it — that there had been a Verehimu before his father, that the man had been called
the Evil King by people who'd lived under him and meant it plainly, not as a figure of speech, and
that his own father, Aethelgard, had been the one to end it. Not through the ordinary mechanism of
succession. Through exile. His father had looked at the man who'd made him and decided the House
would survive his absence better than his rule, and had done what needed doing to make that true, and
had then, as far as the General ever knew, spoken of it perhaps three times in his whole remaining
life.

This was the third.

He said his father told him that Drakmund had carried the same inheritance he did — the Scar, the
weight of being first in a bloodline nobody else on Cian shared — and that the difference between what
it made of Drakmund and what Aethelgard had spent his whole adulthood trying to make sure it didn't
make of his own son wasn't the inheritance itself. It was what a man decided to do with the part of it
that was actually his to decide. Drakmund had mistaken the command in his blood for the whole of his
worth, had let it answer every question a patient man would have sat with instead, until there was no
longer a version of him underneath it that anyone recognized as separate from the crown he wore. "He
didn't lose himself to it," Aethelgard said — the General repeated this part carefully, like he'd
turned the words over enough times to be sure he still had them right — "he let it be an excuse not to
have a self at all. That's the actual danger in this family. Not the power. The convenience of letting
it decide things so you never have to."

And then, the General said, his father told him where the coin had come from — the same worn
half-scrip Aethelgard had spun on a table a hundred times across the boy's childhood, the ritual the
boy had assumed, without ever really thinking about it, his father had simply invented. It hadn't been
invented. Drakmund had taught it to Aethelgard first, when Aethelgard was a boy himself — *wait until
it falls* — except that in Drakmund's mouth, the General's father said, it had never once been about
patience for its own sake. It had been a lesson in leverage. Wait, and watch what the room does while
it waits, and use whatever the waiting shows you. The same gesture, the same worn coin, teaching the
opposite thing entirely. Aethelgard had kept the coin. He'd kept the ritual. He said he'd spent decades
deciding, slowly and mostly without noticing he was doing it, to keep the shape of the lesson and throw
away everything his own father had meant by it — until the waiting was just waiting again, a thing you
did to let your own temper fall before you moved, not a thing you did to someone else.

"That's the actual inheritance," Aethelgard told him, and this the General said he remembered whole,
unbroken, exactly as it had been given to him. "Not the Scar. Not even the coin, honestly — I could
have thrown the coin in the river and taught you the same lesson with a stone, a leaf, anything with
weight. The inheritance is that you will spend your whole life being handed things that already have a
shape, things your blood decided before you had any say in it, and somewhere along the way you're
going to have to decide, over and over, which parts of that shape you keep and which parts you throw
away and rebuild with your own hands instead. I can't do that part for you. My father certainly never
did it for me. All I can tell you is that it's possible — that a man can take the exact same object his
own father used to teach him the worst thing he ever learned, and teach his son the opposite lesson
with it, and mean every part of the difference."

The General told me he didn't understand, that night, that his father had just handed him the entire
argument he would eventually build a life around — the difference between a thing given and a thing
chosen, played out across three generations with nothing more dramatic than an old coin on a table. He
said it took him decades, and losing everything the coin's first lesson had ever belonged to, before
he understood what his father had actually been asking him to do. By then the coin was the only piece
of the inheritance he had left to keep.
