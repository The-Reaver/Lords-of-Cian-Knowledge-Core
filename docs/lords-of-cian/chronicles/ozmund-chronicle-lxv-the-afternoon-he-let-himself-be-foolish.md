# The Afternoon He Let Himself Be Foolish

*Locked canon, Batch 306, 2026-09-23 (`MCD-1794`). Character Chronicle track, Ozmund Verehimu's
series, Chronicle LXV — the Aethelgard strand's third wave, sixth entry, set strictly pre-Book-1, in

lord and not yet a king. Narrated by Red Beard (Tarn Cestari) per `VB-020`/`022`/`CC-020`,
reconstructing a story told to him long after Aethelgard's death, matching the strand's established
retrospective register. The strand's first dedicated entry of pure levity and warmth — no lesson, no

Not everything the General told me about his father was carrying a lesson underneath it, and I want
this one on the record exactly as he gave it to me, because I think he'd have minded if I dressed it up
into something wiser than it was. He told me about the day his father fell out of a fishing boat in the
Verehimu Wetlands in front of half the household, and he told it, every single time, laughing before
he'd even reached the part where it happened.

There was no occasion for it. That was, the General said, most of the point — an ordinary afternoon
with nothing on his father's schedule that couldn't wait, which by itself was rare enough to notice,
and Aethelgard had announced at breakfast, without warning, that he intended to catch dinner himself
that evening rather than let the kitchen do it, and that the boy was coming with him whether he wanted
to or not.

He did not, the General admitted, actually know how to fish particularly well. He said his father owned
the tackle the way a lord owns most things he doesn't personally use — someone else's competence,
borrowed by title — and spent the first hour of the afternoon demonstrating, with great confidence, a
technique the General later learned from an actual fisherman was backwards in nearly every particular.
Nothing bit for the first hour. Aethelgard blamed the fish's poor judgment rather than his own
technique, at length, in a tone the General said was somehow both entirely serious and clearly not
serious at all, a register he only ever seemed to find on days like this one.

Something did finally take the line, eventually, and it was, by every account the General ever gave me,
an unremarkable fish — not large, not fighting especially hard, nothing that should have troubled a
grown man standing in a boat he'd been in a hundred times before. Aethelgard hauled back on it with more
enthusiasm than the moment required, lost his footing on a wet plank he'd been warned about twice
already that afternoon, and went backward over the side of the boat in front of his son, two boatmen,
and — the General was particular about this detail, delighted by it every time he told it — a passing
barge crew who witnessed the entire thing and did not, by his account, stop laughing for the rest of the
week.

He came up soaked, weed in his hair, the fish still somehow on the line and flopping indignantly against
the hull, and the General said his father's first act, before even climbing back into the boat, was to
hold the fish aloft and declare, with total conviction, that he had won. Not caught. Won, as though the
whole exchange had been a contest of wills he'd narrowly carried the day in. He climbed back in still
laughing at himself, refused every offer of a dry coat from the mortified boatmen, and spent the ride
back recounting his own defeat to anyone within earshot with visible relish, adding some new
embarrassing flourish to the story each time he told it, until by the time they reached the dock the
fish had grown considerably and the fall had somehow become deliberate.

The General told me his father made him promise, that evening, never to let him live it down, which he
said struck him even then as a strange thing for a lord to ask of his own son — most fathers, he
thought, would have preferred the story quietly forgotten. Aethelgard wanted the opposite. He said,
more or less, that a man who could never once be laughed at by his own household had probably stopped
letting them see him as anything but the lord, and that he'd rather be the man who fell out of a boat
in front of half his people once a year than the man who never gave them the chance to laugh at him at
all.

I asked the General once whether that had been the only time. He said no — that his father made a
point, every so often, of being caught at something foolish where his son could see it, though none of
the others were ever quite as good a story as the fish. He told me he understood, only much later, that
his father had been doing it on purpose the whole time. That a boy who watched his father command
armies of stewards and treasurers and dike-wardens needed, at least as badly, to watch him be
thoroughly, gloriously ridiculous — because a man you can only picture being right about everything is a
great deal harder to actually love than a man you've also seen fall out of a boat.
