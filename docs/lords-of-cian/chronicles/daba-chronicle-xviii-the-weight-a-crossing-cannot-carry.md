# Daba Chronicle XVIII: The Weight a Crossing Cannot Carry

*Locked canon, Batch 296, 2026-09-18 (`MCD-1588`). Entry 18 of 50 in Daba's own Chronicle series,
launched under the Character Chronicle Gameplan's own protagonist-series model. Dramatizes the
discipline of making concentrated force irrelevant during Kanja's mentorship under Daba (`MCD-1568`)
-- narrowing an enemy's effective numbers to whatever a single chokepoint can carry -- the most direct
conceptual ancestor of Kanja's own later Dredge-Line Ambush (`MCD-231`). Abad's approval: "rate 50
Chronicles in batches using as many agents as needed to make it efficient."*

---

There was a scar in the ground three days' walk east of the marsh camps that Daba had never shown
anyone who could not yet understand what it meant. He showed it to Kanja on a grey morning, without
ceremony, leading him along a dry drainage cut that ran between two low ridges and stopping where the
cut widened into what had once been a ford.

"Forty-one of theirs came through here, nine years ago," Daba said. "Trust regulars, sent to burn a
grain store two cells were using to feed a resettlement camp. Forty-one is not a number I could have
fought with what I had then. Eight of us. Eight against forty-one is not a battle, it's an arithmetic
problem with only one answer."

Kanja was studying the ground, the way Daba had begun to notice he studied everything now, as if it
might answer a question before it was asked. "So you didn't fight forty-one."

"I fought six." Daba nodded at the ford. "This crossing was wide enough for eight men abreast, in good
weather. We spent two nights before they came narrowing it -- staked lines under the water, invisible
once submerged, that forced anyone crossing onto a path no wider than three men could safely hold at
once. Forty-one soldiers came to that ford. Six could cross it at a time without drowning themselves in
the staked water on either side. The other thirty-five stood on the far bank and watched, because there
was nowhere for them to stand that mattered."

"And the six who crossed."

"Were not forty-one anymore. They were six, isolated from everyone who could reinforce them, for as
long as it took the next six to cross behind them. We took the first six before the second six were
halfway over. By the time thirty-five men worked out that the water wouldn't let them help their own
people, we were gone, and they had lost twelve without ever bringing more than six against us at a
single moment."

Kanja crouched at the ford's edge, running his hand along the water's surface as though he could still
feel the shape of the old staked lines beneath it, though Daba had pulled them years ago. "You didn't
beat forty-one men. You made sure they were never forty-one men at the same time you had to face them."

"That's the whole doctrine, if you want it in one sentence." Daba sat back on his heels, watching the
boy work the idea rather than simply receive it. "Numbers only matter where numbers can arrive. A force
that can't reach the fight is a force that isn't in it, no matter how large it is standing on the far
bank. Density, mass, reserves -- none of it means anything the instant the ground itself decides how
many of them are allowed to matter at once."

He watched something move behind Kanja's eyes that Daba had come to recognize over these months as
the particular stillness of the boy testing a principle against every fight he could already imagine,
searching for where it broke. It did not, apparently, break easily. Kanja stood, looking not at the
ford but past it, toward some engagement Daba suspected did not exist yet anywhere except inside his
own head.

"Water does the deciding," Kanja said slowly. "Not the stakes. The stakes just told the water where to
be narrow."

"The stakes were the smallest part of it," Daba agreed. "Most of what made this ford ours belonged to
the ground before I ever touched it. I only asked it a favor."

Kanja nodded, and said nothing further, and Daba did not press him, because he had learned that the
boy's silences after a lesson like this one were not empty -- they were the sound of something being
filed away with unusual care, set aside for a use Daba could not yet see and did not need to. He looked
at the old ford a while longer himself, at thirty-five men's worth of wasted patience carved into a
riverbank that had long since stopped showing any sign of the stakes that had made it matter, and
thought, not for the first time, that the ground kept better secrets than people did, and told them
more honestly when it finally chose to speak.
