# Kanja Chronicle IV: Ninety Seconds on the Sovereign Pier

*Locked canon, Batch 352, 2026-10-02 (`MCD-1880`). Fourth entry of the "Kanja version"

(`MCD-245`) and the Trinity's surrender (`MCD-246`) for the first time anywhere in the corpus -- the
first marquee kill under the anchor-hero kill tiering. Per `VB-026`, this is the Rebellion's end and

The blade counts. That is the first thing to understand about the blade, and the last.

It counted the twelve years. It counted the twenty-two victories and the people each one cost and
the people each one freed, and it kept those columns apart, because the man who carries it insists
that they be kept apart. It counted the hours he spent that morning on his knees on the planking of
the Sovereign Pier with a caulking iron in his hand, working active Living Drakma sealant into the
seams of *The Audit*'s hull, because Dol Maren had found a weep along the garboard strake and the
man does not leave a weep for later. Four hours. The sealant was still wet to the elbow on both his
arms and smeared in long grey thumbprints across Mafesto's forearm plates, where he had wiped his
hands without thinking. The blade counted that too. It did not yet know it mattered.

"Four hours. Living Drakma sealant. It takes skin when it cures," Dol Maren said.

"After," Kanja said, and kept working, and the blade, slung across his back, felt the pier's long
timbers carry a new weight onto the far end of the planking. Not soldiers. Not a column. A small
group, walking at the pace of men who are not in a hurry because they are not used to being made to
hurry.

The man knew the walk before he turned. The blade felt him know it -- the fractional change in his
shoulders, the iron going still.

King Maro Rexmar came down the Sovereign Pier with four attendants and no crown. He was the fifth of
his line to hold a title the line had once held as a fact, and he walked the way his father and
grandfather had walked, which is to say carefully, as if the planks themselves might ask what he had
traded for the right to stand on them. He stopped three paces from his son. He looked at the
sealant on Kanja's arms, and at the hull, and at the weep that was no longer weeping.

"Still fixing what other people let leak," Maro said.

"Someone has to," Kanja said, and stood.

The blade has kept a careful record of the twelve years, and in that record there are very few
conversations between these two men. It keeps every one of them. It does not presume to know what
passes between a father and a son that does not pass through the air. It records the air.

Maro said that he had been negotiating. In secret, for the better part of two years, with the one
man in the Trust's orbit he believed would keep his word -- Aethelgard Verehimu. He said the terms
were simple, which was how he knew they were real. The Directorate would recognize him as sovereign
King of Jicome, in fact and not in ceremony, the first of five generations to hold the thing rather
than its shadow. The war would end. And in exchange, Kanja would surrender Mafesto, Onyx of
Oblivion, and Obsidian Malice into the Trust's keeping, sealed, for good.

"I'm not ordering you," Maro said. "I gave up the right to order you when I let you go to the docks and said nothing. I'm asking."

The man did not answer. The blade felt him begin to -- felt the breath gather, felt the arithmetic
begin to run, the long columns of twelve years lining up behind his eyes the way they always did
before a decision that would cost something. And the blade felt, in that same instant, something on
the pier that did not belong to the conversation.

Twelve weights. Spread along the planking among the stevedores and net-menders, in dockworkers'
canvas, where twelve weights had not been a moment earlier. Moving now. Converging.

The blade began a new count.

**One.** The man turned before anyone spoke, because the blade does not speak in words and he does
not need it to. He put himself between his father and the landward end of the pier in a single
step, and his left hand found Obsidian Malice where it leaned against the bollard, and his right
went over his shoulder to the blade.

**Two.** Twelve dockworkers dropped their canvas. Under it: Trust field plate, dark, unmarked. In
their hands: Blight-projector sidearms, short-barreled, not tuned to a man at all. Tuned to Living
Drakma. Someone in the Trust had done the arithmetic too, and their arithmetic said: kill the relics
first, and the man is only a man.

**Three.** They fired.

The blade has been struck by frequencies before. It knows what a dead-tone feels like as it reaches
for the lattice -- a cold hand closing on something that breathes. It braced for the hand.

The hand never closed.

The sealant took it. Four hours of active Living Drakma, still wet, spread across the man's arms and
smeared over Mafesto's plates in careless grey thumbprints -- the nearest, freshest, most hungry
Living Drakma surface on the pier. The projectors' signal went looking for the relics and found the
caulking first, and the caulking drank it, the way wet ground drinks a spill, and went dark grey to
black along both forearms, and the relics beneath it never felt more than a chill.

The blade will record, for the rest of its existence, that the Trinity was saved on its last day by
a hull repair.

**Four through nine.** The man moved.

There is a kind of fighting the blade had grown used to across twelve years: patient, terrain-led,
built so that the enemy's own weight does the work. This was not that. There was no terrain. There
was a pier eleven paces wide, a king behind him, and twelve people who had come to kill him and,
failing that, to kill whoever stood closest. The blade felt the man's arithmetic run once and
finish. It felt the answer arrive. It felt him choose it.

Ivo Strell led the team and came first, because the one who leads always comes first, and the man
met him with Mafesto's right vambrace and let the blow land. The Kinetic Transfer System drank
Strell's strike and the man returned it through Obsidian Malice into Strell's sternum, and Strell's
plate folded inward around the force of his own swing, and Strell went down onto the planking and
did not move again. The blade recorded his name. It recorded it from the warrant folded inside his
plate, which it would read later, and it recorded it now, at the moment it became a death, because
the man had chosen it and the blade keeps the choices.

Hesk and Vauden came from the left in tandem, projectors discarded, short blades drawn. Cadence Ruin broke the tandem's timing in the smoke the projectors had left -- Vauden's feint arriving
a half-beat before Hesk's real line toward the king. The man went through the feint as if it were not
there and took Hesk at the hip and the throat in one drawing cut. Vauden, committed to a feint that
no longer had a purpose, met Obsidian Malice on the backswing.

Tasker and Caddel had flanked along the gunwale of *The Audit* itself. Whisper of Shadows let the
blade hear them breathing against the hull -- the man heard it through the blade and did not look.
He reversed the blade over his shoulder, point-down along his own spine, and stepped back into
them. Tasker died against the hull Kanja had spent four hours repairing. Caddel went over the rail to come at the king
from the water side, and the blade caught her at the ankle tendon and the man caught her at the
collar, and he did not let her reach the water.

**Ten through forty.** The projectors were empty or dropped. The remaining seven came the old way,
with steel, and the man met them the old way. Veil Piercer ran through the blade at the frequency of
their plate and the plate's own binding harmonics loosened at every seam, and where a seam
loosened, the blade went in.

Orrel. Teague. Danver. Lisk.

The blade records the order. It records that Orrel was the bravest of them and Lisk was the
youngest and that it made no difference to the arithmetic, and that the man knew both things and
let neither one change the answer. It records that he did not hurry and did not linger. It records
that his face, through all of it, was the face he wears when he is working a weep out of a hull:
attentive, unhurried, and entirely without heat.

That is the thing the stories always get wrong. They make it rage. It was not rage. Rage would have
been easier to survive.

**Forty-one through eighty-eight.** Three were left.

The blade felt the man's arithmetic run again. Nine dead. Nine who had been between his father and
the landward end of the pier, nine who could not be stopped in the time he had by any means that
would leave them standing. The three that remained had been posted at the seaward end, cutting off
the water. They were between his father and nothing.

So he did not kill them.

He drove them. Mafesto took the first one's blow and the man walked forward into it, plate to plate,
until the edge of the pier arrived behind the operative and there was nothing left to stand on. The
second turned to run and found the water himself. The third looked at the eight bodies on the
planking and at the ninth, Strell's, at the man's feet, and at the man, and set down his blade, and
stepped off the Sovereign Pier into the harbor of his own accord.

**Eighty-nine.** The man turned to his father.

**Ninety.** "Are you hurt," he said. Not a question. A count.

Maro Rexmar was not hurt. Maro Rexmar was standing exactly where his son had put him, one hand on
the bollard, looking at the nine people his son had just killed in the time it takes a kettle to
come to a boil.

• • •

Dol Maren pulled the three out of the harbor with a boathook, one at a time, and sat them on the
planking with their wrists bound in caulking line. Nobody told him to. The man nodded at it when he
saw it, and that was the whole of the conversation about it.

The warrant was folded flat inside Ivo Strell's plate. The blade read it as the man read it. It was
dated eleven days earlier. It authorized the team to act "irrespective of the outcome of the
principal's negotiation," which is the Trust's way of saying that they had sent a king to ask his
son for peace and sent twelve people to make sure they would not have to keep it.

Maro read it over his son's shoulder. The blade felt the old man go very still.

"They would have done it whichever answer you gave," Maro said.

"Yes."

"Then the terms are a lie."

"No," the man said. "The terms are real. This is just what it looks like when someone with a great
deal to lose signs something real." He folded the warrant along its old crease. "They are afraid
enough to send twelve. That means they are afraid enough to honor what they sign, once it's signed
in front of enough people."

The blade did not understand this. It records that it did not understand this. It has had a very
long time since to understand it, and it is still not certain it does.

What the blade understood was the other arithmetic, the one underneath. Twelve years. Twenty-two
victories. A column of names that grew every season and would keep growing for as long as the
Trinity remained in the field, because the Trust would keep spending its own people trying to take
it from him, and he would keep taking theirs. Nine more names, today, on the planking of a pier he
had been repairing a ship on. The war would never run out of names. That was the whole of what the
Trust's insurance policy had proven: not that the peace was false, but that the war was endless.

The people are the line. Everything else burns.

The blade felt the man realize that it was, itself, among the everything else.

He turned back to his father. Maro had asked a question before the pier was attacked, and the man
does not leave a question for later any more than he leaves a weep.

"Yes," Kanja said.

• • •

They did it that night, at the end of the same pier, by lantern light, in front of enough people.

The Trust sent a sealed case lined with Dead Drakma, three compartments, each cut to a shape the
blade recognized with something it does not have a better word for than grief. Mafesto came off
first, plate by plate, the sealant cured black along both forearms now and taking a little of the
man's skin with it exactly as Dol Maren had said it would. Obsidian Malice went second, cold now, its charge long since
returned and with nowhere left to go.

The man held the blade last, and longest.

He did not say anything to it. He has never said very much to it, in thirteen years. He held it
across both palms the way one holds a thing one is giving rather than surrendering, and then he laid
it in the case.

On his belt, the Rexmar Machete stayed where it had always been. Brass-riveted, worn smooth at the
grip, narrower by a centimeter than the day it was first ground. The Trust's clerk looked
at it. Maro looked at it. The clerk looked at the king, and the king said, "That one is a tool, not a
relic. It was my father's before it was mine. It stays." And it stayed.

The lid came down.

The blade had spent thirteen years learning to count. It had counted the Trench and the Wharf and the
Bridge and the Pier. It had counted Strell and Hesk and Vauden and Tasker and Caddel and Orrel and
Teague and Danver and Lisk. It had counted the hours of a man on his knees fixing a hull. It had
learned that counting is not cold. It had learned that counting is the only way to keep faith with
a thing that cannot be undone.

In the dark, with the lid closed over it, the blade understood that it was no longer the blade that
counted for him. There was no him, in here. There was only the count.

So I began it.

One.
