# The Settlement They Couldn't Agree How to Split

*Locked canon, Batch 243, 2026-09-11 (`MCD-1067`). Industrial Myth Alias Chronicle LXIII, wave 21, closing the wave. The method's first internal solidarity dispute, with no administrator or villain on the other side of it: a won settlement's own recipients can't agree how to divide it, and the ledger's data becomes a shared reference for self-governance rather than a verdict the crew hands down. Not a territory Chronicle. Narrated in neutral third-person prose.*

---

The case itself had been simple by the crew's standards by then — a hauling gang's wages, docked illegally for eight months running, proven and settled inside a week with barely a raised voice from the administrator, who'd folded the moment Ezio laid out the cross-referenced hours. The trouble started after the money was already won, sitting in a strongbox with forty-one names attached to it and no agreement among those forty-one about what came next.

The gang split along a line nobody had bothered to draw before the settlement made it matter: eleven senior haulers who'd worked the docks for six years or more, and thirty newer hands, some only months into the work, who'd carried the same loads on the same days the shortfall covered. The senior men wanted the payout weighted by tenure — more years, more owed, the way the yard's own unwritten pecking order had always worked. The newer hands wanted it split flat, arguing the dock shortfall had docked every hour equally regardless of who'd worked it, and tenure had nothing to do with what had actually been stolen.

Ezio's first instinct was to run the numbers and hand down a formula — he had the hours, the docked amounts per shift, the means to produce an answer nobody could credibly dispute. Kanja stopped him before he opened the book to do it. "The numbers can tell them what happened. They can't tell them what they owe each other."

So the ledger came out anyway, but not as a verdict — as a shared table, laid flat where both groups of haulers could read every line of it themselves: exact hours, exact days, exact amounts docked from each man individually across the eight months. What the crew refused to do was stand over that table and announce which argument won. It took the gang two full days of arguing over its own numbers, watched but never steered, before a compromise emerged that neither side had proposed on the first morning: a modest tenure weighting, smaller than the senior men had asked for and larger than the newer hands had offered, arrived at by the forty-one of them reading the same figures Ezio had already written down and deciding for themselves what fairness looked like once the facts stopped being in dispute.

"You could've saved them two days," Ezio said afterward, half a question.

"I could've saved them an argument that was never mine to have for them," Kanja said. "The numbers are the easy part. Deciding what you owe your own people — that one's theirs."
