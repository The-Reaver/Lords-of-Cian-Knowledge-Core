Word had reached the network again, as it did every so often, carried in the loose way rumor travels
along trade roads that no one in 1804 had ever bothered to trace back to its source -- a name this
time, one of several the same man seemed to collect the way other men collected debts, spoken by a
grain merchant two provinces off who claimed a cousin had seen him with his own eyes and swore the
account was true. Daba let it pass through the cell the way he let all such rumors pass through it,
neither confirming nor denying, because confirming would have meant explaining a connection that no
living record anywhere would ever be permitted to show, and denying would have meant lying to people
he did not lie to.

He sat with it longer than he let anyone see, that evening, on the low wall outside whatever the cell
was currently calling home. Perrin found him there and sat without asking permission, which was, by
now, its own kind of permission granted in advance.

"You've heard the name too," Perrin said.

"I've heard several of them, over the years. They keep multiplying." Daba turned a stone over in his
hand, not for any reason, only because his hands had never learned to sit entirely still. "I taught a
boy to read the ground on a dry wash a mile and a half from anything with a name, because I wanted the
first lesson to cost him nothing but a bruised ego and a long walk home. I remember exactly how he
stood when he finally understood what I was showing him -- like a man discovering a limb he hadn't
known he had. That boy is being sung about, now, in five different registers, under five different
names, by people who will never once hear the word 1804 in their lives." He set the stone down. "I find
I don't mind it. I want to be honest with you about that, because I think you'll face the same
question yourself someday, and I'd rather you hear the true answer from me than build your own out of
guessing."

"You don't mind that he gets remembered and you don't."

"I don't mind that he gets remembered. I built this whole life on the principle that being remembered
and being effective are, past a certain size, opposites. I chose the second one on purpose, before I
ever met him, for reasons that had nothing to do with him at all." Daba looked out at the dark tree
line, unremarkable, doing exactly the job it had been chosen for. "What I sit with, some evenings, isn't
envy. It's a stranger thing. It's that the boy I taught used to need everyone he lost written down by
name, so none of them went into a ledger as a number -- I watched that need get built in him before he
ever met me, at a table with a woman and a ledger, a story that isn't mine to tell in full. And the life
I've built since is the exact opposite of that need. Nobody in 1804 goes into any ledger at all, by
name or by number, if I've done my work correctly. The safest thing I can do for every person under my
protection is make sure no record of them exists anywhere a stranger could ever find it." He exhaled,
something between a laugh and its absence. "I have spent my whole adult life perfecting the one thing
the boy I loved as a son once told me, without knowing he was telling me anything, that he could never
forgive if it were done to the people he lost. I don't think it's a contradiction. I think it's just
what the work actually costs, when you look at it honestly instead of at the doctrine that makes it
sound clean."

Perrin was quiet for a while. "And the reward," he said finally. "You said there was one. You always
say there's one."

"The reward is smaller than the cost, and I've never once regretted the trade." Daba stood, brushing
dust from his knees, already turning his attention back toward the cell's ordinary evening business.
"It's watching someone I trained lead a cell of their own three provinces from here, doing work that
saves real people, and knowing that if it ever goes wrong, the trail stops at them and goes no
further -- never back to me, never back to this wall, never back to a single name I've ever loved.
That's not glory. It was never going to be glory. It's just the quiet, specific proof that being small
enough not to be seen is still, after everything, doing exactly what I built it to do." He looked at
Perrin a moment longer than the sentence required. "Someday that'll be your reward too. I wanted you to
hear, from someone who's already spent decades collecting it, that it's enough. It has to be. It's the
only kind this work was ever going to pay."
