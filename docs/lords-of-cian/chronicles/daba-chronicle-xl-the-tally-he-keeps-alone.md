Once a year, on the anniversary of the Rookery, Daba took the private list out and read it through
from the first name to the last, alone, somewhere no one in 1804 would think to look for him.

The list had started with the Rookery's own dead, because he had felt, in the first raw months after
the fire, that someone owed those 1,804 people the discipline of being counted individually rather than
folded into a number the Trust's own report had already reduced them to. It had grown since, slowly,
in the small hand he used for it, with names that had nothing to do with that first night at all:
couriers, cell leads, people who had died carrying a message or holding a position or simply being
somewhere the Trust's attention happened to fall. Tamsin's name was near the bottom now, in ink not yet
faded the way the older entries had faded, and reading it still cost him something specific and
familiar, the particular ache of a decision he had made correctly and would make the same way again and
had never once managed to make painless.

He did not read the list to punish himself. He had considered, in earlier years, whether that was what
he was doing, and had decided, honestly, that it wasn't -- the list was not penance, it was
accounting, and accounting was the one discipline he trusted himself never to abandon, because
everything he had built ran on the conviction that a debt correctly counted was a debt that could,
eventually, be answered. He simply did not know, most years, what answering it would actually look
like, or whether the shape of an answer would arrive in his own lifetime or in someone else's.

The doubt was quieter than people who did not know him well would have guessed. It rarely showed itself
as hesitation -- he had never once, in eleven years, delayed an operation because he was unsure the
doctrine was right. It showed itself instead in exactly this: an hour, once a year, spent reading a list
no one else was permitted to see, weighing losses against a network that was still, by any honest
measure, smaller than the danger it was meant to eventually answer. Kether's question from two years
before came back to him every time he sat with it. So did Serel's, and Pallow's Reach's, and a dozen
others that had never made it into any Chronicle anyone would write, because most of what the doctrine
cost him never rose to the level of a story. It simply accumulated, name by name, in a hand that had
grown steadier with practice at the exact task that should, by rights, have made it shake.

What kept him at it, year after year, was not certainty that the arithmetic was correct. He had long
since stopped expecting certainty from himself on that question, and had come to think that anyone who
claimed to have it, running something like 1804, was either lying or dangerous. What kept him at it was
narrower and, he had decided, sufficient: the Rookery had burned because it was large enough to be seen
and unprepared for what being seen cost. Everything he had built since was an answer to that one fact,
imperfect and expensive and frequently unbearable in its particulars, and he had never yet found an
alternative to it that did not simply move the cost onto someone else's list instead of his own.

He closed the book before dawn, the way he always did, and put it away somewhere none of them would
think to look, and went back to the work the same as any other day -- because the work did not care how
long he had sat with the names, and because he had learned, over eleven years of carrying this, that
grief and doubt were both allowed to live in him permanently without ever once being allowed to decide
anything for him. That distinction, more than any tactic he had ever taught anyone, was the actual
discipline. He was not sure, most years, that he had mastered it. He knew only that he had not yet
failed to practice it, and that as long as that remained true, the list would keep growing slowly
rather than all at once, and that difference, however small it looked from outside, was the entire
reason 1804 still existed to grieve anyone at all.
