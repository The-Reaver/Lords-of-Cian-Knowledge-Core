The well at Ostrey Hollow had never frozen in living memory, and it did not freeze that night
either. It simply stopped giving water back a shape a bucket could hold. Senna understood this
before she understood anything else about what had happened -- that the rope came up slack, that
the water at the bottom of the shaft was moving in a way water did not move, folding on itself like
cloth being wrung by hands nobody could see, and that the third time the bucket came up dry she
stopped hauling and went to wake her uncle instead.

Her uncle woke, listened, and did not laugh at her the way she had half-expected. That alone told
her something. Doran had kept the old caretaker's post at the ridge station above Ostrey Hollow for
eleven years, the way his father had kept it before him and his father's father before that, a
duty handed down with no instructions attached beyond *keep the seals painted and don't ask what
they're for*. He was already reaching for his boots when she finished the sentence.

"The seals," he said. Not a question.

They climbed the ridge path together in the dark, and Senna watched her uncle's face change as the
station came into view -- not with the shape of fear she expected, but with something closer to a
man recognizing, finally, in his fifty-ninth year, what the thing he had spent his whole life
maintaining had actually been for. The kennel door -- she had always thought of it as a door,
though it had no hinges and no handle, only a ring of dark stone sunk into the hillside with old
paint worn to a memory of itself in a pattern none of the caretakers, going back generations, had
ever been told the meaning of -- stood open. Not broken. Open, the way a hand opens when whatever
it was holding stops needing to be held.

Something had come out.

It found them an hour later, at the tree line, and what struck Senna most in the moment before her
uncle pulled her behind him was how small it was. She had built, without meaning to, an expectation
of a monster to match the dread that had walked her up the ridge, and instead there was a shape
roughly the size of a large dog, low to the ground, its edges not quite agreeing with the air around
them the way a heat shimmer disagrees with a horizon -- present and also somehow argued-with by the
space it occupied. It made no sound. That was the worst part, Senna would say later, to anyone who
would still listen to her after that winter: not that it was silent, but that its silence felt
*deliberate*, the silence of something that had decided, on its own, that sound was a debt it no
longer owed the world it had been kept out of.

Doran did not run. He put himself between it and his niece and began, in a voice she had never
heard him use, to recite something that was not quite a prayer and not quite a set of
instructions -- fragments of a pattern he had painted onto stone every autumn of his adult life
without once being told what the pattern was for, and that his body, it turned out, had learned
anyway, the way hands learn a task the mind was never given the words to explain. The shape at the
tree line paused. Whether it understood him, or simply registered a frequency it had spent however
many centuries conditioned to associate with return rather than pursuit, neither of them would ever
know. It did not attack. It circled once, unhurried, and Senna felt something pass across her skin
like a hand laid flat and drawn slowly away -- not painful, not even entirely unpleasant, just
*marking*, the way a tide leaves a line on sand to show how far it once reached. Then it was gone
into the treeline, and the night was only a night again.

Doran did not sleep that winter. He painted the seals twice as often as the old schedule called
for, though he could no longer say with any honesty what good the seals had ever done, since
whatever they had kept in had plainly decided keeping was no longer the arrangement. He wrote to
the regional register, twice, describing what he had seen in language stripped as bare of
interpretation as he could manage, and received back, both times, an acknowledgment so
administratively calm that it frightened him more than the creature had. *Noted. No further action
required at this time.* As though somewhere far away, someone already knew, and had already decided
that one small settlement's fright did not rise to the level of a response.

Senna carried the mark for six days before it faded from where anyone could see it, a faint
discoloration along her collarbone shaped like nothing in particular. She never spoke of it as
having hurt. She spoke of it, when she spoke of it at all, as having felt like being *counted* --
the specific, clinical attention of something taking an inventory rather than choosing a meal, and
she found, to her own surprise, that this frightened her less than being hunted would have and more
than being ignored. To be counted meant you had been found worth counting. She did not know, and
would have no way of ever knowing, whether that was a mercy or simply an indifference too large to
notice the difference.

What none of them at Ostrey Hollow ever learned -- what the register's calm acknowledgments never
told them, and what no caretaker's inherited fragments of pattern had ever been built to explain --
was that theirs was the smallest of what had happened that season. A hundred kennels like Doran's,
tended by a hundred families who had never been told what they tended, stood open across the width
of the world that same handful of days, each one a small door in a hillside that had held something
back for longer than any living caretaker's family line, keeping a promise none of them had known
they were part of until the promise broke. Somewhere far past Ostrey Hollow's ridge, larger doors
had opened onto larger things, and men and women with names the register would remember were
already moving to answer them. Ostrey Hollow was not one of the places anyone came to explain
things to. It was only, ever, one of the places something had been let out.

Doran kept painting the seals every autumn until his hands gave out and Senna's began the work
instead, neither of them any closer to knowing what the pattern meant than the caretakers before
them had been -- only that it had, once, on one particular night, been enough to make something
that no longer had to listen choose, for reasons of its own, to listen anyway.
