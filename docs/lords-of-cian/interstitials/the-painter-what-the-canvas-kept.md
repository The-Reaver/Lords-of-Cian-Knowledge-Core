They called him Vantine in that country, though no one who used the name could have told you where
they had first heard it, or whether it was a name at all rather than a sound that had simply
attached itself to him the way a burr attaches to cloth, indifferent to whether the cloth wanted it.
He came through perhaps once in a generation, sometimes twice, always alone, always on foot, and
always with the same case slung across his back -- a long, flat, leather-wrapped shape that
travelers along that road had learned, over the centuries, not to ask about.

Coll asked about it anyway, because Coll was nineteen and had not yet learned the things a road like
that one taught people who survived it, and because the stranger, when he stopped at the waystation
that evening, did not have the look of a threat. He had the look, if anything, of a tired
craftsman -- unhurried hands, paint worked so deeply into the creases of his knuckles that no amount
of washing had ever fully lifted it, and eyes that settled on a person the way a buyer's eyes settle
on a piece of furniture, assessing grain and joinery before it occurs to them to notice the room
around it.

"What's in the case," Coll said, refilling the man's cup because it seemed the hospitable thing to
do, and because the silence that followed the question was long enough that filling it with
something, anything, felt necessary.

"My work," the man said. Not unkindly. Not an invitation to ask further, either, though Coll, being
nineteen, heard it as one.

"You're a painter, then."

"I am the Painter," the man said, and something in the particular weight he placed on the word --
not a trade, a title, worn the way a very old coat is worn, familiar past the point of noticing its
own shape -- should have ended the conversation. Coll did not notice. Coll asked to see.

What happened next, no one at the waystation could later agree on with any precision, because no one
else had been close enough to see it happen and far enough to survive having seen it. The keeper
remembered the stranger unwrapping the case with the particular care of a man who has done the same
motion ten thousand times and never once let the ten-thousandth time become careless. The stableboy
remembered a canvas that seemed, in the lamplight, to hold more depth than a folded piece of cloth
should reasonably hold, the way a held breath holds more air than the same lungs, unheld, ever seem
to need. Nobody remembered Coll agreeing to anything. Nobody remembered Coll refusing, either. There
was, in the accounts gathered afterward by the one traveling clerk thorough enough to write any of
it down, a conspicuous silence exactly where Coll's own voice should have been.

By morning the stranger was gone, and so was Coll, and the waystation's collective memory of the
previous evening had the particular soft, unfocused quality memories get when something has been
deliberately smoothed over rather than simply forgotten -- not erased, the clerk wrote, in the one
line of her report that the regional register later flagged and kept rather than filed away
unread, but *edited*, the way a painting is edited by a second layer laid carefully over the first,
leaving the shape of what was underneath still faintly visible to anyone who knew to look for the
seam.

What the Painter does with what he takes, no living account has ever confirmed, because no one who
could confirm it has ever come back to say. The oldest fragments -- Old Dominion relic-fragments,
older than the fall the Dominion itself is remembered for -- describe a canvas that does not simply
depict its subjects but *holds* them, layer folded beneath layer beneath layer, a recursive
gallery in which nothing painted is ever quite finished being painted, each new addition settling
into the same worked surface as the ones before it rather than displacing them. Whether the
figures within that canvas experience anything at all, whether Coll is a picture now or still,
somehow, a person trapped inside the idea of one, is a question the Painter has never been recorded
answering, and no one who has asked it to his face has lived to report whether he even considered it
worth answering.

He does not hunt. This, more than anything, is what makes him difficult to guard against, difficult
even to properly fear in the ordinary sense fear usually takes: he does not track, does not pursue,
does not seem to want anything from the world beyond the occasional subject willing, or curious
enough, or simply present enough, to sit for him. He walks a country once in a generation and
leaves, and the country closes back over the gap he leaves behind the way water closes over a stone,
and life along that road goes on, mostly, the way it always has, with one fewer curious nineteen-
year-old in it than there used to be, and no clean way for anyone left behind to say, with certainty,
whether that loss was a tragedy, a transaction, or simply weather -- an old and patient thing passing
through, taking what it takes because taking is, and has perhaps always been, simply what it is.

The waystation still stands. Travelers still stop there. The clerk's report still sits, unresolved,
in a regional register that has long since stopped expecting an answer to the questions it raises,
filed under a classification reserved for phenomena the world has decided, collectively and without
ever quite voting on it, are safer left alone than understood. No one at the waystation speaks of
Vantine by name anymore, not out of superstition exactly, but out of the same unspoken caution with
which a person avoids naming a debt they have no intention of ever being able to pay. The case, when
it comes through again -- and it will, in a generation, or two, unhurried, on foot, indifferent to
which generation happens to be standing at the waystation when it arrives -- will be a little
heavier than the last time anyone weighed it, and no one who notices that will ask why.
